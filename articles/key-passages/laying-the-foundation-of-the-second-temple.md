# Laying the Foundation of the Second Temple

Ezra 3 tells how the Jews who came home from exile in Babylon began to rebuild the house of God in Jerusalem. In the seventh month they gathered "as one man", rebuilt the altar and restored the daily offerings; in the second month of the second year after their arrival they laid the foundation of a new Temple (3:1-8, WEB).[^s1] At the foundation ceremony the old men who had seen Solomon's Temple wept aloud while others shouted for joy, "so that the people could not discern the noise of the shout of joy from the noise of the weeping" (3:13, WEB).[^s1]

## Why it matters

The passage marks a new beginning after the disaster of the exile. Worship comes first: the altar is rebuilt and offerings resume before a single foundation stone is laid (3:6).[^s1] It also gives Scripture's honest picture of mixed emotion in the life of God's people. Rabbi Tzvi Sinensky, teaching the chapter for Yeshivat Har Etzion, draws two lessons from it: moments of joy can hold sadness, and an imperfect achievement is still a reason to rejoice.[^s10]

## Context

Ezra 3 is the returned exiles' first act as a community in Jerusalem, led by Zerubbabel, a grandson of King Jehoiachin who later served as governor of Judah, and Jeshua (Joshua) the high priest.[^s7] Opposition then stalled the work for years, until the prophets Haggai and Zechariah stirred them to build again (5:1-2).[^s7][^s2] The finished Temple was far plainer than Solomon's: the Catholic Encyclopedia notes that the Ark was never recovered and the Holy of Holies stood empty.[^s8]

## Walking through the text

**The altar and the feast (3:1-6).** Jeshua and Zerubbabel build the altar "as it is written in the law of Moses the man of God", and set it on its old base, "for fear was on them because of the peoples of the countries" (3:2-3, WEB).[^s1] They offer burnt offerings morning and evening, keep the Feast of Tabernacles ("the feast of tents") and the other appointed feasts, all while the Temple's foundation "was not yet laid" (3:4-6).[^s1] Sinensky notes two shadows over this hopeful start: fear, and an altar without a Temple.[^s10]

**Preparing to build (3:7-9).** Cedar is brought from Lebanon through Sidon and Tyre, as in Solomon's day, but now under Cyrus's grant, a reminder that the builders worked under Persian rule (3:7).[^s1][^s10]

**The foundation ceremony (3:10-11).** Priests in their vestments sound trumpets and the Levites, sons of Asaph, play cymbals "after the order of David king of Israel" (3:10, WEB).[^s1] They sing in turn, "For he is good, for his loving kindness endures forever toward Israel," and all the people shout (3:11, WEB).[^s1] The refrain is the one that runs through Psalm 136: "Give thanks to Yahweh, for he is good; for his loving kindness endures forever" (136:1, WEB).[^s4][^s10]

**Joy and weeping (3:12-13).** Many of the priests, Levites and family heads, "the old men who had seen the first house", weep with a loud voice, while many others shout for joy, and the mingled noise is heard far away (3:12-13, WEB).[^s1]

## Interpretation

Most readers take the old men's tears as grief at how small the new building looked beside the one they remembered, a feeling the prophet Haggai addresses directly.[^s8][^s10] He asks who remembers the house in its former glory and whether the new one seems like nothing, then tells the people to be strong and work, for God is with them, and promises that its latter glory will exceed the former (Haggai 2:3-9).[^s3] Zechariah adds that Zerubbabel's hands laid the foundation and will finish it, and asks, "who despises the day of small things?" (Zechariah 4:9-10, WEB).[^s5] Christians have often read Haggai's promise in light of the coming of the Messiah; the Catholic Encyclopedia, for example, links the greater glory of the latter house to Christ, treating the Temple as one continuing house from Solomon's day to the Messiah's.[^s8]

## Scholarly views

Scholars read the chapter's chronology in different ways.[^s9][^s6] Ezra 3:8-10 has Zerubbabel and Jeshua lead the foundation work soon after the return under Cyrus, but Ezra 5:16 says that Sheshbazzar laid it, and Haggai 2:18, read on its own, seems to date the foundation to the reign of Darius, around 520 BC.[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s9]

- **Traditional reading.** The foundation was laid in the second year after a return around 537 BC; work then stopped until 520 and the Temple was finished in 516 BC.[^s8][^s9] On this view Ezra 5:16 names Sheshbazzar because he was the governor named in Cyrus's decree, and Haggai may describe a second foundation ceremony, as ancient temple rituals allowed.[^s9] Andrew Steinmann dates the return to 533 and the foundation to 532, reading Haggai 2:18 as looking back to that event.[^s6]
- **Later or two-stage reconstructions.** Some scholars, such as Cross and Halpern, treat Haggai and Zechariah as the better guide and place Zerubbabel's return and the foundation around 520; others, such as Japhet and Tomasino, posit two separate returns, one under Sheshbazzar and a later one under Zerubbabel.[^s6][^s9]
- **Sheshbazzar and Zerubbabel.** Older scholarship debated whether they were one man with two names; the Jewish Encyclopedia sets out both views and notes that Ezra never hints that they are the same.[^s7]

## Common misreadings

The weeping is sometimes read as ingratitude or unbelief. The text does not criticise the old men, and Haggai answers their grief with encouragement and a promise rather than rebuke.[^s1][^s3] Nor is the chapter a story of finished success: it begins a long, interrupted work that the prophets later revived.[^s2][^s7]

## Connections

The passage is **part of** the Book of Ezra and **features** Zerubbabel and Jeshua son of Jozadak, who lead the building (3:2, 3:8).[^s1] It **features** the Temple in Jerusalem, rebuilt here on a humbler scale.[^s8] Its song of praise **alludes to** the refrain of Psalm 136, and Sinensky reads the ceremony as renewing the link with David's psalms.[^s10][^s4] The chapter **teaches** that worship comes first: the altar and the offerings are restored before the foundation is laid (3:6).[^s1] Its scene of mingled joy and tears **speaks to** grief, especially the sorrow of remembering better days.[^s10] It is **explained by** the Book of Haggai, whose prophecies frame the foundation and the people's discouragement.[^s9][^s3] It belongs to the return from the **Babylonian exile** and the beginnings of **Second Temple Judaism**.[^s7]
