# Jeremiah's Temple Sermon

Early in King Jehoiakim's reign, God sent Jeremiah to stand in the gate of the Temple in Jerusalem and speak to everyone coming in from the towns of Judah to worship (Jeremiah 7:1-2; 26:1-2).[^s1][^s2] His message was startling: the Temple would not save people who used it as a shelter for injustice. The sermon is recorded twice in the book. Chapter 7 gives its words, and chapter 26 tells what happened when Jeremiah preached it: he was nearly put to death.[^s1][^s2][^s4]

## Why it matters

Here God plainly refuses to let worship be separated from the way people live.[^s3] The sermon also stands behind Jesus's words in the Temple, that it had been made "a den of thieves" (Mark 11:17, KJV), taken from Jeremiah 7:11.[^s7][^s1] Knowing Jeremiah's sermon helps readers hear what Jesus was saying.[^s6]

## Context

Jeremiah 26:1 places the sermon "in the beginning of the reign of Jehoiakim".[^s2] Michael Avioz notes that many scholars date it to about 609-608 BC, the unsettled years after King Josiah's death under Egyptian domination, when people sought security in the Temple and Jeremiah's task was to unsettle that false calm.[^s3]

To do so he pointed to **Shiloh**, about twenty miles north of Jerusalem, where the tabernacle and the ark had stood in the days of Eli and Samuel.[^s5] After the Philistines defeated Israel and captured the ark (1 Samuel 4), Shiloh faded, and it was probably destroyed around 1050 BC.[^s5] Psalm 78 remembers that God "forsook the tabernacle of Shiloh" (Psalm 78:60, KJV).[^s9]

## Walking through the text

**The call to change (7:1-7).** God's offer comes first: "Amend your ways and your doings", and he will let them dwell in the land (7:3, KJV; compare 7:5-7).[^s1] Then comes the warning: "Don't trust in lying words, saying, The temple of Yahweh, the temple of Yahweh, the temple of Yahweh, are these" (7:4, WEB).[^s1] Real security lies in justice between neighbours, refusing to "oppress the foreigner, the fatherless, and the widow" (7:6, WEB), not shedding innocent blood, and not following other gods.[^s1]

**Stolen safety (7:8-11).** Jeremiah lists theft, murder, adultery, false oaths and the worship of Baal (7:9), an echo of the Ten Commandments, and then pictures the same people standing in God's house saying, "We are delivered" (7:10, WEB).[^s1] God's question follows: "Is this house, which is called by my name, become a den of robbers in your eyes?" (7:11, WEB).[^s1] A robbers' den is where robbers hide after their crimes; Avioz reads the image as the Temple turned into a refuge for criminals.[^s3]

**Remember Shiloh (7:12-15).** "But go now to my place which was in Shiloh" (7:12, WEB): what God did there he will do to the house "in which you trust" (7:14, WEB), and he will cast Judah out as he cast out Ephraim (7:15).[^s1]

**The sermon's aftermath (Jeremiah 26).** When Jeremiah warned that God would "make this house like Shiloh" (26:6, KJV), priests, prophets and people seized him and said he must die.[^s2] Before the princes of Judah, he answered that God had sent him and urged them to change their ways (26:10-15).[^s2] Some elders recalled that Micah had prophesied in Hezekiah's day that "Zion shall be plowed like a field", and that Hezekiah, instead of killing him, "besought the LORD" so that the LORD relented (26:18-19, KJV), quoting Micah 3:12.[^s2][^s8] The officials ruled, "This man is not worthy to die" (26:16, KJV).[^s2] Another prophet, Urijah, was killed by Jehoiakim, but Ahikam son of Shaphan protected Jeremiah (26:20-24).[^s2]

## Interpretation

Jeremiah was not against the Temple. Avioz argues that his target was the contempt for the covenant that temple worship could cover up.[^s3] Shortly afterwards God tells him not even to pray for this people (7:16), and says that when he brought Israel out of Egypt his chief command was "Listen to my voice, and I will be your God, and you shall be my people" (7:21-23, WEB).[^s1] Jewish congregations read a haftarah beginning at Jeremiah 7:21 alongside *Tzav*, the Torah portion on the sacrifices, hearing in it that God cares more for obedience than for empty offerings; the commentator Malbim warned against relying on the Temple's forgiveness while continuing to sin.[^s12][^s11]

Jonathan Bentall reads both accounts as a **conditional** warning: judgment is announced so that the hearers will repent and judgment can be turned aside, and the way chapter 26 retells the sermon supports that reading.[^s4] The elders' memory of Micah shows the same pattern (26:19).[^s2]

**Common misreadings.** One is to treat the sermon as God rejecting worship or the Temple itself, which reads past the call to "amend your ways" and the promise to let them dwell in the land.[^s1][^s3] Another is to use it to caricature Judaism as empty ritual. The sermon is an inside word from a prophet of Israel to his own people, and Jewish tradition itself reads it as a summons to integrity.[^s11] Its challenge applies to any community of faith, Christian churches included, that trusts its buildings or rituals while neglecting justice.[^s3]

**Jesus and Jeremiah 7.** When Jesus drove out the sellers and money-changers he combined Isaiah 56:7, God's promise that his house would be "my house of prayer" for all peoples, with Jeremiah 7:11 (Mark 11:15-17).[^s7][^s10] The IVP commentary on Matthew's parallel calls the act a prophetic sign of coming judgment, like Jeremiah's own symbolic acts, and notes that the quotation exposes the illusion that the Temple shields those whose conduct opposes God's will.[^s6] In Mark the chief priests and scribes respond, like Jeremiah's accusers, by seeking how they might destroy him (11:18).[^s7]

## Connections

The sermon is **part of** the Book of Jeremiah (7:1-15) and is told again in chapter 26.[^s1][^s2] It **features** the prophet Jeremiah and King Jehoiakim, in whose reign it was preached,[^s2] the Temple where Jeremiah stood,[^s1] and Shiloh, the ruined sanctuary he held up as a warning.[^s5] In Jeremiah 26:18 the elders **quote** the Book of Micah (Micah 3:12).[^s2][^s8] It **teaches** about **worship** that cannot be separated from covenant faithfulness[^s3] and about **repentance**, since its warnings are conditional.[^s4] It is related to **Jerusalem**, to the **Babylonian Exile** that followed, to Jesus's **cleansing of the Temple**, where Jeremiah 7:11 is quoted, and to **Reading in Context**: "the temple of the LORD" was a slogan lifted from God's promises without their conditions.[^s1][^s7][^s3]
