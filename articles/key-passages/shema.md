# The Shema

The Shema is Deuteronomy 6:4-9, named after its first Hebrew word, *shema*, "hear". It opens with Israel's great confession, "Hear, Israel: Yahweh is our God; Yahweh is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4, WEB), and goes straight on to the call to love God "with all your heart, and with all your soul, and with all your might" (6:5, WEB) and to keep his words in the heart, in the home and on the road (6:6-9).[^s1] It became the central confession of Jewish prayer, and Jesus named it the first of all the commandments.[^s9][^s4]

## Why it matters

For Jews the Shema is said every morning and evening, and its first verse has been on the lips of the faithful at death: the Talmud remembers Rabbi Akiba dying as he spoke its last word, *eḥad*, "one".[^s9] When a scribe asked Jesus which commandment comes first, Jesus answered with the Shema itself, beginning "Hear, Israel, the Lord our God, the Lord is one" (Mark 12:29, WEB).[^s4] Christians who confess the Trinity still confess that God is one; the Catechism of the Catholic Church grounds that teaching in Deuteronomy 6:4 and Mark 12:29.[^s12]

## Context

Deuteronomy 6 introduces the commandments Israel is to keep in the land they are about to enter, so that they and their children's children might fear the LORD (6:1-3).[^s1] The chapter then warns that when Israel enjoys cities and vineyards they did not build or plant, they must not forget the God who brought them out of Egypt (6:10-12). It closes with a child asking what the commandments mean, and the answer is a story: "We were Pharaoh's bondservants in Egypt", and the LORD brought us out (6:20-23, WEB).[^s1] The Shema is framed by memory of rescue: Israel's love answers what God has already done.

## Walking through the text

**The confession (6:4).** The call to hear is addressed to the whole people. After it come four Hebrew words, *YHVH eloheinu YHVH eḥad*, literally "YHVH our God YHVH one", which can be translated in more than one way.[^s8]

**Love with everything (6:5).** The confession is followed at once by a command to love the one God with heart, soul and might: the whole person.[^s1]

**Words on the heart and in the home (6:6-7).** These words are to be "on your heart" (6:6, WEB), taught diligently to children, and talked of at home and on the way, lying down and rising up (6:7).[^s1] Jewish interpretation read the last pair as a command to recite these passages at night and in the morning, the origin of the twice-daily Shema.[^s8]

**Signs on hand, head and door (6:8-9).** The words are to be bound as a sign on the hand and between the eyes, and written on doorposts and gates.[^s1] Jewish practice kept these commands in the tefillin bound on the arm and head and in the mezuzah, a small parchment holding Deuteronomy 6:4-9 and 11:13-21 fixed to the doorpost; Josephus already described the mezuzah as an old and well-established custom.[^s9][^s10]

## Interpretation

**One prayer in three parts.** In Jewish worship the Shema came to include Deuteronomy 11:13-21, which again tells Israel to lay these words on heart and hand, teach them to their children and write them on doorposts, and Numbers 15:37-41, on the fringes (*tzitzit*) that remind Israel to "remember and do all my commandments" (Numbers 15:40, WEB).[^s2][^s3][^s9] Twice-daily recitation goes back at least to late Second Temple times, and the Mishnah calls saying the first verse accepting "the yoke of the kingship of God".[^s8][^s9] Tigay notes that the closing blessing uses legal language, so that saying the Shema works like a solemn oath renewing the covenant.[^s8]

**Jesus and the Shema.** In Mark 12:28-32 Jesus quotes Deuteronomy 6:4-5 as "the first commandment" and pairs it with love of neighbour as the second; the scribe agrees that "he is one, and there is none other but he" (12:32, WEB).[^s4][^s5]

**One God, one Lord.** Writing about food offered to idols, Paul affirms that there is no God but one, then says: "yet to us there is one God, the Father ... and one Lord, Jesus Christ" (1 Corinthians 8:4, 6, WEB).[^s6] Following Richard Bauckham, Martin Feltham reads this as Paul reworking the Shema around Christ, placing Jesus within the identity of Israel's one God.[^s11] The Catechism likewise teaches that Jesus affirms God as the one Lord and shows that he himself is Lord, and that this does not contradict faith in one God.[^s12]

**Common misreadings.** One is to treat the Shema as a formula to agree with. James answers that believing "God is one" is good, yet "the demons also believe, and shudder" (James 2:19, WEB); the Shema's own next verse asks for love, not just assent.[^s7][^s1] Another is to treat it as belonging only to Judaism, or as a weapon in Jewish-Christian argument. It is Israel's confession, still prayed daily, and the commandment Jesus put first; Christians can receive it with gratitude and respect.[^s8][^s4]

## Scholarly views

Scholars translate 6:4 in different ways. Jeffrey Tigay sets out three main readings: *YHVH is our God, YHVH alone* (the New JPS rendering, following the medieval commentators Ibn Ezra and Rashbam), which stresses Israel's exclusive loyalty; *the LORD our God, the LORD is one*, the traditional rendering, which speaks of God's uniqueness or indivisibility; and *YHVH our God is one YHVH*, which some read against dividing the LORD into local forms. Tigay tentatively prefers the first, while admitting it is not certain, and notes Stephen Geller's view that "one" means the LORD is supreme.[^s8] The Catechism keeps the traditional wording, and the WEB renders Jesus' quotation in Mark 12:29 the same way.[^s12][^s4]

## Connections

The Shema is **part of** [Deuteronomy](bg:deuteronomy) and **features** the [Israelites](bg:israelites) it addresses.[^s1] It **teaches** [love](bg:love) for God (6:5),[^s1] [monotheism](bg:monotheism), as a confession of the One God, and the [kingdom of God](bg:kingdom-of-god), since reciting it means accepting God's kingship.[^s9] Said as a covenant oath, it also **teaches** the [Mosaic covenant](bg:mosaic-covenant).[^s8] It links to the [Ten Commandments](bg:ten-commandments), Jesus' [great commandment](bg:great-commandment) in the [Gospel of Mark](bg:gospel-of-mark), [loving your neighbour](bg:loving-your-neighbor), [prayer](bg:prayer), [Second Temple Judaism](bg:second-temple-judaism) and [rabbinic Judaism](bg:rabbinic-judaism).[^s4][^s8]
