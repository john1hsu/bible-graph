# Paul at the Areopagus

Driven out of Macedonia, Paul arrived in Athens ahead of his companions and waited there for Silas and Timothy to join him. Acts 17:16-34 tells what happened next: Paul's debates in the synagogue and the marketplace, his summons to the Areopagus, and the speech he gave there, one of the best-known sermons in the New Testament.[^s1] The King James Version calls the place "Mars' hill" (17:22), a translation of the Greek name "hill of Ares".[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

This is the fullest example in Acts of the gospel being preached to people with no background in Israel's Scriptures. Instead of opening with the prophets, Paul begins with the God who made the world and gives "to all life, and breath, and all things" (17:25, KJV), and only then turns to repentance and the risen Jesus.[^s1][^s4] For Christians today, the passage models how to speak of faith with respect and clarity to people who do not share it. It also gave the church a line about God's nearness that is still quoted in teaching today: "in him we live, and move, and have our being" (17:28, KJV).[^s1][^s7]

## Context

Paul came to Athens after being driven out of Thessalonica and Berea (Acts 17:1-15).[^s1] Luke says his spirit "was stirred in him" when he saw a city "wholly given to idolatry" (17:16, KJV), so he reasoned with Jews and God-fearers in the synagogue and with anyone in the marketplace (17:17).[^s1] Epicurean and Stoic philosophers called him a "babbler", and others thought him "a setter forth of strange gods", because he preached "Jesus, and the resurrection" (17:18). Patrick Gray notes that the listeners may have taken the Greek word for resurrection, *anastasis*, as the name of a second deity paired with Jesus.[^s1][^s2]

The Areopagus was both a rocky hill below the Acropolis and the ancient council named after it. By Paul's time the council oversaw religion and education in the city.[^s3] The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* describes Paul's appearance as a hearing about his public teaching rather than a criminal trial. It suggests the council probably met in the Royal Stoa in the marketplace, which could hold a crowd, rather than on the windswept summit.[^s3]

## Walking through the text

1. **A point of contact (17:22-23).** Paul calls the Athenians "too superstitious" (KJV), a Greek word that can also mean "very religious". He then points to an altar inscribed "TO THE UNKNOWN GOD": "Whom therefore ye ignorantly worship, him declare I unto you."[^s1][^s4]
2. **The Creator (17:24-25).** God made the world, "dwelleth not in temples made with hands", and needs nothing from human hands.[^s1] Stephen had said the same about the Temple in Jerusalem, quoting "the prophet": "Hath not my hand made all these things?" (Acts 7:48-50, KJV).[^s8]
3. **One humanity seeking God (17:26-27).** God made all nations "of one blood" and set their times and boundaries so that they would seek him, "though he be not far from every one of us."[^s1]
4. **The poets' witness (17:28-29).** Paul cites "certain also of your own poets": "For we are also his offspring."[^s1] The line comes from the opening hymn to Zeus in Aratus's *Phaenomena*, a Greek poem about the heavens.[^s5][^s2] Paul's conclusion is that the "offspring of God" should not imagine the divine as an image of gold, silver or stone (17:29).[^s1]
5. **The turn (17:30-31).** God overlooked "the times of this ignorance" but "now commandeth all men every where to repent". He has fixed a day of judgment "by that man whom he hath ordained" and has given proof by raising him from the dead.[^s1]

At the mention of resurrection, some mocked, others wanted to hear more, and a few believed. Among them were "Dionysius the Areopagite", a member of the council, and "a woman named Damaris" (17:32-34).[^s1][^s3]

## Interpretation

Readers have long asked how far Paul affirms Athenian religion. The IVP commentary reads the opening as limited and somewhat ironic: the Athenians are religious, but in ignorance. It also notes that Paul's wording avoids simply identifying their unknown god with the true God. The repeated stress on ignorance and the call to repent (17:30) show that he is correcting their worship, not approving it.[^s4] Gray argues that the speech also has the philosophers in view. Stoics and Epicureans already mocked popular idol worship, and in effect, Paul invites them to find in Israel's God what their own critique of idols was reaching for.[^s2]

Paul's theme of God made known through creation echoes Romans 1:19-20, where "that which may be known of God is manifest" in what he has made.[^s9] The Catholic *Catechism* quotes Acts 17:26-28 to teach that humans are by nature religious beings, made to seek God.[^s7]

Was Athens a failure? Gray judges the visible results small. He argues that Luke's readers would still see their own conversions mirrored in the few who believed, a faithful remnant.[^s2]

## Common misreadings

Paul does not praise idolatry or say every religion leads to God. The speech moves from common ground to a call for repentance centred on the risen Jesus.[^s1][^s4] Nor is the speech a purely philosophical lecture. Its picture of a creator who needs no temple and judges the world comes straight from Israel's Scriptures, even where Paul quotes a Greek poet.[^s2][^s8]

## Connections

The speech is **part of** the Acts of the Apostles (17:16-34) and **features** Paul, the city of Athens, and Dionysius the Areopagite.[^s1] Dionysius of Corinth, writing around 170 and quoted by Eusebius, said this Dionysius became the first bishop of Athens.[^s6] The passage **teaches** the resurrection of the dead, the claim that drew both mockery and faith (17:31-32).[^s1] It also **teaches** what theologians call general revelation, the knowledge of God available through creation, as the IVP commentary's discussion of Paul's point of contact shows.[^s4] It is **explained by** the Greco-Roman world of Athenian philosophers and their schools, which Gray uses to read the audience, and by Aratus's *Phaenomena*, the source of Paul's quotation.[^s2][^s5]
