# The Binding of Isaac

Genesis 22:1-19 is one of the most searching stories in the Bible. "God tested Abraham" (22:1, WEB) by telling him to take "your son, your only son, Isaac, whom you love" to the land of Moriah and offer him as a burnt offering (22:2). Abraham goes, binds Isaac on the altar, and takes the knife, until the angel of the LORD calls out to stop him; a ram caught in a thicket is offered instead, and God swears to bless Abraham and, through his offspring, all the nations (22:9-18).[^s1] Jewish tradition calls it the *Akedah*, "the binding", after the verb in verse 9.[^s6]

## Why it matters

The chapter is the climax of Abraham's story: the son through whom every promise was to come is the son God asks for. Judaism made it an image of faithful obedience and martyrdom,[^s6] and the New Testament returns to it to speak of faith (Hebrews 11:17-19; James 2:21-23) and of the God who "didn't spare his own Son" (Romans 8:32, WEB).[^s2][^s3][^s4]

## Context

The story begins "after these things" (22:1), once the long-awaited Isaac has been born. The reader is told at once that this is a test; Abraham is not.[^s1] Scholars note how much rides on that opening, read together with the angel's second speech (22:15-18).[^s8] The chapter ends with Abraham returning to Beersheba, followed by news of his brother Nahor's family, which introduces Rebekah, Isaac's future wife (22:19-23).[^s1]

## Walking through the text

**The command (22:1-2).** God calls, and Abraham answers, "Here I am." The command piles up the cost: son, only son, Isaac, whom you love.[^s1]

**The journey (22:3-8).** Abraham splits the wood and travels three days, telling the servants that he and the boy will worship and "come back to you" (22:5, WEB). He lays the wood on Isaac and carries the fire and the knife himself. Then comes the story's one conversation: Isaac asks, "where is the lamb for a burnt offering?" and Abraham replies, "God will provide himself the lamb for a burnt offering, my son" (22:7-8, WEB).[^s1]

**The altar (22:9-14).** Abraham builds the altar, arranges the wood, binds Isaac and lays him on it. The angel calls twice, "Abraham, Abraham!", and forbids him to touch the boy: "now I know that you fear God, since you have not withheld your son" (22:12, WEB). Abraham sees a ram caught by its horns, offers it "instead of his son", and names the place "Yahweh Will Provide" (22:13-14).[^s1]

**The oath (22:15-19).** The angel calls again. God swears by himself to bless Abraham, multiply his offspring like the stars and the sand, and bless all the nations of the earth through his offspring, "because you have obeyed my voice" (22:18, WEB).[^s1]

## Interpretation

**What kind of test?** The text does not say why God tested Abraham. Medieval Jewish thinkers asked how an all-knowing God could "learn" anything: Maimonides read the test as showing how far love and fear of God can reach, and Nahmanides related it to foreknowledge and free will.[^s6] The Mishnah (Avot 5:3) counts it as the last of Abraham's ten trials.[^s6]

**Jewish reading and worship.** Genesis 22 is read on the second day of Rosh Hashanah, whose prayers ask God to remember the binding, and the ram's horn (*shofar*) recalls the ram offered in Isaac's place.[^s6][^s7] The story became a lasting image of Jewish faithfulness under persecution.[^s6][^s7]

**Christian reading.** Hebrews says Abraham reasoned that God could raise the dead, and that "figuratively speaking" he did receive Isaac back from the dead (11:19, WEB).[^s2] James asks whether Abraham was not "justified by works" when he offered Isaac, and says that "by works faith was perfected" (2:21-22, WEB), so fulfilling the Scripture that Abraham believed God (2:23).[^s3] The church fathers read the story as a type of Christ: Irenaeus says Abraham's readiness points to God being pleased to offer "His own beloved and only-begotten Son",[^s13] and Tertullian sees Isaac carrying the wood as Christ carrying "the 'wood' of His own passion".[^s12] The Catechism of the Catholic Church presents it as the final purifying of Abraham's faith, linking it to Romans 8:32,[^s10] and the Catholic Easter Vigil reads Genesis 22:1-18 as its second reading.[^s11] Moberly traces Christian reading from the New Testament to Protestant interpreters such as Gerhard von Rad.[^s9]

**Common misreadings.** The story is no warrant for harming a child or obeying a voice that demands violence: its turning point is God stopping the knife and providing a substitute, and rabbinic tradition applied the prophets' condemnations of child sacrifice to this story too, insisting that God never desired it.[^s1][^s6][^s7] Nor does the text say how old Isaac was. It calls him "the boy", yet he carries the wood; one midrash makes him 37, while the medieval commentator Ibn Ezra judged him younger.[^s1][^s6]

## Scholarly views

Tradition receives Genesis, with the rest of the Pentateuch, as the work of Moses. Source critics usually assign the chapter to the Elohist source, because it calls God *Elohim*, with the name YHWH added by an editor; others assign it to the Yahwist, drawing on older material.[^s6] Many think the angel's second speech (22:15-18) was added later, which also affects how the opening "test" is read.[^s8] Hermann Gunkel read the story as explaining why a ram replaced a child at an ancient sanctuary, and John Skinner as a protest against human sacrifice; Lombaard urges interpreters to name their methods, since each shapes what they see.[^s6][^s8] The land of Moriah is linked in 2 Chronicles 3:1 to the Temple mount in Jerusalem, a link Jewish tradition embraced, while some scholars see Moriah as a once-separate sacred site whose name the story explains.[^s5][^s6][^s8]

## Connections

- The binding of Isaac is **part of** the Book of Genesis (22:1-19).[^s1]
- It **features** Abraham, Isaac and the land of **Moriah**, which 2 Chronicles 3:1 links to the Temple site in Jerusalem.[^s1][^s5]
- It **teaches** faith: Hebrews 11:17 begins, "By faith, Abraham, being tested, offered up Isaac" (WEB).[^s2]
- It **teaches** the Abrahamic covenant, which God reaffirms by oath in 22:16-18.[^s1]
- Christian tradition reads it as **prefiguring** the crucifixion of Jesus, with Isaac bearing the wood as Christ bore the cross.[^s12][^s13]
