# Psalm 22

Psalm 22 is a prayer headed "A psalm of David" that opens with the most famous cry of abandonment in Scripture: "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Psalm 22:1, WEB).[^s1][^s2] It is a lament that moves from feeling forsaken, mocked and surrounded to a vow of praise so wide that the ends of the earth and people not yet born are drawn in.[^s9] Jesus prayed its first words from the cross, and the Gospels tell the story of the crucifixion in its language.[^s4][^s3]

## Why it matters

The psalm shows that faith can speak honestly to God in the dark: the psalmist gets no answer day or night, yet keeps saying "my God", because there is nowhere else to turn.[^s1][^s9] And when Jesus cried "Eloi, Eloi, lama sabachthani?" (Mark 15:34, WEB), he prayed this psalm; Benckhuysen notes it is now almost impossible to read it without thinking of the cross.[^s4][^s8]

## Context

Psalm 22 belongs to the Book of Psalms, Israel's book of prayer and praise; its heading names a melody, "The Doe of the Morning".[^s1][^s2] English Bibles count the heading separately, while the Hebrew text and some Catholic Bibles count it as verse 1, so their verse numbers run one higher: English 22:16 is 22:17 in the Hebrew.[^s2][^s11] The NABRE calls it a lament unusual both in its structure and in the intensity of its feeling.[^s2]

## Walking through the text

**The cry and the memory (22:1-11).** God does not answer by day or night (22:1-2), yet the psalmist remembers that "our fathers trusted in you ... and you delivered them" (22:4, WEB) and that God has held him since birth (22:9-10).[^s1] Rav Elchanan Samet reads these memories as both a painful contrast and a source of hope.[^s10] Between them comes the mockery, "I am a worm, and no man" (22:6, WEB), as onlookers sneer, "let him deliver him" (22:7-8); then the plea, "Don't be far from me, for trouble is near" (22:11, WEB).[^s1]

**Surrounded (22:12-21).** Bulls, lions and dogs encircle him; he is "poured out like water" and brought "into the dust of death" (22:14-15, WEB). His hands and feet are attacked (22:16), and his garments are divided by lot (22:18).[^s1] He prays again, "don't be far off, Yahweh" (22:19, WEB), and in the middle of verse 21 the prayer suddenly becomes testimony: "you have answered me."[^s1]

**The widening praise (22:22-31).** The psalmist vows to declare God's name to his brothers in the assembly, because God "has not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted" (22:24, WEB).[^s1] The praise then reaches the humble, the ends of the earth and the nations, because "the kingdom is Yahweh's" (22:28, WEB), and generations yet unborn who will hear "he has done it" (22:31, WEB).[^s1] Enter the Bible observes that this reach, taking in even the dead, goes beyond an ordinary lament.[^s9]

## Interpretation

**In the Gospels and Hebrews.** Matthew and Mark give Jesus' cry from the cross in the psalm's own words (Matthew 27:46; Mark 15:34).[^s3][^s4] Matthew describes soldiers dividing his clothing by lot, passers-by "wagging their heads", and the taunt "He trusts in God. Let God deliver him now" (Matthew 27:35, 39, 43, WEB), all echoing Psalm 22:7-8 and 18.[^s3][^s2][^s8] John says the soldiers cast lots for the seamless coat so "that the Scripture might be fulfilled", and quotes 22:18 (John 19:23-24, WEB).[^s5] Hebrews puts 22:22 on the lips of Christ, who is not ashamed to call those he saves his brothers (Hebrews 2:10-12).[^s6]

**In the early church.** Justin Martyr quoted the whole psalm, arguing that it shows the Son praying to the Father as a man truly able to suffer.[^s13] Augustine heard Christ speaking in it, sometimes in his own person and sometimes for his body, the Church.[^s12]

**In Jewish reading.** Rashi read the psalm as David's prayer for Israel in future exile, noted that the Sages connected its heading to Queen Esther, and took the last verses to point to the messianic age.[^s11] Many Jewish communities recite it on Purim.[^s10] Christians can respect that this is also Israel's prayer, prayed by Jews in their own suffering.

**Common misreadings.** One is to hear the first verse as mere despair, as if lament were a failure of faith; the psalm keeps speaking to "my God" and ends in praise.[^s9] Another is to rush past the anguish, though Benckhuysen calls the victory hard-won.[^s8] A third is to treat the psalm only as predictions, forgetting it is first a real sufferer's prayer.[^s9]

## Scholarly views

**Verse 16.** The Masoretic Hebrew reads "like a lion, my hands and my feet", a line with no verb.[^s14] Rashi and other medieval Jewish commentators read it as hands and feet crushed as in a lion's mouth.[^s11] The Septuagint has "they dug", the Syriac Peshitta "they have pierced", and a Psalms fragment from Nahal Hever, the oldest clear Hebrew witness, has a verb form (*ka'aru*) rather than "like a lion".[^s14] The *Psalms: Layer by Layer* project judges that the evidence favours a verb, though the verb's exact meaning is uncertain.[^s14] The NET Bible keeps the lion ("like a lion they pin my hands and feet") and notes that the New Testament never cites this verse for Jesus' death.[^s7] The WEB has "they pierced my hands and my feet" (22:16).[^s1]

**Jesus' cry.** Interpreters weigh Jesus' words differently. Benckhuysen stresses real anguish, both solidarity with sufferers and suffering on behalf of others.[^s8] Others, reading the cry with the rest of the psalm, also hear its turn to praise; Enter the Bible notes that the New Testament takes up the closing "he has done it" to announce God's work in Jesus.[^s9] Augustine heard Christ speaking for his sinful members.[^s12] Many readers hold these together.

## Connections

Psalm 22 is **part of** the [Book of Psalms](bg:book-of-psalms)[^s1] and **attributed to** [David](bg:david) by its heading.[^s2] It **speaks to** [suffering and lament](bg:suffering-and-lament), giving words to those who feel forsaken.[^s9] Its declaration that "the kingdom is Yahweh's" **teaches** the [kingdom of God](bg:kingdom-of-god).[^s1] Christian interpreters from Justin to Augustine read it as **prefiguring** the [crucifixion of Jesus](bg:crucifixion-of-jesus), which the [Gospel of Mark](bg:gospel-of-mark), the [Gospel of Matthew](bg:gospel-of-matthew) and the [Gospel of John](bg:gospel-of-john) tell in its words.[^s12][^s13][^s3][^s5] It is **preserved in** the [Masoretic Text](bg:masoretic-text), the [Septuagint](bg:septuagint) and a Nahal Hever fragment among the [Dead Sea Scrolls](bg:dead-sea-scrolls), which differ at verse 16.[^s14] It is [Hebrew poetry](bg:hebrew-poetry) shaped for [prayer](bg:prayer).[^s9]
