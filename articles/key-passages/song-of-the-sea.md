# The Song of the Sea

The Song of the Sea is the hymn Israel sings on the far shore of the sea in Exodus 15. Moses and the people sing that the LORD "has thrown the horse and his rider into the sea" (Exodus 15:1, WEB), and Miriam the prophetess leads the women in answering the same words with drums and dancing (15:20-21).[^s1] It is the first great song of praise in the Bible, and Jews and Christians have kept singing it ever since.[^s5][^s9]

## Why it matters

The song shows what to do after God saves: tell the story back to him in praise. Its closing line, "Yahweh will reign forever and ever" (15:18, WEB), proclaims God as Israel's everlasting King, and the Encyclopaedia Judaica treats this declaration of God's eternal sovereignty as the song's main theme.[^s1][^s5] Later Scripture borrows its words, and Revelation puts "the song of Moses" on the lips of the redeemed.[^s4][^s3]

## Context

Exodus 14 tells how a strong east wind drove the sea back, Israel crossed on dry ground with the waters "a wall" on either side, and the returning sea covered Pharaoh's chariots (Exodus 14:21-29). The chapter ends with Israel seeing what God had done: "the people feared Yahweh; and they believed in Yahweh and in his servant Moses" (14:31, WEB).[^s2] The song is their response. Afterwards the story moves straight on to the bitter water at Marah and the springs at Elim (15:22-27), where the singing people soon begin to grumble.[^s1]

## Walking through the text

**The victory at the sea (15:1-12).** The song opens with personal praise: "Yah is my strength and song" (15:2, WEB), and the LORD has become the singer's salvation. It calls him "a man of war" (15:3), retells how his right hand shattered the enemy and the waters piled up at his breath, and asks, "Who is like you, Yahweh, among the gods?" (15:11, WEB).[^s1] Fox notes that the sea here is no rival god but a passive tool of God's will.[^s5]

**The journey ahead (15:13-17).** In his loving kindness God has led "the people that you have redeemed" (15:13, WEB) toward his holy dwelling. The nations of Philistia, Edom, Moab and Canaan tremble as the people pass by, and God will plant them on his mountain, in the sanctuary his own hands established (15:14-17).[^s1]

**The King (15:18).** Everything resolves into one line: the LORD will reign forever.[^s1]

**Miriam's answer (15:19-21).** A prose verse recalls the crossing, then Miriam, "the sister of Aaron", takes up her drum and leads the women, singing back the song's opening words (15:20-21).[^s1] Carol Meyers notes that the Hebrew *tof* is a hand-held frame drum, so "tambourine" is an anachronism, and that Miriam's scene begins a biblical pattern of women celebrating God's victories with drum, dance and song.[^s8]

## Interpretation

**Praise that keeps being sung.** The confession of 15:2 reappears almost word for word in Psalm 118:14 and Isaiah 12:2, so later worshippers could make Israel's first rescue song their own.[^s4] Jewish tradition writes the song in a special brick-like pattern in the Torah scroll, sings it in the daily morning prayers, and reads it on Shabbat Shirah ("the Sabbath of Song") and on the seventh day of Passover.[^s5]

**From the sea to the Lamb.** In Revelation 15:2-3 those who have overcome stand beside a sea of glass and sing "the song of Moses, the servant of God, and the song of the Lamb" (15:3, WEB): the Exodus has become a picture of final salvation.[^s3] Christians across traditions hear the song at Easter. In the Catholic Easter Vigil the reading of the sea crossing must always be used, followed by Exodus 15 as the response, and Reginald Fuller calls the crossing the supreme type of Christ's death and resurrection and of the believer's dying and rising with him in baptism.[^s10] The Orthodox Church in America appoints Exodus 13:20-15:19 as a reading at Vespers on Holy Saturday,[^s11] and Anglican daily prayer includes the song as the canticle "The Song of Moses", especially suited to the Easter season.[^s12] John Paul II, commenting on it as a morning canticle, called it "a canticle, not of war, but of love".[^s9]

**Common misreadings.** "A man of war" (15:3) is not a celebration of human violence: in the story Israel does not fight at all, and even the Egyptians cry out that the LORD is fighting for Israel (14:25).[^s2][^s1] And Miriam is not a footnote; the text calls her a prophetess and has her lead the people's response.[^s1][^s8]

## Scholarly views

The text presents the song as sung by Moses and Israel at the sea (15:1).[^s1] Many scholars think it is among the oldest poems in the Hebrew Bible. F. M. Cross and D. N. Freedman dated it to the 12th-10th centuries BC from its spelling and language, and Peter Craigie judged its archaic grammar to place it securely among early Hebrew poetry; other proposals run as late as the monarchy or the end of the First Temple period.[^s5][^s6] Readers differ over verses 13-17: Craigie took them as looking ahead to the conquest, while Loewenstamm read the sanctuary as Solomon's Temple on Zion.[^s6][^s5] Miriam's couplet (15:21) may be the original core of the song or a refrain sung with it; Cross and Freedman saw it as a title from another tradition, Janzen argued for its priority, and a Dead Sea Scrolls fragment (4Q365) shows ancient readers expanding Miriam's song.[^s5][^s7]

## Connections

- The song is **part of** the Book of Exodus, closing the account of the crossing.[^s1]
- It **features** the Exodus: the Encyclopaedia Judaica calls it probably the oldest surviving account of the sea crossing.[^s5]
- It **features** Moses, Miriam and the Israelites, who sing it (15:1, 15:20-21).[^s1]
- It **teaches** redemption, naming Israel the people God "redeemed" (15:13),[^s1] and the **kingdom of God** in its closing confession that the LORD reigns forever.[^s5]
- Its **genre** is Hebrew poetry, which Craigie treats as among the earliest examples in the Bible.[^s6]
