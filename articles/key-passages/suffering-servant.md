# The Suffering Servant

Isaiah 52:13–53:12 is a fifteen-verse poem about a figure God calls "my servant".[^s1] It opens, "Behold, my servant will deal wisely" (Isaiah 52:13, WEB), promising that he will be exalted; it turns to a man despised and rejected (53:3); and it ends with him bearing the sin of many and praying for transgressors (53:12).[^s1][^s2] The New Testament quotes and echoes it again and again as it explains the death of Jesus.[^s9]

## Why it matters

When an Ethiopian official reading this chapter in his chariot asked Philip whom the prophet meant, Philip "began at the same scripture, and preached unto him Jesus" (Acts 8:35, KJV).[^s3] Jesus applied the line "he was reckoned among the transgressors" to himself (Luke 22:37, KJV),[^s4] Matthew presents 53:4 as fulfilled in Jesus's ministry (Matthew 8:17),[^s5] and 1 Peter 2:24 echoes 53:5: "by whose stripes ye were healed" (KJV).[^s6]

## Context

The poem stands in the second half of Isaiah, among several passages about the Lord's servant.[^s9][^s11] In 1892 Bernhard Duhm singled out four "Servant Songs" there: Isaiah 42:1-4, 49:1-6, 50:4-9 and 52:13–53:12.[^s11][^s9] In the first, the Servant is one God upholds and fills with his Spirit, who will "bring justice to the nations" (Isaiah 42:1, WEB).[^s7] In the second God tells him, "Thou art my servant, O Israel" (Isaiah 49:3, KJV),[^s8] so his identity has long been debated.[^s11] The Great Isaiah Scroll from Qumran, some two thousand years old, contains all sixty-six chapters, this poem included.[^s13]

## Walking through the text

**God's verdict: the Servant will be exalted (52:13-15).** The Servant will act wisely and be lifted up. Many were appalled at how marred he was, yet he will "cleanse many nations", and kings will fall silent at what they had never been told.[^s1]

**A confession: we misjudged him (53:1-3).** A group of speakers asks who has believed their message. The Servant grew up like a root out of dry ground, with nothing in his looks to draw them, and they held him in no regard.[^s2]

**The turn: he suffered for us (53:4-6).** The speakers realise that the pain they thought was God's punishment of him was theirs: "he was pierced for our transgressions", and by his wounds they are healed (53:5, WEB). Like straying sheep, they had each gone their own way, and Yahweh laid on him the iniquity of them all (53:6).[^s2]

**Silent, condemned and buried (53:7-9).** Oppressed, he did not open his mouth, like a lamb led to slaughter. He was cut off from the land of the living for others' disobedience and buried with the wicked and the rich, though he had done no violence and spoken no deceit.[^s2]

**God's purpose: the suffering bears fruit (53:10-12).** It was Yahweh's will to crush him; his life is made "an offering for sin", yet he will see offspring and prolong his days. After his suffering "he will see the light and be satisfied", and the righteous Servant will justify many by bearing their iniquities (53:10-11, WEB). Because he poured out his life to death, God gives him a portion with the great.[^s2]

## Interpretation

At the poem's centre one suffers what others deserve, and they are made whole. The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* sums up 53:4-10 as the Servant bearing their penalty, his death serving as a sin offering on their behalf.[^s9] The word used in 53:10 is the Hebrew *asham*, the guilt offering, and R. T. France argued that Jesus's saying that he came "to give his life a ransom for many" (Mark 10:45, KJV)[^s15] deliberately draws on this verse and on the repeated "many" of 53:11-12.[^s12]

Christians across traditions read the poem as fulfilled in Jesus's death and vindication.[^s9][^s11] Its Christian use began early: Clement of Rome quoted it at length as the Holy Spirit's portrait of Christ, to commend humility to the church in Corinth, since the Lord did not come "in the pomp of pride or arrogance".[^s14]

A careless reading treats the poem only as a list of matching details. A careful one keeps its own movement in view: the shock of the onlookers, their confession and the Servant's vindication. It is the onlookers' "we" that the New Testament invites readers to join (1 Peter 2:24).[^s2][^s6]

## Scholarly views

**Who is the Servant?** Interpreters have proposed collective answers (Israel as a whole, an ideal Israel or a faithful remnant) and individual ones (the prophet himself, Moses, Jeremiah, a king such as Jehoiachin, or the coming Messiah).[^s11][^s9] Shalom Paul notes in the *Encyclopaedia Judaica* that most of the individual historical candidates now have few supporters.[^s11]

**Jewish readings.** The Aramaic Targum calls the Servant the Messiah while applying the sufferings to others, such as Israel or the nations.[^s9] The *Jewish Encyclopedia* records that Saadia identified him with Jeremiah and that Rashi, Ibn Ezra and Kimhi read the confession of chapter 53 as spoken by the nations of 52:15, so that the suffering Servant is Israel, a view many modern critics revived.[^s10] It also notes that both Jewish and Christian tradition have recognised in the poem a picture of the Messiah.[^s10]

**Christian readings.** The New Testament writers are unanimous that Jesus is the Servant.[^s9] Some scholars, such as C. K. Barrett and M. D. Hooker, doubted that Jesus himself drew on Isaiah 53; France answered that his sayings at the Last Supper and in Luke 22:37 show he understood his death through it.[^s12][^s4] The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* itself favours a combined view: the Servant is the ideal Israel, summed up in one individual.[^s9]

## Connections

The poem is **part of** the **Book of Isaiah**, among its passages about the Lord's servant.[^s2][^s9] It **teaches** the doctrine of **atonement** through the Servant who bears others' sins as a sin offering.[^s9] Christians read it as **prefiguring** the **crucifixion of Jesus**, as Philip does in **Acts**, Jesus does in **Luke**, and Matthew and 1 Peter do in their echoes.[^s9][^s3][^s4][^s5][^s6] Its text is **preserved in** the **Dead Sea Scrolls**, whose Great Isaiah Scroll contains the whole book.[^s13] Like **Psalm 22**, it gave the Gospel writers language for the cross, and its interpretation is part of the conversation between Christians and **rabbinic Judaism**.[^s9][^s10]
