# Parable of the Good Samaritan

Few stories Jesus told are better known than this one. The parable is told in Luke 10:25-37, and Luke never actually calls the Samaritan "good"; what the story stresses is that he was "moved with compassion" and "showed mercy" (Luke 10:33, 37, World English Bible).[^s1][^s8]

## Why it matters

The parable is Jesus's own answer to a very practical question: whom am I bound to love? It takes the command "love your neighbor as yourself" from Leviticus 19:18 and shows what it looks like on a dangerous road, and it does so by making a despised outsider the example to follow.[^s1][^s4] Early Christian readers also saw in the rescuer a portrait of Christ himself.[^s9][^s10]

## Context

Luke sets the parable on Jesus's journey to Jerusalem. Shortly before it, a Samaritan village refuses to receive Jesus because he is heading for Jerusalem, and James and John offer to call down fire on it; Jesus rebukes them (Luke 9:51-56).[^s2] Jews and Samaritans shared the books of Moses but quarrelled over where God should be worshipped, Mount Gerizim or Jerusalem, and John's Gospel remarks that "Jews have no dealings with Samaritans" (John 4:9, 20, WEB).[^s3][^s8][^s11] The *International Standard Bible Encyclopedia* reads the parable as evidence that Jesus's love reached past that hostility.[^s11]

The man was "going down" from Jerusalem to Jericho (Luke 10:30); the main route, the Ascent of Adummim, ran beside the deep gorge of the Wadi Qilt, where BiblePlaces locates the story.[^s1][^s12] An older episode may lie in the background: men who then went home to Samaria clothed, fed and anointed a band of captives, put the weak on donkeys and brought them to Jericho, "to their brothers" (2 Chronicles 28:15, WEB). Whether Jesus was echoing it is an open question.[^s7][^s8]

## Walking through the text

**The question (Luke 10:25-29).** A lawyer, an expert in the law of Moses, asks Jesus what he must do to inherit eternal life. Jesus turns the question back, and the lawyer answers with two commands: love God with all one's heart, soul, strength and mind (Deuteronomy 6:4-5) and love one's neighbor as oneself (Leviticus 19:18). Jesus says he has answered correctly. But the man, "desiring to justify himself," asks, "Who is my neighbor?"[^s1][^s6][^s4]

**The story (Luke 10:30-35).** A man going down to Jericho is stripped, beaten and left "half dead." A priest comes by and passes on the other side; so does a Levite. Then a Samaritan traveller sees him, is moved with compassion, binds his wounds with oil and wine, sets him on his own animal, takes him to an inn and cares for him through the night. Next day he pays the innkeeper two denarii and promises to cover any further cost on his return.[^s1]

**The turn (Luke 10:36-37).** Jesus does not answer the lawyer's question; he asks a different one: which of the three proved to be a neighbor to the man? The lawyer answers, "He who showed mercy on him," and Jesus replies, "Go and do likewise" (WEB).[^s1]

## Interpretation

**From "who qualifies?" to "will I be one?"** The lawyer wanted to know where the duty of love stops. Jesus's closing question moves the focus from the person in need to the one who could help: the issue is not whom I must love but whose neighbor I will become. Leviticus itself already pressed the command outward, telling Israel to love the foreigner living among them "as yourself" (Leviticus 19:33-34).[^s1][^s4]

**Why did the priest and the Levite pass by?** A common explanation is that they feared corpse impurity, since the law forbade a priest to make himself unclean for the dead except for close relatives (Leviticus 21:1-3).[^s5] Maurice Ryan notes problems with this: the man was alive, the rule concerned priests and not Levites, both men were going *away* from Jerusalem, and Luke gives no motive at all. Fear for their own safety, among other ordinary reasons, is just as possible; the text leaves the question open, and Ryan suggests the two men are realistic, flawed people in whom readers can recognise themselves.[^s8]

**Not a story against Judaism.** Ryan shows how later Christian readers sometimes made the priest and the Levite stand for Judaism and the Samaritan for Christianity, a reading that fed claims of Christian superiority. But Jesus, a Jew, told this story to a Jewish expert in the Torah, and the commands at its centre come straight from the Torah.[^s8][^s1]

**Christ the Good Samaritan.** Origen of Alexandria recorded an early reading in which the wounded man is Adam, Jericho the world, the Samaritan Christ, the inn the Church and the Samaritan's promised return the Second Coming. Origen thought it a worthy reading but turned it at once into a call to show the same mercy.[^s10] Modern scholars long dismissed such allegory, but Mikeal Parsons argues that a Christ-centred reading fits Luke well, since showing mercy is God's own work in Luke, and that it strengthens rather than replaces the command to imitate the Samaritan.[^s9] Parsons also notes that Luke 10-11 alternates between loving one's neighbor and loving the Lord: this story is followed at once by the scene with Martha and Mary (Luke 10:38-42).[^s9][^s1]

## Connections

- **Gospel of Luke** (`partOf`): Luke 10:25-37.[^s1]
- **Loving your neighbor** (`speaksTo`) and **love** (`teaches`): it is Jesus's answer to "Who is my neighbor?", shown as concrete mercy.[^s1]
- **Samaritans, Levites, Jericho** (`features`): the hero, one who passes by, and the road's destination.[^s1][^s12]
- **Second Temple Judaism** (`explainedBy`): Jewish-Samaritan relations and purity law of the period shaped how first hearers understood it.[^s8]
- It sits beside the **Great Commandment**, which joins the same two commands from Deuteronomy 6:5 and Leviticus 19:18.[^s6][^s4]
