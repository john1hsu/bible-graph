# Ruth's Vow to Naomi

On the road from Moab to Bethlehem, a young widow refuses to turn back. "Where you go, I will go; and where you stay, I will stay. Your people will be my people, and your God my God" (Ruth 1:16, WEB).[^s1] With these words Ruth binds herself to her mother-in-law Naomi, and the book turns on them. The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* sums up the moment simply: Ruth would not be persuaded, and chose to stay with Naomi and trust in the LORD.[^s12]

## Why it matters

These famous lines are about loyalty that costs something. Ruth gives up her homeland, her family's gods and any hope of remarriage among her own people to stay with a bereaved, older woman who has nothing to offer her.[^s1][^s9] The NABRE notes call it a profound oath of loyalty, sealed so completely that even burial will not separate the two women.[^s3]

## Context

The story opens "in the days when the judges judged" with a famine (Ruth 1:1, WEB). Elimelech of Bethlehem takes his wife Naomi and their two sons to Moab; there he dies, the sons marry Moabite women, Orpah and Ruth, and then the sons die too (1:1-5).[^s1] When Naomi hears that the LORD has "visited his people in giving them bread" (1:6, WEB), she sets out for home with both daughters-in-law.[^s1]

On the way she urges them to go back to their mothers' houses, praying that the LORD will "deal kindly" with them as they have dealt with the dead and with her (1:8-9, WEB).[^s1] The NABRE notes that this word, *hesed*, loyal kindness, recurs at 2:20 and 3:10 and works both between God and people and between people.[^s3] She has no more sons for them, she says, and "Yahweh's hand has gone out against me" (1:11-13, WEB).[^s1]

## Walking through the text

**Two farewells (1:14-15).** Both women weep. Orpah kisses Naomi goodbye, "but Ruth joined with her" (1:14, WEB); Young's Literal Translation renders the verb "cleaved", and Hutton notes that in cleaving to Naomi, Ruth binds herself to Naomi's people and God.[^s13][^s6] Naomi points out that Orpah has gone back "to her people, and to her god" and tells Ruth to follow (1:15, WEB).[^s13] The text never condemns Orpah: she does what Naomi asked.[^s6]

**The vow (1:16-17).** Ruth answers with six promises that move from the road to the grave: to go where Naomi goes, stay where she stays, take her people and her God, die where she dies, and be buried there.[^s1] Erin Hutton reads the speech as a short Hebrew poem built on parallel lines and the key verbs "go" and "return".[^s6] Ruth seals it with an oath, "May Yahweh do so to me, and more also, if anything but death parts you and me" (1:17, WEB).[^s1] Keil and Delitzsch note that this is a stock oath formula in which the speaker calls a penalty down on herself if she breaks her word; the NABRE notes find it often in Samuel and Kings.[^s8][^s3] Ruth swears by the name of Israel's God, not Moab's.[^s1]

**Silence and arrival (1:18-22).** Seeing Ruth "steadfastly minded to go with her," Naomi stops arguing (1:18, WEB).[^s13] In Bethlehem she asks to be called Mara, "bitter": "I went out full, and Yahweh has brought me home again empty" (1:20-21, WEB).[^s1] Yet Ruth is beside her, and they arrive "in the beginning of barley harvest" (1:22, WEB).[^s1] The NABRE note on 1:21 observes that in crying out like this, the faithful Israelite opens the door to change.[^s3]

## Interpretation

**A confession of faith.** Many Christian readers hear "your God my God" as Ruth turning to the God of Israel. William Schumacher of Concordia Seminary calls it a confession of faith, set against Orpah's return to her own gods; Matthew Henry saw in it the grace of God inclining the soul to choose the better part.[^s9][^s8] Boaz later praises Ruth for leaving "your father, your mother, and the land of your birth" for an unknown people and for taking refuge under the wings of the God of Israel (2:11-12, WEB).[^s2] Readers have long heard in this an echo of God's call to Abram, "Get out of your country, and from your relatives" (Genesis 12:1, WEB), and scholars such as Gabriel Cohn and Phyllis Trible have drawn the parallel.[^s4][^s11]

**Loyalty to a person.** Leon Kass reads the vow first as devotion to Naomi herself: Ruth takes Naomi's people and God because they are Naomi's, and her oath is a self-curse rather than a formal conversion, which he thinks an anachronistic term.[^s10] The two readings need not compete: love for Naomi and trust in her God arrive in the same breath.

**Jewish readings.** In rabbinic tradition Ruth is the model convert. The midrash imagines Naomi explaining Israel's way of life and Ruth accepting each part in turn; the Talmud draws some laws of conversion from their conversation and contrasts Orpah, "the one who kissed", with Ruth, "the one who clung".[^s7]

**Common misreadings.** The vow is often read at weddings as a promise between spouses. Schumacher notes that it is not, in context, a wedding text: one widowed woman speaks it to another, her mother-in-law.[^s9] The original is a pledge of loyal kindness within a grieving family. Nor should Orpah be made a villain; she obeys Naomi, and the narrator lets her go in peace.[^s1][^s6]

## Connections

The vow is **part of** the [Book of Ruth](bg:book-of-ruth), whose story it sets in motion.[^s1] It **features** [Ruth](bg:ruth-the-moabite), [Naomi](bg:naomi) and [Orpah](bg:orpah), and ends at [Bethlehem](bg:bethlehem) (1:19-22).[^s1] It **teaches** [*hesed*](bg:hesed), the loyal kindness Naomi prays for in 1:8 and that recurs through the book,[^s3] and [faith](bg:faith) as Ruth takes Israel's God as her own.[^s9] Hutton reads it as a short piece of [Hebrew poetry](bg:hebrew-poetry) set within the prose story.[^s6] Through Naomi's lament it **speaks to** [suffering and lament](bg:suffering-and-lament).[^s3] Ruth's leaving of her homeland recalls [Abraham](bg:abraham) in [Genesis](bg:genesis) 12:1,[^s4][^s11] and the Moabite who clung to Naomi appears in the genealogy of Jesus in the [Gospel of Matthew](bg:gospel-of-matthew): "Boaz became the father of Obed by Ruth" (Matthew 1:5, WEB).[^s5]
