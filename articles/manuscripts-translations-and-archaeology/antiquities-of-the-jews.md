# Antiquities of the Jews

*Antiquities of the Jews* (also called *Jewish Antiquities*) is the great history of the Jewish people written in Greek by Flavius Josephus, a Jewish historian who, after the war with Rome, lived in Rome under the favour of the emperors Vespasian, Titus and Domitian.[^s10] He finished it in the thirteenth year of the emperor Domitian, about AD 93–94, in twenty books that run from the creation of the world to the outbreak of the war with Rome in AD 66.[^s8][^s10] For Bible readers it is the most important non-biblical book for understanding the Jewish world of Jesus and the apostles.[^s12]

## Why it matters

The *Antiquities* lets Christians see the people and places of the New Testament through the eyes of a first-century Jewish writer who did not follow Jesus. It describes the Temple, the priesthood, the Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes, and the Herodian rulers who appear in the Gospels and Acts.[^s12][^s10] It also names John the Baptist, James the brother of Jesus, and, in a passage whose wording is disputed, Jesus himself.[^s8][^s12]

## Background

Josephus wrote for educated Gentile readers, hoping to correct their prejudices and present his often-misunderstood people favourably to the Greco-Roman world.[^s8][^s9] He modelled the work's scale on the twenty-book *Roman Antiquities* of Dionysius of Halicarnassus and dedicated it to a patron named Epaphroditus.[^s8][^s10]

The first eleven books retell the Bible's story from Genesis to the return from exile, mainly following the Greek Septuagint, with additions, omissions and legends.[^s9][^s10] The later books draw on 1 Maccabees, the Letter of Aristeas, the historians Nicolaus of Damascus (an adviser of Herod the Great) and Strabo, Roman decrees favourable to Jews, and, for the final decades, Josephus's own experience and informants.[^s8][^s10] Herod the Great fills books 15–17; the period of Jesus and the apostles is covered more briefly in books 18–20.[^s9][^s10]

## Where it touches the New Testament

**John the Baptist.** In book 18 Josephus calls John "the Baptist", a good man who urged Jews to practise righteousness toward one another and piety toward God and to come to baptism. Herod Antipas, fearing John's influence over the crowds, imprisoned him at the fortress of Machaerus and put him to death; some Jews saw the later defeat of Herod's army as God's judgement for it.[^s5] Mark 6:17-29 tells the same death from another angle: John had rebuked Herod for marrying Herodias, his brother's wife, and Herodias's grudge led to his beheading at a banquet.[^s2] Josephus also tells of Antipas's affair with Herodias and the war it provoked with the Nabatean king Aretas, though he calls her first husband simply "Herod", where Mark 6:17 names him Philip.[^s4][^s2]

**The death of Herod Agrippa I.** Acts 12:20-23 reports that at Caesarea "Herod" addressed the people in royal clothing, the crowd hailed him as a god, and he was struck down "because he didn't give God the glory" (WEB).[^s1] Josephus independently describes Agrippa at games in Caesarea in a shining silver robe, flatterers calling him a god, his failure to rebuke them, sudden pain in his belly and death five days later.[^s7]

**James, the brother of Jesus.** In book 20 Josephus reports that the high priest Ananus, a Sadducee, convened the Sanhedrin during a gap between Roman governors and had stoned "the brother of Jesus, who was called Christ, whose name was James" (Whiston).[^s6] This is the James whom Paul met in Jerusalem, "the Lord's brother" (Galatians 1:19, WEB), and Mark 6:3 lists a James among Jesus's brothers.[^s3][^s2]

**Jesus.** Book 18 also contains a short paragraph about Jesus, known as the *Testimonium Flavianum*. In its received form it calls Jesus a wise man and a worker of wonderful deeds, says "he was the Christ", reports his crucifixion under Pilate and his appearing alive on the third day, and notes that Christians still exist.[^s14][^s11]

## Scholarly views

The *Testimonium* has been, in the Posen Library's words, "rigorously debated among scholars", and four positions are held: that it is entirely authentic; that it is authentic apart from a few easily removed Christian additions; that Josephus wrote briefly about Jesus but a Christian copyist substantially reworked it; or that the whole paragraph is a Christian insertion.[^s11] Older reference works were sceptical of the paragraph as it stands: the *Jewish Encyclopedia* judged that in its present form it could not come from Josephus, the *Catholic Encyclopedia* thought it had suffered "repeated interpolations", and the *Schaff-Herzog* encyclopedia reported that its genuineness was generally given up.[^s8][^s9][^s10] The debate centres on this paragraph in book 18: these same reference works raise no comparable objection to the passages on John the Baptist and on James.[^s8][^s9] If authentic in some form, the paragraph offers early outside evidence for Jesus's ministry and death.[^s11]

## Influence

It was the Christian church that preserved Josephus's works, much as it preserved Philo's.[^s8][^s12] Church fathers such as Eusebius, Ambrose and Jerome quoted him often; Jerome called him "the Greek Livy" and Chrysostom valued him as an expounder of the Old Testament's historical books.[^s8][^s9] Eusebius's *Church History* quotes book 18 of the *Antiquities* on both John the Baptist and Jesus.[^s13] A Latin translation was made in the sixth century under Cassiodorus, and after the invention of printing the work spread into German, French and English, most famously in William Whiston's 1737 English translation.[^s8][^s9] His history also reached Hebrew readers through the medieval Hebrew *Sefer Yosippon*.[^s12] The Greek text itself has textual problems, perhaps from copying errors, later editing or Josephus's uneven use of the Hebrew Bible and the Septuagint, which scholars still work through.[^s12]

## Connections

- The *Antiquities* is **authored by** [Josephus](bg:josephus), the first-century Jewish historian.[^s8]
- It **complements** his earlier [*Jewish War*](bg:jewish-war), which tells the story of the revolt in detail, while the *Antiquities* covers the whole history before it.[^s8]
- Its retelling of the Bible is **influenced by** the [Septuagint](bg:septuagint), the Greek Old Testament Josephus mainly followed.[^s9]
- Its uncertain Greek text is **studied in** [textual criticism](bg:textual-criticism).[^s12]
- It sits within [Second Temple Judaism](bg:second-temple-judaism) and is a key witness to the [Pharisees](bg:pharisees), [Sadducees](bg:sadducees) and [the Temple](bg:the-temple).[^s12] It attests [John the Baptist](bg:john-the-baptist) and [Jesus](bg:jesus), as do Tacitus's [*Annals*](bg:annals-of-tacitus), and Eusebius's [*Church History*](bg:church-history-of-eusebius) passed its testimony on.[^s5][^s13]
