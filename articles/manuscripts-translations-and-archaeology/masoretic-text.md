# Masoretic Text

The **Masoretic Text** (often abbreviated **MT**) is the traditional Hebrew text of the Jewish Bible, which Christians know as the Old Testament. It takes its name from the Hebrew word for "tradition", and the scholars who produced it, the **Masoretes**, were in effect "transmitters of tradition".[^s2][^s1] Working from about the sixth to the tenth centuries AD in Babylonia and the land of Israel, they aimed not to interpret Scripture but to hand it on exactly, adding signs that fixed how it was read aloud.[^s2] The school at Tiberias, on the Sea of Galilee, became the most influential.[^s1][^s4]

Jews have treated this text as authoritative for nearly two thousand years, and almost every printed Hebrew Bible is based on it.[^s8]

## Why it matters

When you open the Old Testament in English, you are almost always reading a translation made from a printed edition of the Masoretic Text.[^s1] Footnotes that mention "the Masoretic Text" or "the Septuagint" are naming different witnesses to the wording of Scripture.[^s1] Knowing what the Masoretes did shows both the faithful labour behind your Bible and why translations occasionally differ.[^s2]

## Description

**Consonants, vowels and accents.** Hebrew was traditionally written with consonants only; readers supplied the vowels from a living tradition of reading aloud.[^s1] The Masoretes devised two sets of small dots and lines placed in and around the consonants: one marks the vowels, the other — the "accents" — marks stress, pauses and links between words, dividing the text into units of sense for public reading.[^s1][^s2] The consonants of Genesis 1:1, for example, can be read more than one way, either as a complete statement about the beginning or as the opening of a longer sentence, and the reading tradition settles which.[^s1][^s15] Of several competing systems, the Tiberian prevailed.[^s4][^s2]

**The Masorah.** Around the text the Masoretes wrote notes, collectively called the Masorah. The *small Masorah* in the side margins gives brief notes, often in abbreviated Aramaic, that count how many times a word or spelling occurs.[^s7] The *large Masorah* in the upper and lower margins lists those occurrences by quoting a snippet from each verse — necessary because the Masoretes worked long before concordances or numbered verses.[^s7] They also counted the verses, words and letters of each section and noted the midpoints, so that any later change could be detected.[^s2]

**Written and read.** Where the Masoretes believed a word should be read differently from how it was written, they did not alter the consonants. They left the written form (the *ketiv*) in the text and noted the form to be read (the *qere*) in the margin.[^s3][^s4] The sixteenth-century scholar Elijah Levita counted 848 such notes.[^s5] In Psalm 100:3 the written word means "not", while the word to be read, pronounced identically, means "his".[^s13] The King James Version follows the written form — "it is he that hath made us, and not we ourselves" — while the World English Bible follows the reading — "It is he who has made us, and we are his."[^s12][^s11] One reading became so constant that it was never marked: the Masoretes placed the vowels of *Adonai* ("Lord") under the four letters of God's name so that readers would say "Adonai" rather than pronounce the name. The later form "Jehovah" arose from misunderstanding this combination.[^s6]

**The great codices.** The Masoretes wrote in codices (bound books) rather than scrolls.[^s1] In the early tenth century Aaron ben Asher and Ben Naphtali of Tiberias led rival schools, and Ben Asher's text became the recognised standard.[^s3] The **Aleppo Codex**, written around 930 with Ben Asher's vowels and accents, was relied on by Maimonides. Damaged in riots in 1947, it survives only in part, most of the Torah lost, and is kept at the Shrine of the Book in the Israel Museum, Jerusalem.[^s9] The **Leningrad Codex**, dated 1008 or 1009, is the oldest complete Hebrew Bible in a single volume; its colophon says it was copied from Ben Asher's exemplars.[^s1][^s7][^s5] It underlies the standard scholarly edition, *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia*.[^s7]

## Significance

Rabbi Akiva's saying in the Mishnah, "Tradition is a fence to the Torah", captures the spirit of the Masoretes' work.[^s10] They preserved not only the letters but the ancient way of reading them: the Catholic Encyclopedia observed that the consonantal text is remarkably uniform, and that ancient translations such as the Septuagint reflect much the same pronunciation, which suggests the reading tradition was fixed long before the vowel signs were written down.[^s4]

The Masoretic Text is not the prophets' and psalmists' original manuscripts. Its complete copies are medieval, and textual critics compare it with other witnesses, such as the Dead Sea Scrolls and the Septuagint.[^s8][^s1] Translators still follow it for most of the Old Testament, noting in footnotes where they choose another witness.[^s1]

Christians owe this Jewish tradition of care a real debt: it handed on the Scriptures Jesus himself read. At Nazareth, "the book of the prophet Isaiah was handed to him" (Luke 4:17, WEB) — a scroll read aloud in the congregation.[^s16][^s1] Their accents serve the aim of the Levites in Nehemiah 8:8, who read the law "distinctly" and "gave the sense" so the people understood (WEB).[^s14][^s1]

**Common misreadings.** The Masoretes did not write or reshape the Old Testament: they recorded a text and reading tradition they had received, and marked rather than corrected what they thought doubtful.[^s2][^s3] Nor are their vowel signs part of the most ancient text; vowel points were unknown in Jerome's day, and they are a medieval record of an older oral reading.[^s4][^s1]

## Connections

The Masoretic Text is **held** as authoritative Scripture in rabbinic Judaism, where it has been the standard text for nearly two millennia.[^s8] It is **studied in** textual criticism, which compares it with other witnesses.[^s8] It was **championed by** Aaron ben Asher, whose Tiberian codex became the recognised standard.[^s3] It is related to the Septuagint and the Dead Sea Scrolls, the other main witnesses to the Old Testament text,[^s1] to the Aleppo and Leningrad Codices, its most famous manuscripts,[^s9][^s7] and to Second Temple Judaism, whose scribes were already trained to copy the Hebrew text by the first century BC.[^s4]
