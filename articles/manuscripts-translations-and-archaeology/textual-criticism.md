# Textual Criticism

**Textual criticism** is the discipline that tries to restore a written work "as nearly as possible" to its original form when the author's own copy has been lost.[^s1] No autograph of any biblical book survives, so critics compare the surviving manuscripts, ancient translations and quotations in early writers to ask which wording is earliest.[^s3][^s4] Its older name, "lower criticism", sets it beside "higher criticism", which studies authorship, sources and date and builds on its results.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

Every English Bible is translated from an edited Hebrew, Aramaic or Greek text, and footnotes such as "the earliest manuscripts do not include" report textual critics' findings to the ordinary reader.[^s14][^s12] Knowing this guards against two opposite mistakes: thinking that every word of a modern Bible came down untouched, and thinking that copying errors leave us unable to know what Scripture said.[^s3][^s6]

## How copies came to differ

Scripture itself shows a text being recopied. When King Jehoiakim cut up and burned the scroll Baruch had written at Jeremiah's dictation, Jeremiah was told to "take again another scroll" and write the former words (Jeremiah 36:28, WEB), and Baruch wrote them out once more.[^s17]

Later copyists were careful but human. They misheard words, confused similar letters and skipped lines that ended alike;[^s2] occasionally they harmonised one Gospel with another or added an explanation.[^s3] The result is tens of thousands of variant readings, most of them trivial.[^s3]

The evidence is rich: about 5,700 Greek manuscripts of the New Testament survive, the earliest papyrus fragments from the second century and the first complete copies, Codex Vaticanus and Codex Sinaiticus, from the fourth.[^s4] For the Old Testament, critics compare the medieval Masoretic Text with the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Samaritan Pentateuch and early translations such as the Septuagint, and the older evidence shows that some books circulated in more than one Hebrew form.[^s2]

## How the work is done

Critics weigh the age and quality of the witnesses to each reading, then ask which reading best explains the others, preferring, all else equal, the harder reading (scribes tended to simplify) and the shorter (scribes tended to add), but never a reading merely because it suits a favoured doctrine.[^s3] The Coherence-Based Genealogical Method, developed by Gerd Mink at Münster, now traces computationally how thousands of manuscripts relate.[^s4]

Three well-known cases show the results:

- **The ending of Mark.** Mark 16:9-20 is found in more than 99 percent of manuscripts, but Sinaiticus and Vaticanus end at 16:8, where the frightened women "said nothing to anyone" (WEB), and ancient writers such as Eusebius and Jerome reported copies that ended there too.[^s8][^s12] Elijah Hixson judges the verses probably not Mark's own, though Irenaeus already quoted them around AD 180; Bibles keep them with a note.[^s8][^s12]
- **The woman caught in adultery.** John 7:53–8:11 is absent from the earliest manuscripts and appears in different places in others; translations print it in brackets or with a note.[^s14][^s13]
- **The "heavenly witnesses".** The words about "the Father, the Word, and the Holy Ghost" in the King James Version of 1 John 5:7 appear in only a handful of late Greek manuscripts. They arose in Latin, entered Erasmus's third Greek edition in 1522 and through it the King James Version; most modern Bibles footnote them.[^s9][^s16]

## Significance

Textual criticism has found the Bible's text remarkably stable. Gregory Lanier found that today's critical editions of the Greek New Testament and the Septuagint, made with new evidence and methods, still agree with earlier standard editions (Westcott and Hort's of 1881, Rahlfs's Septuagint) about 98.5 percent of the time.[^s6] Most differences scarcely change what a reader understands the author to mean.[^s7] Christian teaching does not rest on these disputed passages: the doctrine of the Trinity does not stand or fall with the King James wording of 1 John 5:7, and the resurrection is attested throughout the New Testament whether or not Mark's Gospel ended at 16:8.[^s9][^s8]

Churches have welcomed the work. In 1943 Pope Pius XII called for critical editions so that the sacred text be restored "as perfectly as possible", explaining that the Vulgate's authority is juridical rather than critical.[^s11] Tyndale House, Cambridge, has likewise published a Greek New Testament seeking the oldest recoverable text.[^s7]

## Scholarly views

**What is the goal?** Eldon Jay Epp argued that "original text" can mean several things, for example when an author used a secretary or several copies went out at once.[^s5] The Münster institute now speaks of reconstructing the "initial text" that stands at the beginning of the surviving tradition.[^s4] Some Reformed writers reply that equating this initial text with the inspired text needs a separate theological argument, and that the church's text should not be left provisional.[^s18]

**Which witnesses deserve most weight?** Mainstream editions weight the earliest papyri and majuscules heavily, while proponents of "Byzantine priority" favour the later majority of manuscripts; Lanier estimates that the Byzantine text still agrees with the mainstream critical text about 92–93 percent of the time, and notes that the Münster method has reassessed its value upward.[^s6]

**One text or several editions?** Because some Old Testament books circulated in more than one Hebrew form, there may not always be a single original to recover.[^s2][^s1] Others would correct the Masoretic Text where older evidence is better: at Deuteronomy 32:8 the Masoretic Text reads "sons of Israel", followed by the King James Version, while a Dead Sea Scroll and the Septuagint read "sons of God", followed by the RSV and ESV; one proposal reported by the Biblical Archaeology Society would print the scroll's reading in critical editions.[^s10][^s15]

## Connections

Textual criticism **contrasts with** higher criticism, which builds on its results.[^s1][^s3] It is **emphasised by** the Catholic Church, whose encyclical *Divino Afflante Spiritu* commends it.[^s11] It belongs to biblical criticism,[^s1] and compares the Masoretic Text, the Septuagint and the Dead Sea Scrolls as witnesses to the Old Testament.[^s2][^s10] Codex Sinaiticus is one of its key New Testament witnesses,[^s4] the Vulgate later absorbed the Latin "heavenly witnesses",[^s9] and the Gospel of Mark holds its most discussed ending.[^s8]
