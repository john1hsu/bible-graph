# Septuagint

The **Septuagint** is the ancient Greek translation of Israel's Scriptures and the earliest known translation of the Bible. Its name comes from the Latin for "seventy", after a story about the translators, and it is often abbreviated with the Roman numeral **LXX**.[^s1] The Pentateuch was translated first, in Egypt in the mid-third century BC, probably for the large Jewish community of Alexandria; the other books followed over the next two or three centuries.[^s1][^s2] It also contains books that are not in the Hebrew Bible, some translated from Hebrew and some written in Greek, which Catholic and Orthodox Christians call deuterocanonical.[^s1][^s2]

Strictly speaking there is no single "Septuagint" but a collection of separate translations by different, mostly unknown translators, gathered together later.[^s2] Some 2,000 Greek manuscripts survive, from the second century BC onward, and the great fourth- and fifth-century codices Vaticanus, Sinaiticus and Alexandrinus contain all or most of it.[^s2]

## Why it matters

For Greek-speaking Jews in the centuries before Christ, the Septuagint was their Bible, and it became the Bible of the church.[^s3] When the apostles preached to the wider Mediterranean world, the Scriptures they quoted were usually in this Greek form, and much of the vocabulary of Christian theology passed through it into Latin and then into modern languages.[^s1][^s3] Knowing about it explains why New Testament quotations of the Old Testament sometimes read differently from the Old Testament in your own Bible, and why Christian Bibles group and number the Old Testament books as they do.[^s3][^s10]

## The story of the Seventy

The *Letter of Aristeas* tells how Ptolemy II Philadelphus, advised by his librarian Demetrius, wanted the Jewish Law for the library at Alexandria. The high priest Eleazar sent seventy-two elders, six from each tribe, who completed the translation on the island of Pharos in seventy-two days; the Jewish community heard it read and accepted it.[^s4][^s3] Philo later embellished the story, saying the inspired translators produced identical versions.[^s4]

Scholars widely regard the letter as written by a Jewish author well after the events it describes, probably to commend the translation's authority, though its core — a translation of the Law in Alexandria in the third century BC — may well preserve real history.[^s1][^s3][^s4]

## Translation approach

Because many hands produced it, the Septuagint's style varies from very literal to quite free.[^s1] The Pentateuch is a careful translation, while some later books take more liberty: Greek Esther is much longer than the Hebrew, Greek Job is noticeably shorter, and Greek Jeremiah is significantly shorter and arranged differently.[^s3][^s2] Some differences come from misreadings or copying errors, but some reflect a different Hebrew text: Hebrew manuscripts of Jeremiah among the Dead Sea Scrolls agree with the shorter Greek form.[^s2]

The translators themselves knew the task was hard. The grandson of Jesus ben Sira, who translated his grandfather's book after arriving in Egypt in the thirty-eighth year of King Euergetes, asked readers' patience because what is said in Hebrew does not keep the same force in another language, adding that the same is true of the Law and the Prophets.[^s5]

## Significance

**In the New Testament.** New Testament writers quote the Old Testament often, and most of their quotations agree with the Septuagint.[^s3][^s1] In Psalm 40:6 the Hebrew, followed by the World English Bible, says "You have opened my ears," but the Greek, as Brenton renders it, says "a body hast thou prepared me."[^s6] Hebrews 10:5-7 quotes the Greek form — "you prepared a body for me" (WEB) — and applies it to Christ coming into the world to do God's will.[^s7] At the Jerusalem council James quotes Amos 9:12 to support welcoming Gentiles.[^s9] Where the Hebrew of Amos speaks of possessing "the remnant of Edom", the Greek reads "the remnant of men", and Acts 15:17 follows it: "that the rest of men may seek after the Lord" (WEB).[^s8][^s9] Commentators have suggested the Greek translator read the Hebrew for *Adam* ("man") in place of *Edom*.[^s9]

**For the text of the Old Testament.** As perhaps the oldest witness to the complete Old Testament, the Septuagint is a key tool for textual critics. Most often it confirms the antiquity and reliability of the Masoretic Text, the Hebrew text behind most English Old Testaments, but in places it preserves an older or different reading that modern translators adopt.[^s2]

**In later history.** As Christians relied on the Septuagint, Jewish scholars produced fresh Greek versions — associated with Aquila, Symmachus and Theodotion — that kept closer to the Hebrew text in use among Jews.[^s3] In the Latin West, Jerome's Vulgate eventually displaced the Greek.[^s3]

## Views across traditions

Christians agree that the apostles used the Septuagint; they differ on the authority it holds today.[^s1][^s11]

**Eastern Orthodox.** The Septuagint is described as the official Old Testament of the Orthodox Church, used by the Greek-speaking Fathers and in Orthodox worship and study to this day. Hence the Psalm numbering in Orthodox prayer books differs: their Psalm 50 is Psalm 51 in most English Bibles.[^s10][^s1]

**Catholic.** The Second Vatican Council teaches that the Church accepted the Septuagint "as her own" from the beginning and honours other ancient versions, especially the Latin Vulgate, while also encouraging accurate modern translations made from the original texts.[^s11]

**Protestant.** The Westminster Confession holds that the Old Testament in Hebrew and the New Testament in Greek are the authoritative texts to which the church finally appeals, and that they should be translated into every people's language.[^s12] Protestant Old Testaments are therefore usually translated from the Hebrew, while consulting the Septuagint for textual questions.[^s2]

## Connections

The Septuagint is **explained by** Second Temple Judaism: it arose among Greek-speaking Jews, probably in Alexandria.[^s1] It **contrasts with** the Masoretic Text, the standard Hebrew text, which it usually supports but sometimes differs from.[^s2] It is **studied in** textual criticism, the discipline that compares witnesses to recover the biblical text.[^s2] It is **held** as the authoritative Old Testament by the Eastern Orthodox tradition.[^s10] It is closely related to the deuterocanonical books, the *Letter of Aristeas* and the Vulgate.[^s1][^s3][^s4]
