# Dead Sea Scrolls

The **Dead Sea Scrolls** are a collection of ancient Jewish manuscripts discovered between 1947 and the 1950s in eleven caves near the ruin of Qumran, on the north-western shore of the Dead Sea.[^s3][^s1] They were copied between about the third century BC and AD 68, and they survive mostly as tens of thousands of fragments from almost a thousand different texts, written in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek.[^s1][^s2] Among them are the oldest known copies of the books of the Hebrew Bible, the Christian Old Testament.[^s15]

## Why it matters

The scrolls are about a thousand years older than the earliest complete manuscripts of the Masoretic Text, and they let readers look over the shoulder of Jewish scribes who copied Scripture before and during the lifetime of Jesus.[^s1][^s3] They show both the great care with which the Hebrew text was handed down and the small differences that crept in, which sometimes help translators recover a better reading.[^s4][^s5] They also open a window onto the Jewish world of Jesus and the first Christians.[^s2][^s6]

## Description

**Discovery.** In late 1946 or early 1947 a Bedouin shepherd looking for a lost animal found a cave holding seven largely intact scrolls in jars.[^s3][^s1] Searches over the next decade turned up ten more caves; Cave 4 alone held more than five hundred manuscripts, though looters reached most of it before archaeologists did.[^s3] Excavations at the nearby settlement of Khirbet Qumran found jars like those in the caves, suggesting a link between site and scrolls.[^s3][^s1]

**Contents.** Scholars usually sort the texts into three groups. The **biblical scrolls** — more than two hundred from Qumran — include every book of the Hebrew Bible except Esther.[^s5][^s2] **Apocryphal and pseudepigraphal works**, such as fragments of *1 Enoch*, belong to books accepted in some canons but not others.[^s2] **Sectarian texts** reflect a particular pious community: rules for its life (the *Community Rule*), a vision of a final war between the "sons of light" and the "sons of darkness" (the *War Scroll*), hymns and liturgies, and commentaries called *pesharim* that read books such as Habakkuk and Nahum as speaking about the community's own history.[^s2][^s4]

**The Great Isaiah Scroll.** The best-known biblical scroll is a nearly complete Isaiah from Cave 1: fifty-four columns on seventeen leather sheets, about seven metres (24 feet) long, copied in the second or early first century BC.[^s4][^s7]

**Access today.** After decades of delayed publication, the Israel Antiquities Authority now offers high-resolution images free online in the Leon Levy Dead Sea Scrolls Digital Library.[^s3][^s15]

## Significance

**A carefully kept text.** The Great Isaiah Scroll, copied centuries before the medieval Hebrew manuscripts, agrees with the Masoretic Text so closely that most differences are matters of spelling; a second, more fragmentary Isaiah scroll from Cave 1 also follows it closely.[^s4] Biblical scrolls found at other Judean Desert sites are nearly identical to the medieval Masoretic Text.[^s5] Many readers see here an illustration of Isaiah 40:8: "the word of our God stands forever" (WEB).[^s12]

**More than one form of the text.** The Qumran scrolls also show that, before the first century AD, Scripture circulated in more than one textual form, and some scrolls side with the Septuagint against the later Hebrew.[^s5][^s4] Three examples appear in modern Bibles:

- **Deuteronomy 32:8.** The Masoretic Text says God set the boundaries of the peoples by the number of "the sons of Israel," but a Qumran manuscript (4QDeut<sup>j</sup>) reads "sons of God," agreeing with the Septuagint.[^s8] So the WEB, KJV and NIV follow the Hebrew, while the ESV reads "sons of God" and the NRSV "gods."[^s9]
- **1 Samuel 10:27.** The NRSV adds, from a Qumran manuscript, a passage about Nahash the Ammonite gouging out the right eyes of the Gadites and Reubenites; its footnote notes that the Masoretic Text lacks it.[^s10]
- **Isaiah 53:11.** The Great Isaiah Scroll, two other Qumran copies and the Septuagint say the suffering servant "will see light"; the Masoretic Text lacks the word. The WEB and NIV include it; the KJV and ESV do not.[^s7][^s11]

This is why Bible footnotes sometimes mention the scrolls; one Christian reference work concludes that they pose no threat to the faith and help recover the pre-Christian Old Testament text.[^s4]

**Background to the New Testament.** The scrolls contain no New Testament text; claims that tiny Greek fragments from Cave 7 are New Testament passages have not persuaded most specialists.[^s6] Yet they share a world with the Gospels. The *Community Rule* quotes Isaiah 40:3 to explain why the community withdrew to the wilderness to study God's law; Mark 1:2-3 applies the same verse to John the Baptist preparing the way of the Lord.[^s6][^s12][^s13] The community's charge to love insiders and hate outsiders contrasts sharply with Jesus's command to love enemies in Matthew 5:43-44.[^s6][^s14] Some texts expect both a royal and a priestly messiah, showing how varied Jewish hopes were when Jesus came.[^s6]

## Scholarly views

**Who owned the scrolls?** Most scholars identify the Qumran community with the Essenes, a Jewish movement described by Philo, Pliny the Elder and Josephus.[^s6][^s3] Others suggest the settlement was a pottery workshop or a way station, and some doubt that a single sect produced every text in the caves.[^s3][^s2]

**What do the biblical scrolls show?** Scholars agree that they confirm the antiquity of the text behind the Masoretic Bible.[^s4][^s5] They differ over the variants: Ronald Hendel would build critical editions from the best readings of each tradition (such as "sons of God" in Deuteronomy 32:8), while James Sanders preferred to keep the traditions separate.[^s8][^s3]

**Jesus, John and Qumran.** Claims that John the Baptist or Jesus trained at Qumran are generally judged speculative: the similarities are real, but so are the differences.[^s6][^s4]

## Connections

The scrolls were **discovered at** Qumran, near the Dead Sea.[^s3] They are **studied in** textual criticism, where they are key witnesses for recovering the wording of the Old Testament,[^s5] and in biblical archaeology, which excavated the caves and the settlement.[^s1] They **complement** the Septuagint, sometimes supplying the Hebrew behind its readings, as in Deuteronomy 32:8.[^s8] They are also related to Second Temple Judaism, whose variety they document, the Masoretic Text, Isaiah, the Essenes and the biblical canon.[^s2][^s5]
