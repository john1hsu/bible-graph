# The Exodus

The Exodus is the Bible's great story of rescue: the God of Abraham, Isaac and Jacob hears the cry of Israelite slaves in Egypt, sends Moses, breaks Pharaoh's grip through the plagues and the first Passover, and leads his people out through the sea and on to Mount Sinai.[^s5][^s1] The English name comes from the Greek of the Septuagint, where *exodos* means a going out or departure; it names both the event and the second book of the Bible, whose first eighteen chapters tell the story.[^s16][^s11] Jewish families still retell it every year at Passover.[^s12]

## Why it matters

God introduces the Ten Commandments not with a demand but with a reminder: "I am Yahweh your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage" (Exodus 20:2, WEB).[^s6] Grace comes before law; Israel obeys because it has already been rescued. Old Testament scholar Christine Hayes describes the Exodus as the pattern for how Israel understood God's salvation — concrete, communal deliverance of a people.[^s12] Christians see that pattern fulfilled in Jesus, whose death the New Testament describes in Passover and Exodus language.[^s9][^s10]

## What happened

**Oppression.** A new king "who didn't know Joseph" forces the growing Israelite population into brutal labour, building the store cities of Pithom and Raamses (Exodus 1:8-14).[^s4]

**The call of Moses.** At the burning bush God says he has seen his people's affliction, heard their cry and "come down to deliver them", and he reveals his name, Yahweh, as his memorial for all generations (Exodus 3:7-15).[^s5]

**Plagues and Passover.** Pharaoh refuses to release Israel, and ten plagues fall on Egypt; Hayes notes that the narrative presents them as Yahweh's victory over Egypt's gods.[^s12] Before the last plague, each household kills a lamb, marks the doorposts with its blood and eats in haste, ready to travel; the Lord passes over the marked houses, and the night is to be kept as a feast "forever" (Exodus 12:1-14).[^s1] When children ask what the ritual means, parents tell the story (Exodus 12:26-27).[^s1] The text gives the number who set out as about 600,000 men on foot besides children, and dates the departure 430 years after Israel came to Egypt (Exodus 12:37-42).[^s1]

**The sea.** A strong east wind drives the sea back all night; Israel crosses on dry ground, and the returning waters cover the pursuing army (Exodus 14:21-28). Israel "feared Yahweh" and believed him and his servant Moses (14:31).[^s2] Moses and Israel then sing the Song of the Sea, and Miriam the prophetess leads the women with tambourines (Exodus 15:1-21); the song calls Israel the people God has "redeemed" and is leading to his holy dwelling.[^s3]

## Significance

The story does not stop on the far shore. Hayes stresses that the road from Egypt leads to Sinai, where Israel enters covenant with God and receives the law; the rescue serves a relationship.[^s12] Passover makes each generation a participant: in Jewish observance every generation is to see itself as having come out of Egypt.[^s12]

The New Testament reads the Exodus as the backdrop to Jesus. At the Transfiguration, Moses and Elijah speak with Jesus about "his departure, which he was about to accomplish at Jerusalem" (Luke 9:31, WEB); the Greek word is *exodos*, and some translations render it "exodus".[^s10][^s11] Paul writes that "Christ, our Passover, has been sacrificed in our place" (1 Corinthians 5:7, WEB),[^s9] and says Israel was "baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea", drawing lessons for the church from what happened afterwards in the wilderness (1 Corinthians 10:1-11).[^s8]

## Common misreadings

- **"Just an escape story."** Reading the Exodus only as liberation misses its goal at Sinai: freedom to belong to God in covenant.[^s12]
- **"Red Sea" as a settled map reference.** The Hebrew name is *yam suf*, commonly understood as "Sea of Reeds"; many scholars think it names marshy waters on Egypt's eastern border rather than the sea known today as the Red Sea, while English Bibles such as the WEB keep the traditional "Red Sea" (Exodus 15:4).[^s16][^s12][^s3] The text's point is God's deliverance, whichever body of water is meant.[^s2]

## Scholarly views

Christians have traditionally read the Exodus as history, and many scholars still do; but its date and historical shape are debated.[^s14][^s13]

- **Early date (about 1446 BC).** Taking 1 Kings 6:1 at face value — Solomon began the temple "in the four hundred and eightieth year" after Israel left Egypt — places the Exodus in the fifteenth century BC.[^s7][^s14]
- **Late date (thirteenth century BC).** Many scholars point instead to the store city of Raamses (Exodus 1:11), linked with Pi-Ramesses, the city of Ramesses II, and to the Merneptah Stele (late thirteenth century BC), which already names "Israel" as a people in Canaan; they read the 480 years as twelve symbolic generations of forty years.[^s4][^s14][^s13] Britannica dates the event to the thirteenth century.[^s16]
- **Historical memory, reshaped.** Other historians, such as Baruch Halpern, regard the event as undocumented outside the Bible and judge a departure on the scale of 600,000 men implausible, but argue that the story's details fit real Egyptian conditions and may preserve the memory of a smaller group escaping forced labour, which became the whole nation's story.[^s13]
- **Archaeology of the route.** James Hoffmeier, who directed the North Sinai Archaeological Project, uses Egyptian texts and geological data to illuminate the eastern frontier waters linked with the Exodus and its route.[^s15]

Whatever one concludes on dates, the biblical writers present the Exodus as something God really did in history.[^s12][^s9]

## Connections

- The Exodus **features** Moses, whom God calls at the burning bush,[^s5] and the Israelites, whose oppression opens the book.[^s4]
- It **occurred at** Egypt: "Yahweh brought the children of Israel out" (Exodus 12:51).[^s1]
- It **precedes** the giving of the law at Sinai, the story's true destination.[^s12] The Ten Commandments open by recalling the rescue.[^s6]
- It **illustrates** redemption: the Song of the Sea calls Israel the people God has "redeemed".[^s3]
- It **prefigures** the crucifixion of Jesus, whom Paul calls "our Passover",[^s9] and Christian baptism, which Paul echoes in "baptized into Moses ... in the sea".[^s8]
