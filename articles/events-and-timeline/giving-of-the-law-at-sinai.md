# The Giving of the Law at Sinai

Three months after leaving Egypt, the Israelites camped at the foot of Mount Sinai, and there God met them (Exodus 19:1-2).[^s1] Over the following chapters he came down on the mountain in fire, spoke the Ten Commandments to the whole people, gave further laws through Moses, and sealed a covenant with them in blood (Exodus 19-24).[^s1][^s2][^s3] Deuteronomy retells the same meeting at "Horeb", its usual name for the mountain (Deuteronomy 5:2).[^s6][^s12] For both Jews and Christians this is one of the defining moments of the Bible: the moment a rescued people became God's covenant people.[^s11]

## Why it matters

The order of events is the heart of the story. God does not hand Israel a law and then promise to save them if they keep it; he first reminds them, "I am Yahweh your God, who brought you out of the land of Egypt, out of the house of bondage" (Exodus 20:2, WEB), and only then gives the commandments.[^s2] Yale scholar Christine Hayes argues that the road from Egypt leads not merely across the sea but on to Sinai: the rescue had a purpose, a life with God shaped by his instruction.[^s11] She calls the relationship formed there "an intersection of law and love".[^s11] Sinai guards against two errors: treating God's law as a ladder to earn his favour, and treating grace as if it asked nothing of those it saves.[^s11][^s2]

## What happened

**Arrival and offer.** At Sinai God tells Moses to remind the people how he "bore you on eagles' wings" and brought them to himself. If they obey his voice and keep his covenant, they will be his own possession, "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation" (Exodus 19:4-6, WEB). The people answer together, "All that Yahweh has spoken we will do" (19:8).[^s1]

**Preparation and theophany.** The people consecrate themselves and boundaries are set around the mountain. On the third morning come thunder, lightning, a thick cloud and a loud trumpet; Yahweh descends "in fire", and the mountain smokes and quakes (Exodus 19:10-19). Moses goes up and down as go-between, and Aaron is summoned too (19:20-25).[^s1]

**The Ten Words.** God speaks the commandments (Exodus 20:1-17). Terrified by the thunder and smoke, the people stand far off and beg Moses, "Speak with us yourself, and we will listen; but don't let God speak with us" (20:19, WEB). Moses tells them God has come to test them, so that the fear of him would keep them from sinning (20:20).[^s2] Deuteronomy adds that God spoke these words to the whole assembly, wrote them on two stone tablets, "and he added no more" (Deuteronomy 5:22).[^s6]

**The covenant sealed.** Moses writes down God's words, builds an altar with twelve pillars for the twelve tribes, and reads "the book of the covenant" aloud; the people again pledge obedience. He sprinkles them with blood, saying, "this is the blood of the covenant, which Yahweh has made with you" (Exodus 24:4-8, WEB). Moses, Aaron, Nadab, Abihu and seventy elders then go up, see the God of Israel, "and ate and drank" (24:9-11). Moses enters the cloud and stays on the mountain forty days and nights to receive the stone tablets (24:12-18).[^s3]

**Broken and renewed.** While Moses is away, the people make a golden calf, and Moses smashes the tablets — "the work of God" — at the foot of the mountain (Exodus 32:1-20).[^s4] Yet God renews the covenant: he tells Moses to cut two new tablets, proclaims himself "a merciful and gracious God", and Moses writes "the ten commandments" again; his face shines as he comes down (Exodus 34:1-10, 27-35).[^s5]

## Significance

Hayes, drawing on Jon Levenson, notes that the Sinai covenant resembles ancient Near Eastern treaties between a great king and a vassal: the king names himself, recalls his deeds, sets obligations, has the text read and kept, calls witnesses, and lists blessings and curses. Most of these elements appear, scattered, in Exodus and Deuteronomy; the point is that Israel owes exclusive loyalty to the God who has proved faithful.[^s11]

Sinai also shapes how later generations remember. Moses tells the next generation that God made this covenant "with us", the living, not only with their parents (Deuteronomy 5:3).[^s6] In Judaism, the festival of Shavuot came, by the rabbinic period, to be celebrated as the season of the giving of the Torah, based on the "third month" of Exodus 19:1.[^s13]

For Christians, Sinai is the background to the New Testament's language of covenant. Jeremiah promised a new covenant, unlike the one made when God led Israel out of Egypt, with the law written on the heart (Jeremiah 31:31-33).[^s7] At the Last Supper Jesus calls the cup "my blood of the ... covenant" (Mark 14:24), words that echo Moses at Exodus 24:8; Bible Hub's notes point out that many manuscripts lack the word "new" in Mark.[^s8][^s3] Hebrews contrasts the fearful mountain "that might be touched" with Mount Zion, where believers come to "Jesus, the mediator of a new covenant" (Hebrews 12:18-24, WEB).[^s9] Augustine noted that fifty days separated the Passover from the giving of the law, just as fifty days separated Easter from Pentecost, when the Spirit — "the finger of God" — was given (compare Acts 2:1-4).[^s16][^s10]

## Common misreadings

- **"The law was the way Israel earned God's love."** The narrative puts rescue first and law second (Exodus 19:4; 20:2).[^s1][^s2]
- **"The Ten Commandments were the whole of Sinai."** Moses also relays God's laws to the people (Exodus 24:3), and Hayes identifies the book of the covenant read in Exodus 24 with the laws of Exodus 20-23.[^s3][^s11]
- **"The golden calf ended the covenant."** The text shows God renewing it, revealing his mercy, after Moses' intercession (Exodus 34).[^s5]

## Scholarly views

Jews and Christians have traditionally read the giving of the law as a real event with Moses as mediator, as the texts present it.[^s1][^s6] Scholars debate several questions.

- **Where was Sinai?** The traditional site, Jebel Musa in the southern Sinai Peninsula, was shown to pilgrims by the fourth century; Justinian later built the fortified monastery at its foot (today St Catherine's).[^s14] Britannica notes that biblical place-names cannot be matched to modern sites, so a positive identification cannot be made,[^s15] and a *Biblical Archaeology Review* sidebar finds no archaeological or historical basis for the tradition.[^s14] Other proposals place the mountain in northern or central Sinai, in Arabia (linked to Midian) or at Har Karkom in the Negev; the *Encyclopaedia Judaica* judges that little evidence fixes it.[^s12]
- **How the account was composed.** Source critics note that some strands of the Pentateuch say "Sinai" and others "Horeb", and Gerhard von Rad argued that the Sinai material was once separate from the Exodus story. Some critical scholars doubt the episode's historicity, noting that thunder and fire on a mountain are common in ancient Near Eastern accounts of divine appearances.[^s12]
- **The treaty form.** Scholars find the treaty parallels illuminating whatever their view of the account's date.[^s11]

## Connections

- It **features** Moses and the Israelites at the mountain[^s1] and Aaron with the elders (Exodus 24:9-11),[^s3] and **occurred at** Mount Sinai.[^s1][^s12]
- It **illustrates** the Mosaic covenant[^s11] and **precedes** the golden calf, made while Moses was still on the mountain.[^s4]
- It is **explained by** ancient Near Eastern treaties,[^s11] and Augustine read it as one that **prefigures** Pentecost.[^s16]
- It follows the Exodus and stands behind the Ten Commandments, Deuteronomy and the new covenant.[^s2][^s6][^s7]
