# The Public Ministry of Jesus

The public ministry of Jesus is the part of his life the Gospels spend most of their pages on: from his baptism by John in the Jordan to his death and resurrection in Jerusalem. In a few crowded years he announced the kingdom of God, gathered disciples, taught, healed and went up to Jerusalem to die.[^s1][^s2] The early church saw this span as the core of its witness: Judas's replacement had to be someone who had been with the apostles "beginning from the baptism of John" until Jesus "was received up" (Acts 1:22, WEB).[^s11]

## Why it matters

Christians confess that the eternal Son became flesh, but the Gospels insist he did so at a datable time and place. Luke fixes the start of the story to "the fifteenth year of the reign of Tiberius Caesar," with Pontius Pilate governing Judea and Annas and Caiaphas as high priests (Luke 3:1-2, WEB).[^s2] The ministry "falls into many stages," as the ISBE puts it, and knowing that shape helps readers place each parable, miracle and saying in the story it belongs to.[^s13]

## Timeline

The Gospels share a broad outline:

- **Beginnings (about AD 27 on one common reckoning).** John preaches a baptism of repentance in the region of the Jordan; Jesus is baptized, the Spirit descends on him, and he is driven into the wilderness for forty days (Mark 1:9-13; Luke 3:21-22).[^s1][^s2] Luke adds that he was "about thirty years old" when he began (Luke 3:23, WEB).[^s2] The International Standard Bible Encyclopedia dates the baptism to about AD 27, counting the fifteenth year of Tiberius from his co-rule with Augustus, which it places in AD 12.[^s13] Counted from the death of Augustus instead, the year falls later.[^s12]
- **Ministry in Galilee.** After John's arrest Jesus comes into Galilee announcing that "the time is fulfilled" and the kingdom is near (Mark 1:14-15, WEB).[^s1] He makes Capernaum, a fishing town on the lake, his base (Matthew 4:12-17).[^s4][^s17] Mark shows the rhythm: Sabbath teaching in the Capernaum synagogue, then a tour of "the next towns" (Mark 1:21, 1:38-39).[^s1] Luke opens this phase with Jesus reading Isaiah in the Nazareth synagogue and declaring the Scripture fulfilled (Luke 4:14-21).[^s3] Matthew sums up the whole Galilean work as teaching, preaching the good news of the kingdom and healing (Matthew 4:23).[^s4]
- **Turning point.** Near Caesarea Philippi Jesus asks, "Who do you say that I am?" Peter confesses him as the Christ, and from then on Jesus teaches that the Son of Man must suffer, be killed and rise again (Mark 8:27-31, WEB).[^s5]
- **The road to Jerusalem.** Luke marks the shift with a solemn note: Jesus "intently set his face to go to Jerusalem" (Luke 9:51, WEB).[^s6] The journey ends at Passover in Jerusalem with his arrest, crucifixion and resurrection; the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia provisionally dates the crucifixion to AD 30, while the Catholic Encyclopedia argues for AD 29.[^s13][^s12]

Peter's own summary in Cornelius's house traces the same arc: it began in Galilee after John's baptism, God anointed Jesus "with the Holy Spirit and with power," he went about "doing good and healing," and he was killed in Jerusalem and raised on the third day (Acts 10:37-40, WEB).[^s10]

## Scholarly views

How long did the ministry last? The Gospels never say, and Christians have answered differently since the early centuries.[^s12]

- **About one year.** The final Passover appears in all four Gospels, but only John names earlier ones, so the first three Gospels on their own leave room for a short ministry.[^s13] Several early writers, among them Clement of Alexandria and Julius Africanus, held that it lasted a single year, taking Isaiah's prophetic language about a "year" of the Lord as its measure.[^s12]
- **Two to three years.** John names three Passovers (John 2:13, 6:4, 11:55).[^s7][^s8][^s9] That alone implies a little over two years. If the unnamed "feast of the Jews" in John 5:1 was also a Passover, the total rises to about three years and some months.[^s12][^s13] Eusebius, working from the high priesthoods of Annas and Caiaphas, concluded the whole span was "not quite four years."[^s14]
- **Exact dates.** Dates depend on how Tiberius's reign is counted and how long the ministry lasted; the ISBE notes that a two-year scheme would move its AD 30 a year earlier.[^s12][^s13]

Historians who are cautious about the Gospels' details still treat the frame as secure. Yale's Dale Martin notes that most scholars accept Jesus's baptism by John as historical, since the church would hardly have invented it, and frames the ministry between that baptism and his execution.[^s15] Britannica's Jaroslav Pelikan likewise starts Jesus's public career at the baptism and centres his preaching on the kingdom of God.[^s16]

## Significance

The ministry shows what the kingdom of God looks like when it arrives in person. Jesus's first words in Mark join a claim about time ("the time is fulfilled") to a call to repent and believe.[^s1] His reading in Nazareth frames everything that follows as good news for the poor, release for captives and sight for the blind.[^s3] Matthew ties his move to Capernaum to Isaiah's promise of light for "Galilee of the Gentiles": the ministry continues Israel's Scriptures.[^s4] And because it ends in Jerusalem at Passover, the teaching and healing become the road to the cross and the empty tomb.[^s5][^s6]

A common misreading treats the ministry as a fixed three-year schedule into which every event must be slotted. The evangelists left its length open; their concern was who Jesus is, not a calendar.[^s12][^s13]

## Connections

- The ministry **occurred during** the Second Temple period, under Tiberius, Pilate and the high priests Annas and Caiaphas.[^s2]
- It **precedes** Pentecost and the apostolic age: Acts begins by recalling "all that Jesus began both to do and to teach" until he was taken up, and then tells of the Spirit's coming.[^s11]
- It is **explained by** Second Temple Judaism, the world of synagogue, temple and kingdom hope in which Jesus taught.[^s15][^s1]
- Within it fall the [calling of the first disciples](bg:calling-of-the-first-disciples), the [Sermon on the Mount](bg:sermon-on-the-mount) and the [parables](bg:parables-of-jesus), with much of its early work centred on [Galilee](bg:galilee), and it ends with the [crucifixion](bg:crucifixion-of-jesus) in [Jerusalem](bg:jerusalem).[^s1][^s4][^s6]
