# Babylonian Exile

The Babylonian Exile is the moment the Old Testament story seems to fall apart. Nebuchadnezzar II of Babylon took Jerusalem, carried King Jehoiachin and Judah's leaders away, and a decade later burned the city and Solomon's Temple; Judah "was carried away captive out of his land" (2 Kings 25:21, WEB).[^s1][^s2] The exile is usually dated from the deportations of about 597 and 587/586 BC to 538 BC, when Cyrus of Persia, having conquered Babylon, let the exiles go home.[^s17][^s16] Yet out of this catastrophe came some of Scripture's deepest words of hope.[^s15]

## Why it matters

The land, the king from David's line and the Temple seemed to be the visible signs of God's promises, and the exile took all three away.[^s15] Christine Hayes describes the theological crisis this posed: had God failed, or abandoned his people?[^s15] The prophets answered that God was judging covenant unfaithfulness, as Deuteronomy had warned that the Lord would "scatter you among all peoples" (Deuteronomy 28:64, WEB), but that judgment would not be his last word.[^s14][^s16] Much of the Old Testament reads differently once you know it was finished, collected or treasured by people living through or after this loss.[^s18][^s15]

## What happened

**The first deportation (597 BC).** Nebuchadnezzar besieged Jerusalem; the young King Jehoiachin surrendered and was taken to Babylon with his mother, officials, soldiers, craftsmen and the Temple treasures, and his uncle Mattaniah was made king as Zedekiah (2 Kings 24:10-17).[^s1] A Babylonian tablet, the Babylonian Chronicle (British Museum, BM 21946), records that the king seized "the city of Judah" on the second day of Adar and appointed a king of his choice.[^s19] Daniel 1:1-7 sets Daniel and his companions among young nobles taken to Babylon in an earlier campaign and given Babylonian names.[^s11]

**The fall of Jerusalem (587/586 BC).** When Zedekiah rebelled, the city was besieged until famine set in; the king fled, was captured, saw his sons killed and was blinded; Nebuzaradan burned "Yahweh's house" and broke down the walls, and more people were carried away, though the poorest were left to tend vineyards and fields (2 Kings 25:1-12).[^s2]

**Life by the rivers of Babylon.** Psalm 137 gives the grief its voice: by the rivers of Babylon "we wept, when we remembered Zion" (Psalm 137:1, WEB).[^s5] Jeremiah, however, wrote to the exiles to build houses, plant gardens, marry, and "seek the peace of the city" where God had sent them, promising return "after seventy years" (Jeremiah 29:4-14, WEB; compare 25:11-12).[^s3][^s12] Ezekiel, a priest among the captives "by the river Chebar", saw "visions of God" there in the fifth year of Jehoiachin's captivity (Ezekiel 1:1-3, WEB).[^s7] Second Kings closes with Jehoiachin released from prison and fed at the Babylonian king's table (2 Kings 25:27-30).[^s2]

**The end of exile (538 BC).** Chronicles and Ezra report that the Lord "stirred up the spirit of Cyrus", who proclaimed that God's people might go up and rebuild the Temple, and returned the Temple vessels to Sheshbazzar (2 Chronicles 36:20-23; Ezra 1:1-11, WEB).[^s6][^s10]

## Significance

Hayes notes that the exile taught Israel that God was not confined to one land: in Ezekiel the divine glory goes east with the exiles and returns with them.[^s15] Isaiah 40 opens a new chapter with "Comfort, comfort my people" (Isaiah 40:1, WEB), announcing that Jerusalem's warfare is over and her sin pardoned; Hayes reads these chapters as promising a new exodus home.[^s8][^s15] Gary Yates shows how Jeremiah held out both a "new exodus" promise and a warning to the exiles in Babylon.[^s21] Without the Temple, worship centred more on prayer, Sabbath and Scripture, and Hayes sees here the point where Israel's national history begins to become Judaism as a religion.[^s15][^s18] Many exiles stayed, founding a Babylonian Jewish community that lasted for centuries.[^s17]

The New Testament keeps the exile in view. Matthew structures Jesus's genealogy around it: fourteen generations "from David to the exile to Babylon" and fourteen more "to the Christ" (Matthew 1:17, WEB).[^s9] Peter writes to Christians "living as foreigners in the Dispersion" and urges them to live "as foreigners here" (1 Peter 1:1, 1:17, WEB).[^s13]

## Common misreadings

- **"Everyone was taken."** Second Kings says the poorest were left behind, and archaeology suggests Judah was thinly populated but not empty.[^s2][^s16]
- **Jeremiah 29:11 as a private promise.** The "hope and a future" was written to a community of exiles told to settle in for seventy years, not to promise an individual quick success.[^s3]

## Scholarly views

- **Dates.** The Babylonian Chronicle fixes the first capture of Jerusalem in 597 BC.[^s19] Scholars divide over whether the city fell in 587 or 586 BC, depending on how the regnal years are reckoned; Britannica gives 587/6, Bible Odyssey 587, the Jewish Encyclopedia 586.[^s17][^s16][^s18]
- **Numbers.** Second Kings speaks of about 10,000 captives in 597 (24:14) and also of 7,000 fighting men plus 1,000 craftsmen (24:16), while Jeremiah 52:28-30 lists three deportations totalling 4,600 people.[^s1][^s4] The Jewish Encyclopedia suggested the smaller lists count only men.[^s18]
- **How harsh was exile?** Traditional readings take Psalm 137 as the exiles' common experience.[^s5] Cuneiform archives from Al-Yahudu ("Judah-town"), dated 572–477 BC, show Judeans farming, paying taxes, renting and marrying over several generations while keeping a distinct identity, and Bible Odyssey argues life was less bleak than the psalm implies.[^s20][^s16] Both can be true: settled life and deep grief for Zion are not opposites, as Jeremiah's letter assumes.[^s3]

## Connections

- The exile **features** Jehoiachin and Nebuchadnezzar II (2 Kings 24:10-12),[^s1] Ezekiel among the captives,[^s7] and Daniel in the Babylonian court.[^s11]
- It **occurred at** Babylon, by whose rivers the exiles wept.[^s5]
- It **precedes** the return from exile under Cyrus.[^s6]
- It **illustrates** the biblical theme of exile and return, which Hayes traces through Ezekiel and Second Isaiah.[^s15]
- It is **corroborated by** the Babylonian Chronicle[^s19] and the Al-Yahudu tablets.[^s20]
