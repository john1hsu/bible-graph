# Pentecost

Fifty days after Passover, Israel kept the Feast of Weeks, a harvest festival that Greek-speaking Jews called *pentēkostē*, "fiftieth".[^s13] Acts 2 tells how, on that festival, some weeks after Jesus's resurrection and ascension, the Holy Spirit came on his followers in Jerusalem and the church's mission began.[^s1][^s2] Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant Christians all celebrate Pentecost on the fiftieth day of Easter.[^s15][^s16][^s18]

## Why it matters

Pentecost is the hinge between the Gospels and the life of the church. The risen Jesus told his disciples to wait in Jerusalem "until you are clothed with power from on high" (Luke 24:49, WEB), promising that when the Spirit came they would "receive power" and be his witnesses to the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8, WEB).[^s3][^s2] For the Catholic Catechism, on that day "the Holy Trinity is fully revealed";[^s16] the Orthodox call it "the birthday of the Church".[^s18]

## Background: the Feast of Weeks

The Law told Israel to count seven weeks from the first sheaf, then bring new grain and two leavened loaves as firstfruits on the fiftieth day, a holy day free of ordinary work (Leviticus 23:15-21).[^s5] Deuteronomy adds a freewill offering and a feast of joy shared with servants, the Levite, the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow, remembering slavery in Egypt; it was one of three yearly pilgrim feasts (Deuteronomy 16:9-16).[^s6] The *Jewish Encyclopedia* notes Josephus's report of large festival crowds and reads Acts 2:5 as evidence of the same gathering. Rabbinic tradition later linked Shavuot with the giving of the Torah at Sinai, a link Philo and Josephus do not mention.[^s13] Augustine saw the fifty days from Easter to Pentecost as mirroring Sinai; Calvin thought this too subtle and suggested God chose a day when Jerusalem was full of visitors who would carry the news home.[^s22]

## What happened

**Waiting.** After forty days of appearances, Jesus was taken up, and the eleven prayed in an upper room with the women, Mary the mother of Jesus and his brothers, about 120 believers in all (Acts 1:3-15).[^s2]

**The Spirit comes.** "Now when the day of Pentecost had come, they were all with one accord in one place" (Acts 2:1, WEB). A sound "like the rushing of a mighty wind" filled the house, tongues like fire rested on each, and all were filled with the Holy Spirit and began to speak in other languages as the Spirit enabled them (2:2-4).[^s1]

**The crowd.** Devout Jews from many nations, living in Jerusalem, each heard the believers in his own language declaring "the mighty works of God" (2:11, WEB); others scoffed that they were drunk (2:5-13).[^s1]

**Peter's sermon.** Standing with the eleven, Peter said: "this is what has been spoken through the prophet Joel" (2:16, WEB). God would pour out his Spirit on all flesh, and whoever calls on the Lord's name will be saved (Acts 2:17-21; Joel 2:28-32).[^s1][^s4] Jesus, crucified but raised by God and exalted to his right hand, "has poured out this, which you now see and hear"; God has made him "both Lord and Christ" (2:22-36, WEB).[^s1]

**The response.** Peter called his hearers to repent and be baptized in Jesus's name for the forgiveness of sins, promising the gift of the Spirit "to you, and to your children, and to all who are far off" (2:37-39, WEB). About three thousand were baptized, and the new community devoted itself to the apostles' teaching, fellowship, the breaking of bread and prayer, sharing goods with any in need (2:41-47).[^s1]

## Significance

**Promise fulfilled.** Joel's promise reaches sons and daughters, old and young, and servants (Joel 2:28-29).[^s4] Britannica notes that Christians saw the Spirit as the firstfruits of a new covenant, echoing the firstfruits loaves of the harvest feast.[^s15][^s5]

**Babel reversed.** At Babel the Lord "confused the language of all the earth" and scattered its people (Genesis 11:1-9, WEB).[^s7] Calvin saw Pentecost turning that punishment of pride into a blessing that gathered the scattered into unity, and many modern interpreters agree.[^s22][^s25] Craig Keener notes that Luke's list of nations may echo Genesis 10, while granting that the text never makes the link explicit.[^s26]

**Pentecost spreads.** Acts tells of similar outpourings among Samaritans (Acts 8:14-17), on Gentiles at Cornelius's house, who spoke in other languages and praised God (10:44-48), and at Ephesus (19:1-7).[^s8][^s9][^s10] Paul still hurried to be in Jerusalem for the feast (Acts 20:16).[^s12] Tertullian already treats the Christian feast as well established; the English name Whitsunday comes from the white garments of those baptized at it.[^s14][^s15]

## Common misreadings

- **"Pentecost is a Christian invention."** It was a feast of Israel long before, and the Spirit came while the city was keeping it (Leviticus 23; Acts 2:1, 5).[^s5][^s1]
- **"Only the twelve received the Spirit."** Acts 2:1 says "they were all" together; the nearest group named is the 120, including women (Acts 1:14-15), and Joel's promise includes "your daughters".[^s1][^s2][^s4]
- **"Peter blamed the Jewish people as a whole."** Peter, a Jew, spoke to fellow Jews, called them to repent, and offered the promise to them and their children; the young community worshipped in the temple with the people's favour (Acts 2:39, 46-47).[^s1]

## Views across traditions

All traditions confess that the risen Christ poured out the Spirit at Pentecost and sent the church to the nations;[^s15] they differ on how believers receive that gift today.

- **Catholic.** Christ's Passover is fulfilled in the outpouring of the Spirit (Catechism 731).[^s16] The apostles later gave the Spirit to the newly baptized by laying on hands (compare Acts 8:15-17; 19:5-6), and Catholic tradition sees here the origin of Confirmation, which "perpetuates the grace of Pentecost" (1288); Eastern Churches confer this anointing, Chrismation, with baptism (1290-1292).[^s17][^s8][^s10]
- **Eastern Orthodox.** The Orthodox Church in America presents Pentecost as the fulfilment of the Old Testament feast of the law, the full revelation of the Trinity (it is also called Trinity Day), and, in its hymns, Babel reversed.[^s18]
- **Lutheran and Reformed.** Luther's Small Catechism confesses that the Spirit "calls, gathers, enlightens, and sanctifies the whole Christian church on earth".[^s21] Calvin read the sign as given for the whole church in every age.[^s22] Many Protestants hold that every believer receives the Spirit at conversion (1 Corinthians 12:13); James Dunn's influential study made receiving the Spirit the chief element of "conversion-initiation".[^s11][^s24]
- **Pentecostal.** The Assemblies of God teaches a baptism in the Spirit that empowers believers for witness, distinct from and subsequent to the new birth, with speaking in tongues (Acts 2:4) as its initial physical evidence.[^s19][^s20] Pentecostal scholar William Atkinson argues that in Luke-Acts the Spirit chiefly empowers prophecy and mission.[^s23][^s24]

Ben Witherington argues that the Greek of Acts 2 does not support reading these tongues as angelic speech,[^s27] while Calvin left open whether the hearers also received a miracle of hearing.[^s22]

## Connections

- It **features** Peter and the apostles[^s1] and **occurred at** Jerusalem, where Jesus told them to wait.[^s1][^s3]
- Peter presents it as **fulfilling** the book of Joel.[^s1][^s4]
- It is **explained by** Shavuot, the Feast of Weeks.[^s13][^s5]
- It **illustrates** the Holy Spirit poured out by Christ[^s16] and the new covenant.[^s15]
- It **contrasts with** the tower of Babel.[^s22][^s25]
- It follows the crucifixion and resurrection Peter preached; Augustine linked it to the giving of the law at Sinai.[^s1][^s22]
