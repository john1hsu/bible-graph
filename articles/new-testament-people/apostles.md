# The Apostles of Jesus

An apostle is, quite literally, "one who is sent." The Greek *apostolos* comes from the verb *apostellō*, "to send out," and means a delegate or envoy rather than a mere messenger.[^s13][^s14] In the New Testament the word belongs first of all to the Twelve, the inner circle Jesus chose from among his wider band of disciples, but it is also used of other commissioned witnesses, most famously Paul.[^s15][^s14] The church remembered the apostles as the people through whom the good news of Jesus first went out to the world, and it saw itself as built on their witness.[^s9][^s15]

## Why it matters

Knowing who the apostles were helps a reader follow the whole New Testament: the Gospels show Jesus forming them, Acts shows them bearing witness, and the letters carry their teaching.[^s15] Mark's account of their calling also gives a simple pattern for every disciple: Jesus appointed them first "that they might be with him," and only then to be sent out to preach (Mark 3:14, WEB).[^s1] Paul's description of himself as the least of the apostles, unworthy of the name because he had persecuted the church (1 Corinthians 15:9), is a reminder that the apostolic calling rested on grace, not merit.[^s6]

## Who they were

The Synoptic Gospels each record Jesus choosing the Twelve (Mark 3:13-19; Matthew 10:1-4; Luke 6:13-16), and Acts 1:13 repeats the list after the Ascension.[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s4] Simon, whom Jesus named Peter, always comes first; next come Andrew and the brothers James and John, the sons of Zebedee, whom Jesus nicknamed Boanerges, Sons of Thunder.[^s1][^s2] Philip, Bartholomew, Matthew the tax collector, Thomas, James the son of Alphaeus and Simon the Zealot follow, and Judas Iscariot, the betrayer, closes the Gospel lists.[^s1][^s2] One name varies: Mark has Thaddaeus, Matthew has "Lebbaeus, who was also called Thaddaeus" (Matthew 10:3, WEB), while Luke and Acts have Judas the son of James.[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s4] *Britannica* lists him as one man, Jude or Thaddaeus, the son of James.[^s14]

The number itself carries meaning. Jesus promised the Twelve that "in the regeneration" they would sit on twelve thrones, "judging the twelve tribes of Israel" (Matthew 19:28, WEB), and some scholars see the number as a deliberate reference to Israel's twelve tribes.[^s5][^s14][^s15] Jesus gave them authority over unclean spirits and over sickness and sent them out to preach (Matthew 10:1; Mark 3:14-15).[^s1][^s2]

The title was not limited to the Twelve. Acts calls Barnabas and Paul "the apostles" (Acts 14:14), Paul greets Andronicus and Junia as "notable among the apostles" (Romans 16:7, WEB), and Hebrews even calls Jesus himself "the Apostle and High Priest of our confession" (Hebrews 3:1, WEB), the one sent from God.[^s10][^s7][^s11][^s13] Older reference works note that the word could also be used of envoys sent by a congregation.[^s12][^s15]

## In the biblical story

After Judas's death, Peter told the believers that his place must be filled by one of the men who had been with them from John's baptism until the Ascension, so that he could "become a witness with us of his resurrection" (Acts 1:21-22, ASV).[^s4] They put forward Joseph Barsabbas and Matthias, prayed that the Lord would show his choice, cast lots, and Matthias was counted with the eleven (Acts 1:23-26).[^s4] Jesus had already told them they would be his witnesses in Jerusalem, Judea, Samaria and to the ends of the earth (Acts 1:8), and the rest of Acts follows that outward movement.[^s4][^s15]

Paul's summary of the early creed shows how central the apostles' witness was: the risen Christ "appeared to Cephas, then to the twelve" (1 Corinthians 15:5, WEB), later to all the apostles, and last of all to Paul himself (15:7-8).[^s6] Ephesians pictures the church as a household "built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets," with Christ Jesus as the chief cornerstone (Ephesians 2:20, WEB), and Revelation sees the twelve names of "the twelve Apostles of the Lamb" on the foundations of the new Jerusalem (Revelation 21:14, WEB).[^s9][^s8]

A common misreading is to treat "disciple" and "apostle" as the same thing. Luke says Jesus called his disciples and "from them he chose twelve, whom he also named apostles" (Luke 6:13, WEB): every apostle was a disciple, but not every disciple was an apostle.[^s3][^s15] Another is to assume that the New Testament itself tells us where each apostle went and how each died; Acts records very little about their later lives, and by the second century even the days of their deaths were unknown; most such stories come from later tradition.[^s13][^s15]

## Scholarly views

Scholars and traditions weigh several questions differently. On **what made someone an apostle**, the *Encyclopedia of the Bible* and the *Catholic Encyclopedia* stress having been with Jesus and having seen the risen Lord, following Acts 1:21-22, while the ISBE article argues that the deeper mark was a missionary calling, not personal acquaintance with Jesus.[^s15][^s13][^s12] On **who counts**, writers differ over whether James the brother of the Lord was called an apostle, and over Romans 16:7, where older works read the second name as a man's name, Junias, while the *Encyclopedia of the Bible* allows that it may be Junia, a woman, as the World English Bible renders it.[^s15][^s12][^s13][^s7] On **history**, the *Catholic Encyclopedia* defended the Synoptic accounts against critics who doubted that Jesus himself appointed the Twelve, and reference works note some variation and uncertainty in the names on the lists.[^s13][^s14][^s15]

Churches also differ on **how apostolic authority continues**. The *Catholic Encyclopedia* presents bishops as the apostles' successors, citing Clement of Rome and Ignatius, while holding that some apostolic prerogatives were personal.[^s13] The ISBE and the *Encyclopedia of the Bible* emphasise that the apostles' position was unique and that their authority, or many of their functions, could not be handed on, so that their preaching remains the norm for the church's teaching.[^s12][^s15] Readers can weigh these views in light of the texts above and their own church's teaching.

## Connections

- **Jesus** — the apostles exist because Jesus called and sent them, and Hebrews names him the first Apostle, sent by the Father.[^s1][^s11]
- **The Israelites** — the number twelve recalls the twelve tribes of Israel, whom the apostles are promised to judge (Matthew 19:28).[^s5][^s14]
- **Shaliach** — the *Encyclopedia of the Bible* links *apostolos* to the Hebrew *shaliach*, an authorised agent, and the later Jewish saying that a man's agent is as himself.[^s15]
- **Second Temple Judaism** — later Judaism sent accredited envoys, often in pairs, from Jerusalem to Jewish communities abroad, a practice reference works compare with the apostles' sending.[^s15][^s12]
- **Peter, Paul and Barnabas** — Peter heads every list; Paul and Barnabas are called apostles outside the Twelve.[^s2][^s6][^s10]
- **Acts of the Apostles** — Acts opens by restoring the Twelve and sending them as witnesses to the ends of the earth.[^s4]
