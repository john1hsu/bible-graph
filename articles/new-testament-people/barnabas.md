# Barnabas

Barnabas is the early church's great encourager. A Levite from Cyprus named Joseph (or Joses), he was given a nickname by the apostles that Luke explains as "Son of Encouragement" (Acts 4:36, WEB), and he lived up to it.[^s1] He stood up for Saul when no one else trusted him, welcomed the first largely Gentile church at Antioch, and set out with Paul on the first missionary journey.[^s2][^s3][^s4] Acts calls him "a good man, and full of the Holy Spirit and of faith" (Acts 11:24, WEB).[^s3]

## Why it matters

Barnabas is a hinge figure in Acts: he links the Jerusalem church with the mission to the Gentiles and brought Paul into that work.[^s10][^s12] His life also shows a kind of discipleship that is easy to overlook — generosity with money, readiness to trust people others write off, and joy at seeing God at work among newcomers.[^s1][^s2][^s3]

## Life and story

**A generous Levite.** Barnabas first appears among the Jerusalem believers who shared what they had: he sold a field and "laid it at the apostles' feet" (Acts 4:37, WEB).[^s1] As a Levite he belonged to the tribe set apart for temple service, which may explain why a man born on Cyprus was living in Jerusalem.[^s11]

**Sponsor of Saul.** When the newly converted Saul tried to join the disciples in Jerusalem, "they were all afraid of him"; Barnabas brought him to the apostles and told them how Saul had seen the Lord and preached boldly at Damascus (Acts 9:26-27, WEB).[^s2]

**At Antioch.** Believers scattered after Stephen's death — some of them men of Cyprus and Cyrene — began preaching the Lord Jesus to Greeks in Antioch, and many believed (Acts 11:19-21).[^s3] Jerusalem sent Barnabas, who "had seen the grace of God" and was glad, urging them all to remain near to the Lord (Acts 11:23, WEB).[^s3] He then went to Tarsus to find Saul and brought him to Antioch, where they taught for a whole year and the disciples were first called Christians (Acts 11:25-26).[^s3] The two carried Antioch's famine relief to the elders in Judea (Acts 11:29-30).[^s3]

**The first journey.** Barnabas heads the list of Antioch's prophets and teachers, and the Holy Spirit said, "Separate Barnabas and Saul for me" (Acts 13:1-2, WEB).[^s4] They sailed first to Cyprus, Barnabas's homeland, where the proconsul summoned "Barnabas and Saul" (Acts 13:4, 13:7, WEB).[^s4] From then on Luke usually writes "Paul and Barnabas" (Acts 13:46), a quiet sign that Paul was becoming the leading voice.[^s4][^s10] At Lystra the crowd took Barnabas for Zeus ("Jupiter" in the WEB) and Paul for Hermes, "because he was the chief speaker"; Luke here calls them both "the apostles" (Acts 14:12-14, WEB).[^s5]

**Jerusalem and the parting.** Barnabas went with Paul to Jerusalem over whether Gentile believers must be circumcised, and the assembly listened as "Barnabas and Paul" reported God's signs among the nations (Acts 15:2, 15:12, WEB).[^s6][^s10] The apostles' letter calls them "our beloved Barnabas and Paul," men who had risked their lives for Christ (Acts 15:25-26, WEB).[^s6] Paul, too, records going up to Jerusalem with Barnabas and receiving with him "the right hand of fellowship" for the Gentile mission (Galatians 2:1, 2:9, WEB).[^s7] Later, Barnabas wanted to bring his cousin John Mark on a second journey; Paul refused because Mark had left them in Pamphylia, and the disagreement grew so sharp that they separated — Barnabas sailing with Mark to Cyprus (Acts 15:36-40).[^s6][^s8]

## Legacy in Scripture

Barnabas drops out of Acts after the parting, but not out of Paul's letters.[^s12] Paul names him as one who, like himself, worked for a living rather than claim support (1 Corinthians 9:6).[^s9] Paul also tells honestly of a low moment at Antioch, when Jewish believers withdrew from eating with Gentiles and "even Barnabas was carried away" (Galatians 2:13, WEB).[^s7] And Mark, the young man Barnabas refused to give up on, later appears with Paul as "the cousin of Barnabas," commended to the church (Colossians 4:10).[^s8] Barnabas's feast is kept on 11 June in both Eastern and Western calendars.[^s11][^s14]

## Scholarly views

Scholars discuss the meaning of his name: the Aramaic is often thought to mean "son of prophecy," and Luke's "Son of Encouragement" may be a description of his character as much as a strict etymology.[^s10][^s11] Several later traditions are also weighed differently. Eastern Orthodox tradition honours him as an apostle of the Seventy, who was stoned in Cyprus and whose relics were found there in the late fifth century, a find that strengthened the Church of Cyprus's claim to apostolic foundation and independence.[^s14] Clement of Alexandria and Eusebius also count him among the Seventy, though the *Catholic Encyclopedia* thinks Acts points the other way; it and *Britannica* treat the stories of his martyrdom, and of missions to Milan, Alexandria or Rome, as late and unreliable, noting that the *Journeys and Martyrdom of Barnabas* is a fifth-century work.[^s11][^s12] Tertullian cited the Letter to the Hebrews as an epistle "under the name of Barnabas," and some early writers credited him with the *Epistle of Barnabas*; most scholars today think neither was written by him.[^s13][^s11][^s12]

## Common misreadings

Some readers treat the split in Acts 15 as proof that Barnabas was simply wrong and Paul right. Luke does not take sides; he reports a sharp disagreement between two faithful men, and the later letters show both Barnabas and Mark still held in honour (1 Corinthians 9:6; Colossians 4:10).[^s6][^s9][^s8] Others assume "apostle" applies only to the Twelve, but Acts 14:14 uses the word of Barnabas and Paul.[^s5]

## Connections

Barnabas **was a member of** the apostles in the wider sense Luke uses in Acts 14:14.[^s5] He **was a member of** the Levites, according to Acts 4:36.[^s1] His Levite background and life in Jerusalem are **explained by** the temple-centred Judaism of the Second Temple period.[^s11] His joy at what he saw in Antioch **illustrates** grace: he "had seen the grace of God" and was glad (Acts 11:23).[^s3] He is also tied to Paul, Acts, Peter (Galatians 2:11-13), his cousin John Mark, the Jerusalem council and the *Epistle of Barnabas* later attached to his name.[^s4][^s7][^s8][^s6][^s11]
