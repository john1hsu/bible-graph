# John the Baptist

John the Baptist stands at the hinge of the two Testaments. All four Gospels begin Jesus' public story with him: a prophet in camel's hair at the Jordan, calling Israel to repent and be baptized because someone greater was coming.[^s3][^s5][^s7] Jesus said that no one born of a woman had arisen greater than John, and called him "much more than a prophet" (Matthew 11:9-11, WEB).[^s6] Yet John's whole message pointed away from himself: "He must increase, but I must decrease" (John 3:30, WEB).[^s8]

## Why it matters

The Gospels show that Jesus did not appear out of nowhere: John is the messenger Malachi promised and the "voice" of Isaiah 40, so his arrival signals that God's promised visit to his people has begun.[^s3][^s6][^s10][^s11] His humility remains a picture of Christian witness.[^s8][^s17]

## Life and story

**Birth.** Luke tells how the angel appeared to the elderly priest Zacharias (Zechariah) in the temple and promised that his wife Elizabeth, who had been unable to have children, would bear a son named John. He would be filled with the Holy Spirit even before his birth and would go before the Lord "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:13-17, WEB).[^s1] When Elizabeth's "relative" Mary visited, the unborn child leaped for joy (Luke 1:36, 1:41-44).[^s1] At the naming, Zechariah wrote on a tablet that the child's name was John, and then sang that the child would be called "a prophet of the Most High" (Luke 1:63, 1:76).[^s1] The boy grew strong in spirit and lived in the wilderness until he appeared publicly to Israel (Luke 1:80).[^s1]

**Preaching and baptism.** Luke dates John's call carefully, "in the fifteenth year" of Emperor Tiberius, when Pontius Pilate governed Judea (Luke 3:1-2).[^s2] John dressed in camel's hair with a leather belt and ate locusts and wild honey (Mark 1:6).[^s3] He preached "the baptism of repentance" for the forgiveness of sins, and people from Judea and Jerusalem came to be baptized in the Jordan, confessing their sins (Mark 1:4-5).[^s3] He warned that descent from Abraham was no shelter, and told people to "produce fruits worthy of repentance" (Luke 3:8, WEB).[^s2] Asked what to do, he answered concretely: share coats and food, collect only the tax due, and do not extort (Luke 3:10-14).[^s2] The Jewish historian Josephus likewise remembered him as a good man who urged righteousness and piety.[^s13]

**Pointing to Jesus.** John insisted he was not the Messiah, nor Elijah, nor "the prophet"; he was only "the voice of one crying in the wilderness" (John 1:19-23, WEB).[^s7] One mightier than he was coming, whose sandals he was unworthy to untie, and who would baptize "in the Holy Spirit" (Mark 1:7-8).[^s3] When Jesus came to be baptized, John protested that he needed Jesus' baptism instead, but Jesus said it was fitting "to fulfill all righteousness" (Matthew 3:13-15).[^s5] In John's Gospel he points to Jesus as "the Lamb of God, who takes away the sin of the world," and two of his own disciples, one of them Andrew, then follow Jesus (John 1:29, 1:35-40, WEB).[^s7]

**Prison and death.** John rebuked Herod Antipas for marrying Herodias, his brother's wife, and Herod imprisoned him (Luke 3:19-20).[^s2][^s14] From prison John sent disciples to ask, "Are you he who comes, or should we look for another?" Jesus answered by pointing to the blind seeing and the poor hearing good news (Matthew 11:2-6, WEB).[^s6] At Herod's birthday banquet, Herodias's daughter, prompted by her mother, asked for John's head; Herod, though "exceedingly sorry," had him beheaded, and his disciples buried him (Mark 6:17-29, WEB).[^s4]

## Legacy in Scripture

Jesus identified John with the promised Elijah: "if you are willing to receive it, this is Elijah, who is to come" (Matthew 11:14, WEB), echoing Malachi's promise to send "Elijah the prophet" before the day of the Lord (Malachi 4:5, WEB).[^s6][^s12] John's own denial that he was Elijah (John 1:21) is usually read as refusing to be Elijah literally returned, while fulfilling Elijah's role, as Luke 1:17 puts it.[^s7][^s15][^s14] The Gospels apply Malachi 3:1 ("I send my messenger") and Isaiah 40:3 ("Prepare the way") to him (Mark 1:2-3; Matthew 3:3; Matthew 11:10).[^s3][^s5][^s6][^s11][^s10] Years later Paul met disciples in Ephesus who knew only "John's baptism," and taught them that John had told people to believe in the one coming after him, that is, Jesus (Acts 19:1-7, WEB).[^s9]

The Catholic Catechism calls him "more than a prophet," the one in whom the Spirit completes the work of the prophets and who bears witness to the Light.[^s18] The Orthodox Church celebrates him as "Forerunner and Baptist" and keeps his nativity on 24 June, a feast also among the oldest in the West.[^s19][^s14]

## Scholarly views

Historians widely accept John as a real, influential prophet, since Josephus, in a passage generally judged authentic, describes him independently.[^s13][^s15] The two accounts give different reasons for his death: the Gospels point to his rebuke of Herod's marriage, while Josephus says Herod feared his popularity might spark an uprising. Many scholars see these as complementary, a private grievance and a public, political motive.[^s4][^s13][^s15] Since the Dead Sea Scrolls were found, scholars have debated whether John had contact with the Essene community. Both were priestly, ascetic and expecting judgment, but John founded no separate community and called the whole nation, sinners included, to a once-for-all baptism; views range from real influence to mere resemblance.[^s16][^s15] Scholars also debate the roots of his baptism (purity washings, prophetic promises of cleansing, Qumran rites), whether Jesus first moved in John's circle, and how historical Luke's birth narrative is.[^s15][^s17]

## Common misreadings

John's question from prison is sometimes taken as proof he lost his faith; the text does not say so, and interpreters suggest perplexity at a kingdom unlike the judgment he announced, or concern for his disciples.[^s6][^s15] Others treat John's baptism as identical to Christian baptism, but Acts 19 distinguishes the two.[^s9][^s18]

## Connections

John is presented as **fulfilling** Isaiah (Matthew 3:3) and Malachi (Matthew 11:10, 11:14).[^s5][^s6] His call to bear fruit **illustrates** repentance.[^s2] He is **corroborated by** Josephus's *Antiquities*, and his priestly roots and washings are **explained by** Second Temple Judaism.[^s13][^s15] He is also linked to Jesus and his baptism, the first disciples, Elijah, Mary, Herod Antipas and the Essenes.[^s1][^s3][^s7][^s16]
