# Paul

Paul is the New Testament's great missionary and letter-writer. Born a Jew in Tarsus and trained in the law in Jerusalem, he first appears as Saul, a zealous persecutor of Jesus's followers; after meeting the risen Christ near Damascus he spent the rest of his life carrying the gospel to the Gentiles.[^s2][^s1] Acts tells much of his story, and thirteen letters in the New Testament bear his name, so knowing Paul opens up a large part of the Bible.[^s11][^s12]

## Why it matters

Historians treat Paul's own letters as the best source for his life and thought, and they fill a large part of the New Testament.[^s12] His story is also a living picture of the gospel he preached: the man who had persecuted the church calls himself the least of the apostles, yet says that by God's grace he is what he is (1 Corinthians 15:9-10).[^s5] Reading his letters well depends on knowing who he was and where he came from.[^s11]

## Life and story

**Saul the Pharisee.** Paul describes himself as "born in Tarsus of Cilicia, but brought up in this city at the feet of Gamaliel" (Acts 22:3, WEB), and he lists his credentials as circumcised on the eighth day, of the tribe of Benjamin, "a Hebrew of Hebrews" and, regarding the law, a Pharisee (Philippians 3:5).[^s2][^s4] He was also a Roman citizen by birth (Acts 22:25-28) and supported himself by a trade, tentmaking (Acts 18:3).[^s2][^s9] Two names were natural for such a man: Saul, a Hebrew name meaning "asked for," and Paul, a Roman name meaning "little"; Acts simply speaks of "Saul, who is also called Paul" (Acts 13:9, WEB).[^s8][^s11]

**Persecutor turned apostle.** Saul approved of Stephen's death and then "ravaged the assembly," dragging believers to prison (Acts 8:1-3, WEB).[^s7] On the road to Damascus a light flashed around him and a voice asked, "Saul, Saul, why are you persecuting me?" (Acts 22:7, WEB); in Acts 9 the Lord tells Ananias that Saul is his chosen instrument to carry his name to Gentiles, kings and Israel.[^s2][^s1] Paul himself describes the event as God revealing his Son in him so that he might preach among the Gentiles (Galatians 1:15-16).[^s3] Most scholars place it in the early to mid 30s AD.[^s11][^s12]

**Missionary years.** After time in Arabia and Damascus, a fifteen-day visit to Peter (Cephas) in Jerusalem and a time in Syria and Cilicia (Galatians 1:17-24), Paul was sent out with Barnabas from the church at Antioch (Acts 13:1-4).[^s3][^s8] For roughly two decades he founded churches in Asia Minor and Greece, including the church at Corinth, while working with his own hands.[^s12] He lists the cost: beaten with rods three times, stoned once and shipwrecked three times (2 Corinthians 11:25).[^s10]

**Arrest and Rome.** Arrested in Jerusalem in the late 50s and tried there, Paul was sent to Rome as a prisoner, where Acts leaves him for "two whole years in his own rented house," preaching the kingdom of God "without hindrance" (Acts 28:30-31, WEB).[^s6][^s12] Acts does not record his death. Clement of Rome, in a letter to the church at Corinth, says Paul was seven times thrown into captivity, preached in east and west, came to "the extreme limit of the west" and suffered martyrdom; later tradition places his death in Rome, possibly during Nero's persecution in the mid-60s.[^s13][^s12]

## Legacy in Scripture

Paul's letters to churches and co-workers — from Romans to Philemon — make up thirteen of the New Testament's twenty-seven books, and Acts follows his journeys from chapter 13 to its last verse.[^s12][^s8][^s6] In them he defends the welcome of Gentiles into God's people without first becoming Jewish, and he speaks of a righteousness that comes from God through faith in Christ rather than from his own keeping of the law (Philippians 3:9).[^s12][^s4] Acts presents his mission as fulfilling the Lord's call to be "a light for the Gentiles" (Acts 13:47, WEB).[^s8]

## Scholarly views

Christian tradition has received all thirteen letters as Paul's own, and many scholars still accept Pauline authorship of them all; the *Encyclopedia of the Bible*, for example, draws on Ephesians, Colossians, 1 Timothy and Titus as Paul's own letters.[^s11] Most critical scholars, however, distinguish seven undisputed letters (Romans, 1 and 2 Corinthians, Galatians, Philippians, 1 Thessalonians and Philemon) from others they think were written by Paul's followers, with opinion fairly evenly split on Colossians and 2 Thessalonians and most doubting Ephesians and the Pastoral Letters (1 and 2 Timothy, Titus).[^s12][^s14] Scholars who hold the latter view stress that it is a question about authorship, not about whether these books belong in the canon.[^s14] Historians also discuss how to combine Acts, a second-hand account, with Paul's own letters, which they treat as the primary evidence when the two seem to differ.[^s12] Hebrews, sometimes linked with Paul in the past, does not name him as author.[^s14]

## Common misreadings

A popular picture says Saul "changed his name" to Paul when he converted. Acts never says so: it uses "Saul" well after his conversion and introduces "Paul" in Acts 13:9 simply as his other name.[^s8][^s1] Another mistake is to imagine that Paul left Judaism behind; he still introduced himself as "a Jew" (Acts 22:3, WEB), and he counted his former achievements as loss not because they were wicked but because knowing Christ surpassed them (Philippians 3:7-8).[^s2][^s4]

## Connections

Paul **was a member of** the Pharisees, as he says in Philippians 3:5.[^s4] His life **illustrates** grace: he calls himself unworthy to be an apostle, yet says God's grace made him what he is (1 Corinthians 15:9-10).[^s5] His training under Gamaliel and his zeal for the law are **explained by** the Jewish world of the Second Temple period.[^s11] His ministry and martyrdom are **attested outside the Bible** by Clement of Rome's First Epistle to the Corinthians.[^s13] He is closely tied to the Acts of the Apostles, which tells his story, to Barnabas, his companion on his first journey (Acts 13:1-4), and to Peter, whom he visited in Jerusalem (Galatians 1:18).[^s8][^s3]
