# Book of Jeremiah

The Book of Jeremiah is the record of a prophet who spoke for God through the last forty years of the kingdom of Judah and into the Babylonian exile. Its opening dates his work from the thirteenth year of King Josiah to Jerusalem's captivity under Zedekiah.[^s1] Christians count it among the major prophetic books of the Old Testament, and it weaves together prophecy, history and biography in a way that rewards slow reading.[^s17][^s14]

## Why it matters

Jeremiah shows what faithfulness looks like when a nation is collapsing. He warns that worship without justice cannot save,[^s2] tells exiles to build houses and pray for the city of their captors,[^s5] and, in the darkest hour, promises a new covenant in which God will write his law on his people's hearts and remember their sin no more.[^s6] Jesus took up that promise at the Last Supper ("This cup is the new covenant in my blood", Luke 22:20, WEB),[^s11] and Hebrews 8 quotes the passage at length.[^s10] His anguished prayers also invite readers to bring honest complaints to God.[^s3]

## Background

**Author.** The book opens by naming Jeremiah, one of the priests of Anathoth, as the one whose words it records.[^s1] The Catholic bishops' introduction places Anathoth a few miles north-east of Jerusalem and suggests he was born around 650 BC.[^s14] God calls him before his birth, overrides his protest that he is too young, and appoints him to uproot and tear down, but also to build and plant (Jeremiah 1:4-10).[^s1] His scribe Baruch son of Neriah wrote down his oracles at dictation (Jeremiah 36:4).[^s8]

**Date and setting.** Jeremiah's ministry began about 627/626 BC and outlasted the fall of Jerusalem in 587/586 BC.[^s17] He lived through Josiah's reform, Josiah's death in 609 BC, the first Babylonian deportation under Jehoiachin in 598/597 BC and Zedekiah's final years on the throne.[^s14] During the final siege he was confined in the guard's courtyard,[^s7] and afterwards he and Baruch were taken against their will to Tahpanhes in Egypt (Jeremiah 43:1-7).[^s9] The details of his death are unknown.[^s14]

**Audience.** First the people, kings and priests of Judah; then the exiles in Babylon, to whom he wrote (Jeremiah 29:1).[^s5] The book's present form was probably fixed after the exile.[^s15]

## Outline

The book is not arranged in strict chronological order.[^s16] A common outline:

- **Chapters 1–25:** Jeremiah's call and oracles against Judah and Jerusalem, including the Temple sermon (chapter 7) and the "confessions", his personal laments (chiefly chapters 11–20).[^s16][^s17]
- **Chapters 26–29:** clashes with priests and false prophets, and the letter to the exiles.[^s14]
- **Chapters 30–33:** the "Book of Consolation", promises of restoration and the new covenant.[^s16]
- **Chapters 34–45:** narratives of Jeremiah's sufferings, the burning of his scroll, the fall of Jerusalem and the flight to Egypt.[^s16][^s15]
- **Chapters 46–51:** oracles against the nations, ending with Babylon.[^s14][^s15]
- **Chapter 52:** a historical appendix on Jerusalem's fall, largely drawn from 2 Kings 24:18–25:30.[^s17]

## Key themes

**True and false worship.** At the Temple gate Jeremiah warns against chanting "the temple of Yahweh" as a charm while oppressing foreigners, orphans and widows and following other gods (Jeremiah 7:4-11).[^s2] Jesus later echoed his "den of robbers" when he cleared the Temple (Mark 11:17).[^s12]

**Judgment and the seventy years.** Jeremiah announces that the nations will serve the king of Babylon seventy years (Jeremiah 25:11-12),[^s4] and he tells the exiles not to expect a quick return but to seek the peace of Babylon, because in its peace they will find theirs (Jeremiah 29:7, WEB).[^s5]

**Hope beyond judgment.** The well-loved promise of "hope and a future" (Jeremiah 29:11, WEB) was spoken to a whole people in exile, with a homecoming after seventy years in view.[^s5] In the same spirit Jeremiah buys a family field at Anathoth while the Babylonians besiege Jerusalem, as a sign that fields will again be bought in the land (Jeremiah 32:6-15).[^s7]

**The new covenant.** Jeremiah 31:31-34 contrasts the covenant broken after the Exodus with a new one: the law within, everyone knowing the Lord, and sins forgiven.[^s6] The New Testament reads this as fulfilled in Christ (Luke 22:20; Hebrews 8:8-13).[^s11][^s10]

**The suffering prophet.** In Jeremiah 20 the prophet says God's word is "a burning fire shut up in my bones" (Jeremiah 20:9, WEB) and then curses the day he was born.[^s3] Matthew also hears Jeremiah's image of Rachel weeping for her children (Jeremiah 31:15) in the grief of Bethlehem's mothers (Matthew 2:17-18).[^s6][^s13]

## Scholarly views

Traditional readings take the book as Jeremiah's preaching, compiled by Baruch soon after the events; the *Asbury Bible Commentary*, for example, holds that the oracles are Jeremiah's words, the narratives likely Baruch's, and the final editing light.[^s16] Many critical scholars since Bernhard Duhm and Sigmund Mowinckel distinguish poetic oracles, biographical prose and sermons in a style close to Deuteronomy, and debate how far later editors shaped them; the *New Catholic Encyclopedia* notes that the poetry's authenticity is now rarely disputed, while the prose sermons are still discussed.[^s15]

There are also two forms of the text. The Greek Septuagint is about one-eighth shorter than the Hebrew Masoretic Text and places the oracles against the nations in the middle of the book rather than at the end.[^s19][^s15] Emanuel Tov notes that some Hebrew fragments of Jeremiah from Qumran share features of the shorter Greek form,[^s18] and the *Asbury Bible Commentary* judges that the two textual traditions probably existed side by side from early on.[^s16]

## Connections

The book **features** the **Babylonian Exile**, writing to the deported community.[^s5] It **features Jerusalem** and the Temple at the heart of the Temple sermon,[^s2] the prophet **Jeremiah**, to whom it is **attributed**,[^s1] his scribe **Baruch**,[^s8] and **Nebuchadnezzar II**.[^s4] It **teaches** the **new covenant**[^s6] and contrasts it with the **Mosaic covenant** made after the Exodus. Its closing chapter **parallels 2 Kings**;[^s17] it is **part of** the **Major Prophets** and belongs to the genre of **prophecy**.[^s17][^s14] Its text is **preserved in** the **Septuagint**[^s19] and in fragments among the **Dead Sea Scrolls**.[^s18] The **Book of Ezra** tells of the return Jeremiah's letter looked toward, and **Reading in Context** is the best guard against lifting Jeremiah 29:11 out of the letter to the exiles in which it stands.[^s5]
