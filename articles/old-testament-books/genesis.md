# Genesis

Genesis is the Bible's book of beginnings. Its Hebrew name, *Bereshit*, comes from its first word, "In the beginning"; the Greek name used by the Septuagint and Philo, *Genesis*, means "origin".[^s10][^s12] It opens with God creating "the heavens and the earth" and declaring everything he made "very good" (Genesis 1:1, 1:31, WEB), and it ends fifty chapters later with the family of Jacob settled in Egypt.[^s1][^s10] In between it tells how sin entered and spread through the world, and how God answered by calling one man, Abraham, through whom "all the families of the earth" would be blessed (Genesis 12:3, WEB).[^s4] It is the first of the five books that Jews call the Torah and Christians often call the Pentateuch.[^s10]

## Why it matters

Almost every great thread of the Bible starts in Genesis: God as Creator, human beings made in his image (Genesis 1:27), sin and its consequences, covenant, promise, election and faith.[^s1][^s11] The Catechism of the Catholic Church says the first three chapters hold "a unique place" among Scripture's texts on creation and remain the main source for teaching on the mysteries of the beginning.[^s16] The New Testament keeps returning here: Paul builds his teaching on faith on Genesis 15:6, "Abraham believed God, and it was accounted to him for righteousness" (Romans 4:3, WEB), and calls the promise of Genesis 12:3 the gospel announced to Abraham in advance (Galatians 3:8).[^s8][^s9]

## Background

The book names no author. Jewish and Christian tradition attributed the Torah, Genesis included, to Moses; a well-known passage of the Babylonian Talmud says Moses wrote "his own book", while Joshua (in one rabbinic view) added the last verses describing Moses' death.[^s13] Older reference works written from a traditional standpoint, such as the 1915 *International Standard Bible Encyclopedia*, defended the book's unity against critical theories.[^s11] Modern critical scholars, by contrast, see Genesis as woven from several older traditions and completed long after Moses, in or after the exile (see *Scholarly views*).[^s10][^s15] Readers of every view agree that Genesis was given to Israel to explain where the world, the nations and Israel itself came from, and why God chose Abraham's family.[^s12]

## Outline

Genesis falls into two great parts, held together by a repeated heading, "This is the history of the generations of…" (Hebrew *toledot*), which introduces each new section (Genesis 2:4; 37:2).[^s2][^s6][^s11]

1. **Primeval history (chapters 1–11):** creation (1:1–2:3); Eden and the fall (2–3); Cain and Abel and the generations before the Flood (4–5); the Flood (6–9); the table of nations and the tower of Babel (10–11).[^s10][^s11]
2. **Abraham (11:27–25:18):** God's call and covenant promises, the long wait for a son, and the testing of Abraham.[^s4][^s5][^s11]
3. **Isaac, Jacob and Esau (25:19 to chapter 36):** the promise passes to Isaac and then, through struggle, to Jacob, who is also called Israel.[^s10][^s11][^s6]
4. **Joseph (37–50):** Jacob's favourite son is sold by his brothers, rises in Egypt, and saves the family from famine.[^s6][^s10]

## Key themes

- **Creation and the image of God.** God creates by his word, blesses humanity, and makes male and female in his image (Genesis 1:26-28); Genesis 2:7 adds the intimate picture of God breathing life into the first man.[^s1][^s2]
- **Sin and its spread.** Disobedience in Eden is followed by murder, violence and pride, yet God keeps acting in mercy. Genesis 3:15 speaks of enmity between the serpent and the woman's offspring; the Catholic Catechism calls this verse the *Protoevangelium*, the "first gospel", a first announcement of the Redeemer.[^s3][^s17]
- **Promise and covenant.** God promises Abram a great nation, a land and blessing for all peoples (Genesis 12:1-3), and seals the promise by covenant (Genesis 15:18).[^s4][^s5]
- **Faith.** Abram, still childless, is told to count the stars, and "He believed in Yahweh, who credited it to him for righteousness" (Genesis 15:6, WEB).[^s5]
- **Providence.** Joseph tells his brothers, "you meant evil against me, but God meant it for good" (Genesis 50:20, WEB): God's purposes are not defeated by human wrongs.[^s7]

## Scholarly views

From the eighteenth century, beginning with Jean Astruc's observation that Genesis alternates between the divine names *Yahweh* and *Elohim*, scholars proposed that the book combines earlier sources; Julius Wellhausen's 1878 statement of this "Documentary Hypothesis" became the classic form.[^s15][^s12] Britannica summarises the usual three strands in Genesis: a Yahwist (J), an Elohist (E) and a Priestly source (P), the last commonly dated to the fifth century BC, each preserving still older material.[^s10] Since the late 1970s the field has divided: many European scholars have moved to non-documentary models, while a "neo-documentary" approach, especially in North America, has revived and refined source analysis.[^s14] Traditional scholars, Jewish and Christian, have defended the unity of the book and its link to Moses.[^s11][^s13] Christians also read the creation week in more than one way: the Catholic Biblical Commission, for example, allowed that the "days" may be taken as ordinary days or as longer periods, while insisting on the historical foundations of creation, the fall and the promise of a Redeemer.[^s18]

## Common misreadings

One misreading treats Genesis 1–11 merely as a science textbook; another treats it as mere myth with no claim on us. Both miss the book's main concern, which is who God is, who we are, and how God's promise answers human sin.[^s16][^s18] A second mistake is to read the patriarchs as moral heroes in every scene. Genesis is frank about their deceptions and family wounds, and the hero of the book is the faithful God who keeps his promise through flawed people.[^s6][^s7]

## Connections

Genesis is **part of** the [Pentateuch](bg:pentateuch), the five books of the Torah.[^s10] It **features** [Abraham](bg:abraham), whose call in Genesis 12 begins the story of Israel, along with [Isaac](bg:isaac) and [Joseph](bg:joseph-son-of-jacob), whose story begins at Genesis 37.[^s4][^s10][^s6] It **teaches** the [Abrahamic covenant](bg:abrahamic-covenant), sealed in Genesis 15.[^s5] Tradition **attributes** it to [Moses](bg:moses), a view scholars debate.[^s13][^s14] Its closing scenes lead directly into [the Exodus](bg:the-exodus), and its themes of creation, fall and promise shape [the story of the Bible](bg:the-story-of-the-bible) as a whole.[^s10][^s11]
