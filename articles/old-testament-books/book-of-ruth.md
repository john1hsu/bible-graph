# Book of Ruth

The Book of Ruth is a short story of four chapters that opens "in the days when the judges judged" with a famine in the land (Ruth 1:1, WEB).[^s1] A family from Bethlehem moves to Moab; the husband and both sons die, and the widow Naomi returns home with her Moabite daughter-in-law Ruth, who refuses to leave her.[^s1] In Bethlehem, Ruth gleans in the barley fields of Boaz, a relative of Naomi's husband, and the story ends with their marriage, the birth of Obed, and a genealogy reaching to King David (4:13-22).[^s2][^s4] The *Jewish Encyclopedia* calls it a prose narrative of idyllic character.[^s12]

## Why it matters

Ruth shows God at work in ordinary lives: no miracles, no prophets, only a famine, a harvest and people who choose loyalty. The USCCB introduction notes that dialogue makes up about two-thirds of the text and that God blesses each loving decision from behind the scenes.[^s11] Ruth is also an outsider, repeatedly called "the Moabite", who comes to take refuge under the wings of Israel's God (2:12) and becomes the great-grandmother of David.[^s2][^s4][^s11] Matthew names her in the genealogy of Jesus (Matthew 1:5).[^s9]

## Background

**Author.** The book is anonymous. A Talmudic tradition says Samuel wrote it, but the *Catholic Encyclopedia* and the *Encyclopedia of the Bible* both judge that unlikely.[^s13][^s10]

**Date and setting.** The events are set in the period of the judges; the book itself was written later, and its date is debated (see *Scholarly views*).[^s10][^s11]

**Place in the Bible.** The Greek Septuagint, the Latin Vulgate and English Bibles place Ruth after Judges and before Samuel, as a bridge from the tribal era to the monarchy.[^s13][^s11] In the Hebrew Bible it belongs to the Writings (Ketuvim) as one of the five scrolls (Megillot); in some Hebrew orders it follows Proverbs, next to the portrait of the "woman of worth" in Proverbs 31.[^s12][^s11] Jews read it at the feast of Weeks (Shavuot), and My Jewish Learning gives three reasons: its harvest setting, Ruth's embrace of Israel's God echoing Israel's receiving of the Torah, and its link to David.[^s10][^s16]

## Outline

1. **Loss and return (chapter 1):** famine, three deaths, Orpah's farewell, Ruth's vow "Where you go, I will go" (1:16, WEB), and Naomi's bitter homecoming as the barley harvest begins (1:19-22).[^s1]
2. **The field (chapter 2):** Ruth "happened" to glean in Boaz's portion of the field (2:3); Boaz protects her, and Naomi blesses the Lord "who has not left off his kindness" (2:20, WEB).[^s2]
3. **The threshing floor (chapter 3):** at Naomi's prompting Ruth asks Boaz, a near kinsman, to spread his garment over her; he praises her kindness and calls her a worthy woman, but a nearer kinsman must be asked first (3:9-12).[^s3]
4. **The gate and the genealogy (chapter 4):** before the elders the nearer kinsman declines to redeem; Boaz buys the land and marries Ruth; Obed is born, and the line runs from Perez to David (4:1-22).[^s4]

## Key themes

- **Loyal kindness (Hebrew *hesed*).** Naomi prays that the Lord will "deal kindly" with her daughters-in-law (1:8, WEB), and kindness returns at each turn of the story (2:20; 3:10).[^s1][^s2][^s3] Odo and Ugwu argue that Boaz had no legal obligation to marry Ruth and that his public commitment goes beyond duty, an act of *hesed*.[^s14]
- **Redemption.** The Law let the nearest kinsman buy back a poor relative's land (Leviticus 25:25) and asked a brother to marry his brother's widow so that the dead man's "name not be blotted out of Israel" (Deuteronomy 25:5-10, WEB).[^s8][^s6] Ruth 4 weaves both customs together, including the old sign of the sandal.[^s4] The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* highlights the redeemer-kinsman (*go'el*) as a central theme.[^s10]
- **Providence.** God is seldom said to act directly, yet he gives food (1:6) and conception (4:13); the rest unfolds through chance and choice.[^s1][^s4][^s10]
- **The outsider welcomed.** Israel's harvest laws left gleanings "for the poor and for the foreigner" (Leviticus 19:9-10, WEB), and Ruth, a Moabite widow, is exactly that person.[^s7][^s2]

## Scholarly views

All Christian traditions and Judaism receive Ruth as Scripture; its canonicity has never been seriously questioned.[^s10] Its date and purpose are debated. Many scholars favour an early date in the monarchy, citing its classical Hebrew style, as the *Catholic Encyclopedia* did.[^s11][^s13] Others date it after the exile, pointing to its distant view of the judges and some late language, and read it as a gentle counterpoint to the measures of Ezra and Nehemiah against marriages with foreigners.[^s12][^s11] Proposed purposes include explaining David's ancestry, showing how a Moabite could join Israel despite Deuteronomy 23:3, or simply offering an edifying example of loyalty and providence.[^s13][^s10][^s5] The *Jewish Encyclopedia* also notes a tension: by levirate custom Obed would count as Mahlon's heir, yet the genealogy lists him as Boaz's son.[^s12]

## Common misreadings

Ruth 1:16-17 is often read at weddings as a love vow between spouses. In context, a widowed daughter-in-law speaks it to her mother-in-law, binding herself to Naomi's people and God until death.[^s1] The threshing-floor scene (chapter 3) is sometimes read as scandalous; the text itself has Boaz praise Ruth as a worthy woman and handle the matter openly at the city gate.[^s3][^s4]

## Connections

Ruth **features** Bethlehem, where the story begins and ends (1:1, 1:19, 4:11), and its three main characters, Ruth, Naomi and Boaz.[^s1][^s2] It **features** David, the climax of its closing genealogy (4:17-22).[^s4] It **teaches** redemption through the kinsman-redeemer, and its marriage is **explained by** the custom of levirate marriage.[^s10][^s12] It **has the genre** of prose narrative.[^s12] Jewish tradition **attributes** it to Samuel, an attribution modern scholars doubt.[^s13] Matthew's genealogy of Jesus names Boaz and Ruth (Matthew 1:5), and Jerome saw Ruth the Moabite as fulfilling a prophecy in Isaiah.[^s9][^s15] Its welcome of a Moabite gleaner connects to the harvest laws for the poor and the foreigner (Leviticus 19:9-10) and to loving one's neighbour, and its harvest setting to the feast of Weeks (Shavuot).[^s7][^s16]
