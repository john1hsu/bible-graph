# Deuteronomy

Deuteronomy is the fifth book of the Bible and the last of the five books of the Torah, the Pentateuch.[^s15][^s11] It opens, "These are the words which Moses spoke to all Israel beyond the Jordan" (Deuteronomy 1:1, WEB), and its Hebrew name, *Devarim* ("words"), comes from that first line.[^s1][^s17] Forty years after leaving Egypt, Israel is camped in the land of Moab, and Moses, who will not cross the Jordan, gives his farewell: he retells their journey, restates the law given at Horeb (another name for Sinai), and urges a new generation to keep the covenant in the land (Deuteronomy 1:3-5).[^s1][^s11] The English name comes from the Greek and Latin Bibles. It rests on the phrase in Deuteronomy 17:18 where the king is to write himself "a copy of this law" (KJV); the Greek translators read it as a "second law", and Jewish tradition also calls the book *Mishneh Torah*, "repetition of the Torah".[^s18][^s12][^s17]

## Why it matters

Deuteronomy gave both Judaism and Christianity some of their most treasured words. Its Shema, "Hear, Israel: Yahweh is our God. Yahweh is one" (Deuteronomy 6:4, WEB), stands at the centre of Jewish worship, and its verses are placed in the mezuzah on the doorpost and in the tefillin worn at morning prayer.[^s3][^s17] When Jesus was asked for the greatest commandment, he answered with Deuteronomy 6:5, love God "with all your heart", calling it "the first and great commandment" (Matthew 22:37-38, WEB).[^s10] The Catechism of the Catholic Church likewise says that Jesus summed up our duties to God with this saying, and roots it in the call "Hear, O Israel".[^s16] In the wilderness temptation, all three of Jesus' answers to the devil come from Deuteronomy (Matthew 4:4, 7, 10; compare Deuteronomy 8:3; 6:16; 6:13).[^s9][^s7][^s3][^s14]

## Background

The book presents itself as Moses' speeches on the plains of Moab, and both Jewish and Christian tradition long received it as his work, with the account of his death (chapter 34) usually credited to Joshua or a later hand.[^s14][^s12] Most modern scholars date its core later and connect it with the "book of the law" that the priest Hilkiah found in the Temple in the eighteenth year of King Josiah, about 622 BC (2 Kings 22:3, 8-13).[^s8][^s11][^s13] Its first audience, on either view, is Israel about to live in the land God promised, tempted to forget the God who brought them out of Egypt.[^s13][^s12]

## Outline

1. **Introduction and first speech (1:1–4:43):** Moses recalls the journey from Horeb and calls Israel not to forget what they saw.[^s1][^s12]
2. **Second speech (4:44–28):** the Ten Commandments repeated (chapter 5), the Shema and its exhortations (6–11), the law code (12–26), and the blessings and curses (27–28).[^s2][^s13][^s14]
3. **Third speech (29–30):** covenant renewal and the call to "choose life" (30:19, WEB).[^s5][^s13]
4. **Last words (31–34):** Joshua commissioned, the Song of Moses (32), Moses' blessing of the tribes (33) and his death on the edge of the land (34).[^s12][^s6]

## Key themes

- **One God and wholehearted love.** God chose Israel in love, and Israel is to answer with love that is loyalty, expressed in keeping his words and teaching them to their children (Deuteronomy 6:4-9).[^s3][^s13][^s12]
- **The covenant for every generation.** Moses tells people born after Sinai that God made the covenant at Horeb not only with their fathers "but with us, even us, who are all of us here alive today" (Deuteronomy 5:3, WEB).[^s2]
- **One place of worship.** Sacrifice is to be brought to "the place which Yahweh your God shall choose", where he puts his name (Deuteronomy 12:5, WEB); the city is not named in the book.[^s4][^s13]
- **Care for the vulnerable.** Israel's laws protect the poor, the widow, the orphan, the Levite and the resident foreigner, because Israel remembers being slaves in Egypt.[^s12][^s13]
- **Blessing or curse.** The commandment is "not too hard for you, neither is it far off" (Deuteronomy 30:11, WEB); Moses sets life and death before the people and urges them to choose life.[^s5]

## Scholarly views

The traditional view, still held by many conservative Jewish and Christian scholars, is that Moses is the book's author, apart from the account of his death; one reference work argues that its treaty-like shape points to a single historical occasion rather than a long growth.[^s14][^s12] Most critical scholars see the core law (roughly chapters 5–26 and 28) as a seventh-century work tied to Josiah's reform, later framed by chapters 1–4 and other additions. On this view Deuteronomy also serves as the opening of a larger history running through Joshua to 2 Kings.[^s11][^s13] The 1906 *Jewish Encyclopedia*, which accepts a late date, rejects calling the book a forgery and describes it as a prophetic restatement of older law.[^s12] Scholars on both sides compare Deuteronomy's form with ancient Near Eastern treaties. Some map it onto the older suzerainty-treaty pattern of preamble, history, stipulations, blessings and curses; others, such as Christine Hayes, see a closer match with the seventh-century Assyrian vassal treaties of King Esarhaddon.[^s14][^s13]

## Common misreadings

One misreading treats Deuteronomy as a dry list of rules. The book is really a sermon, full of memory, warning and appeal, and it grounds obedience in God's prior love.[^s12][^s16] Another reads its blessings and curses as a simple rule that good people always prosper. Deuteronomy is speaking to a nation about its covenant life in the land, and Christians read it alongside the rest of Scripture.[^s5][^s13]

## Connections

Deuteronomy is **part of** the [Pentateuch](bg:pentateuch).[^s15] It **features** [Moses](bg:moses) and the [Israelites](bg:israelites) on the plains of Moab, and it retells [the giving of the law at Sinai](bg:giving-of-the-law-at-sinai), which it calls Horeb (Deuteronomy 5:1-6, 22-27).[^s1][^s2] It **features** [Joshua son of Nun](bg:joshua-son-of-nun), on whom Moses laid his hands (Deuteronomy 34:9).[^s6] It **teaches** the [Mosaic covenant](bg:mosaic-covenant), made at Horeb and renewed in Moab.[^s2][^s11] Tradition **attributes** it to Moses.[^s14] Its form is **explained by** [ancient Near Eastern treaties](bg:ancient-near-eastern-treaties).[^s13] It also links to the [Ten Commandments](bg:ten-commandments), the [Shema](bg:shema), King [Josiah](bg:josiah), the [Deuteronomistic history](bg:deuteronomistic-history), [the Exodus](bg:the-exodus) it remembers, and Jesus' pairing of love for God with [love of neighbour](bg:loving-your-neighbor) (Matthew 22:37-40).[^s3][^s8][^s10][^s13]
