# 1 Kings

1 Kings tells the story of Israel's kings from the last days of David to the death of Ahab: Solomon's wisdom and his Temple, the splitting of the kingdom, and the prophets who confronted kings in God's name.[^s15][^s16] It is the first half of what was originally one book, Kings, which runs on into 2 Kings.[^s15][^s17]

## Why it matters

Here the house of God is built and dedicated, and Solomon's prayer, "heaven and the heaven of heavens can't contain you" (1 Kings 8:27, WEB), still shapes how Christians think about God's presence and about prayer.[^s4] The book also shows how quickly a gifted, blessed king can drift, and it gives us Elijah on Mount Carmel asking Israel to choose between the LORD and Baal.[^s6][^s9] Jesus and the apostles drew on it: Solomon's glory is outshone by the lilies, someone "greater than Solomon" has come, and the Most High does not live in houses made with hands.[^s11][^s12][^s13]

## Background

**Name and place in the canon.** In the Hebrew Bible, Kings (*Melakhim*) is one book among the Prophets; the Greek translators divided it and counted Samuel and Kings together as four "Kingdoms".[^s15][^s17] So Bibles that follow the Septuagint or the Vulgate, including older Catholic versions, call this book 3 Kingdoms or 3 Kings, while Protestant and most modern Bibles call it 1 Kings.[^s14][^s18]

**Author and date.** The book names no author. The Talmud names Jeremiah, a view the *Catholic Encyclopedia* reports many interpreters found probable.[^s14] Because Kings ends with the exiled king Jehoiachin's release in Babylon (about 562 BC) but not with the return from exile, its final form is usually placed during the exile.[^s14][^s15][^s17]

**Sources.** The writer points readers to fuller records: "the book of the acts of Solomon" (1 Kings 11:41) and the chronicles of the kings of Israel and of Judah, cited again and again.[^s6][^s15] Scholars also see court records behind chapters 1-2 and cycles of prophet stories behind the accounts of Ahijah, Elijah and Micaiah.[^s17]

## Outline

1. **Solomon becomes king** (1-2): Adonijah's bid fails; Zadok anoints Solomon at Gihon (1:38-40).[^s1]
2. **Solomon's wisdom and glory** (3-10): the dream at Gibeon, the two mothers, the Temple and its dedication, and the queen of Sheba's visit.[^s2][^s4][^s5]
3. **Solomon's fall** (11): foreign wives turn his heart; Ahijah tears his cloak into twelve pieces.[^s6]
4. **The kingdom divides** (12-16): Rehoboam's harsh answer, Jeroboam's golden calves at Bethel and Dan, and a run of northern kings down to Omri and Ahab.[^s7][^s8]
5. **Elijah and Ahab** (17-22): drought, Mount Carmel, Horeb, Naboth's vineyard, and Ahab's death at Ramoth Gilead after Micaiah's warning.[^s9][^s10][^s16]

## Key themes

**Wholehearted worship.** Kings judges each king by his faithfulness to the LORD, not by his wars or wealth.[^s18] Solomon "loved Yahweh" yet "sacrificed and burned incense in the high places" (3:3, WEB); in old age his foreign wives drew his heart after other gods (11:1-10).[^s2][^s6] Jeroboam's calves became "a sin" (12:30, WEB), and Ahab provoked the LORD more than any king before him (16:30-33).[^s7][^s8]

**God's promise to David.** The Temple is built under God's word: if Solomon walks in his statutes, God will keep the promise made to David and dwell among his people (6:11-13).[^s3] Even in judgement one tribe is left to Solomon's son for David's sake and for Jerusalem's (11:11-13).[^s6] The *Catholic Encyclopedia* calls Kings an explanation of Nathan's oracle to David.[^s14]

**A house of prayer for all.** Solomon asks God to hear the foreigner who prays toward the Temple, so that "all the peoples of the earth may know your name" (8:41-43, WEB), and to forgive Israel when it repents even in a land of captivity (8:46-50).[^s4]

**The prophetic word.** Prophets announce the split of the kingdom, call down drought and fire, and refuse to flatter kings: "what Yahweh says to me, that I will speak" (22:14, WEB).[^s6][^s9][^s10][^s16]

## Scholarly views

Traditional Jewish and Christian readers have received Kings as inspired history, and some have followed the Talmud in naming Jeremiah as its author.[^s14] Many modern scholars read it as the last part of a "Deuteronomistic History" (Joshua to Kings) that judges Israel's story by the standards of Deuteronomy.[^s16][^s17][^s18] Many think a first edition appeared under Josiah, between 621 and 586 BC, and was completed in the exile, though the stages are disputed.[^s15][^s16] Some, like Britannica, see it as largely one writer's work.[^s18] Scholars agree it is theological history: it selects and shapes its sources to teach, sometimes at the expense of detail.[^s16][^s17] Outside the Bible, an Assyrian inscription, the Kurkh Stele (about 853 BC), names "Ahab of Israel" among the kings who fought Shalmaneser III.[^s19]

## Common misreadings

Solomon's wisdom is not a story of success to copy. Wealth and fame came as gifts he did not ask for (3:13), and the same king who prayed so beautifully ended by building shrines to other gods.[^s2][^s6] Nor is the split of the kingdom simply a political accident; the text says it was "brought about from Yahweh" through Rehoboam's harshness (12:15, WEB).[^s7]

## Connections

1 Kings **features** [Solomon](bg:solomon), from Gibeon to his fall,[^s2] and [David](bg:david) in his old age.[^s1] It **features** [the Temple](bg:the-temple), whose dedication fills chapter 8,[^s4] [Elijah](bg:elijah), whose contest on Carmel ends with "Yahweh, he is God!" (18:39, WEB),[^s9] and [Ahab](bg:ahab), son of Omri and husband of Jezebel.[^s8] It **teaches** the [Davidic covenant](bg:davidic-covenant), the promise its story sets out to explain,[^s14] and [monotheism](bg:monotheism), the choice Elijah sets before Israel.[^s9] It **parallels** [2 Chronicles](bg:2-chronicles), whose author used Kings as a source.[^s16] Tradition **attributes** it to [Jeremiah](bg:jeremiah).[^s14] It is **corroborated by** the [Kurkh Stele](bg:kurkh-stele), which names Ahab.[^s19] It continues in [2 Kings](bg:2-kings) and is read with [Deuteronomy](bg:deuteronomy) and the [Deuteronomistic history](bg:deuteronomistic-history).[^s15][^s17]
