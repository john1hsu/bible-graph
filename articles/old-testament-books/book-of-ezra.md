# Book of Ezra

The Book of Ezra picks up the story of Israel where the exile in Babylon ends. It opens with a decree of Cyrus, king of Persia, allowing the Jewish exiles to go home and rebuild the house of God in Jerusalem (Ezra 1:1-4).[^s1] Its ten chapters tell two stories: the first returnees rebuilding the Temple against opposition (chapters 1–6), and, decades later, the priest and scribe Ezra arriving to teach the Law and call the community back to faithfulness (chapters 7–10).[^s13][^s11] In the Hebrew Bible, Ezra and Nehemiah were originally a single work, later divided in Christian and then Jewish Bibles.[^s10]

## Why it matters

Ezra answers a question the earlier history books leave open: after the Temple was destroyed and the people deported, did God's promises end? The book's answer is that God "stirred" both a pagan king and his own people to begin again (1:1, 1:5).[^s1] It shows a small community restoring worship first, and gives the Bible one of its clearest portraits of a teacher of Scripture: Ezra "had set his heart to seek the law of Yahweh, and to do it, and to teach" (7:10, WEB).[^s5] The community it describes, gathered around the Temple and the Torah, is the beginning of the Jewish world Jesus was born into.[^s10]

## Background

**Author.** The book does not name its author. The Talmud credits Ezra with writing it, and Catholic scholar Charles Souvay notes that modern scholars disagree about how much of it comes from him.[^s12] From 7:27-28 the narrative shifts into Ezra's own voice, and scholars treat this part of the book as his first-person memoir.[^s5][^s11] Many scholars since the nineteenth century have linked Ezra–Nehemiah to the author of Chronicles (see *Scholarly views*).[^s10]

**Date and setting.** The story runs from Cyrus's first year after his conquest of Babylon in 539 BC to Ezra's work under a king named Artaxerxes.[^s14][^s5] Ezra 1:1-3 repeats almost word for word the last verses of 2 Chronicles (36:22-23), so that the two books join like overlapping links of a chain.[^s8][^s1]

**Languages.** Unusually, parts of Ezra are written in Aramaic, the official language of the Persian Empire: most of 4:8–6:18, which quotes letters and decrees, and the king's letter to Ezra in chapter 7.[^s10]

**Names.** Bible traditions number the books differently. The Greek Septuagint calls Ezra–Nehemiah *Esdras B*, and the Latin Vulgate calls Ezra "1 Esdras" and Nehemiah "2 Esdras".[^s12][^s10] In Protestant Bibles and the Apocrypha, "1 Esdras" names a different book, a Greek retelling that overlaps with Ezra.[^s12]

## Outline

Christopher Lortie shows that chapters 1–6 are built around the two verbs of Cyrus's decree, "go up" and "build": the going up succeeds, the building stalls, and prophets revive it.[^s15]

1. **The return (1–2):** Cyrus's decree; the Temple vessels returned under Sheshbazzar; the list of returnees.[^s1][^s11]
2. **Altar and foundation (3):** the altar rebuilt and daily offerings restored; at the laying of the foundation, the people sing "his loving kindness endures forever" while older priests who had seen the first Temple weep (3:11-12, WEB).[^s2]
3. **Opposition (4):** neighbouring peoples' offer to help is refused, letters are sent to Persian kings, and "work stopped on God's house" until Darius's second year (4:24, WEB).[^s3]
4. **Completion (5–6):** the prophets Haggai and Zechariah stir the builders; Darius confirms Cyrus's decree; the Temple is finished in Darius's sixth year and dedicated, and Passover is kept with joy (6:14-22).[^s4]
5. **Ezra's journey (7–8):** Ezra, a priest descended from Aaron and "a ready scribe in the law of Moses", travels from Babylon with a royal letter (7:1-28).[^s5]
6. **Confession and reform (9–10):** Ezra's prayer of confession over marriages with neighbouring peoples, and the community's covenant to put them away (9:1–10:5).[^s6][^s7]

## Key themes

- **God's sovereignty over history.** Lortie argues that the book presents Persian kings as instruments moved by God, not as the real authors of the restoration.[^s15][^s1]
- **Worship restored.** The altar comes before the walls, and the story of the Temple ends with Passover (3:1-6; 6:19-22).[^s2][^s4]
- **The word of God.** Prophets drive the rebuilding (6:14), and Ezra's mission is to teach the Law, as he later reads it to the gathered people with Levites who "gave the sense" (Nehemiah 8:8, WEB).[^s4][^s9]
- **A remnant and repentance.** Ezra's prayer thanks God for leaving a remnant and confesses that "our God has not forsaken us in our bondage" (9:9, WEB).[^s6]

## Scholarly views

Christians of every tradition receive Ezra as Scripture; questions of authorship and chronology are debated.[^s12][^s10] The traditional view dates Ezra's arrival to the seventh year of Artaxerxes I (about 458 BC), thirteen years before Nehemiah's.[^s10][^s12] Other scholars place Ezra after Nehemiah, in the seventh year of Artaxerxes II (about 397 BC), or in the 37th year of Artaxerxes I (428 BC).[^s10][^s12] On authorship, many scholars since Leopold Zunz (1832) assign Ezra–Nehemiah to the Chronicler, while others argue for separate authors and an earlier date for Ezra–Nehemiah.[^s10][^s13]

## Common misreadings

Chapters 9–10 are often read as simple hostility to outsiders. In the text, Ezra's concern is that Israel is adopting the detestable practices of surrounding peoples and repeating the unfaithfulness that led to exile (9:1, 9:10-14), and the book itself makes room for people who "separated themselves" from those practices to join the Passover (6:21).[^s6][^s4] These chapters are hard and deserve slow reading in context. A second misreading treats the book as a straight chronicle: chapter 4 gathers letters from later reigns before returning to Darius, so it is arranged by theme rather than strictly by date.[^s3][^s11]

## Connections

Ezra **features** Ezra himself, whose mission fills chapters 7–10, and Zerubbabel, who leads the rebuilding with the priest Jeshua (3:2; 4:3).[^s5][^s2] It **features** Cyrus the Great, whose decree opens the book (1:1-4).[^s1] It **features** the restored community from which Second Temple Judaism grew; the *Encyclopaedia Judaica* traces the practice of public Torah reading to Ezra's reading of the Law.[^s10] Jewish tradition **attributes** the book to Ezra.[^s12] Its opening **parallels** the close of 2 Chronicles.[^s8] Its picture of Cyrus is **corroborated by** the Cyrus Cylinder, which records his return of deported peoples; the British Museum notes that the Jews' return fits this same policy, though the cylinder does not name them.[^s14] The book presupposes the **Babylonian exile** and continues in the **Book of Nehemiah**.[^s13][^s10]
