# Book of Isaiah

The Book of Isaiah is one of the great prophetic books of the Old Testament.[^s16] It opens as "the vision of Isaiah the son of Amoz" about Judah and Jerusalem in the days of four kings: Uzziah, Jotham, Ahaz and Hezekiah (Isaiah 1:1, WEB).[^s1] Across sixty-six chapters it moves from judgment on a proud city to comfort for exiles and, finally, to a vision of new heavens and a new earth.[^s17][^s11]

## Why it matters

In Nazareth Jesus read Isaiah 61 and announced that the Scripture was fulfilled that day (Luke 4:16-21).[^s12] Mark introduces John the Baptist with Isaiah's "voice of one crying in the wilderness" (Mark 1:3; Isaiah 40:3),[^s14][^s6] Matthew hears the Immanuel sign in the birth of Jesus (Matthew 1:22-23),[^s13] and Philip "preached to him Jesus" to an Ethiopian official reading Isaiah's Servant passage (Acts 8:28-35, WEB).[^s15] The Catholic bishops' introduction notes that the Servant poems are reflected in the New Testament's understanding of Christ's passion and glorification.[^s16]

## Background

**Author.** The heading names Isaiah son of Amoz.[^s1] He received his call in a vision of the Lord enthroned in the Temple "in the year that King Uzziah died" (Isaiah 6:1, WEB).[^s2][^s16] He is sent to meet King Ahaz (Isaiah 7:3), and the book's narrative chapters are set in the reign of Hezekiah.[^s3][^s5][^s19] Whether he wrote the whole book is discussed under *Scholarly views*.[^s18]

**Date and setting.** The Catholic bishops' introduction dates his ministry from about 742 BC to Sennacherib's siege of Jerusalem in 701 BC, possibly longer.[^s16] These were decades of Assyrian power: Rezin of Syria and Pekah of Israel marched on Jerusalem (Isaiah 7:1),[^s3] Samaria fell in 722 BC,[^s19] and Sennacherib invaded Judah in Hezekiah's fourteenth year (Isaiah 36:1).[^s5] Chapters 40–66 address a later situation: Jerusalem in ruins, its people in exile, and the Persian king Cyrus named as the Lord's "anointed" (Isaiah 45:1, WEB).[^s19][^s7][^s18]

**Audience.** Judah's kings and people; then exiles in Babylon and those who returned.[^s18]

## Outline

A widely used outline:[^s17][^s18][^s19]

- **Chapters 1–12:** judgment and hope for Judah and Jerusalem, including Isaiah's call (chapter 6) and the Book of Immanuel (chapters 6–12).[^s16]
- **Chapters 13–23:** oracles against the nations.[^s19]
- **Chapters 24–27:** a vision of judgment on the whole earth, often called the Isaiah Apocalypse.[^s16][^s19]
- **Chapters 28–35:** a series of woes, closing with judgment on Edom and a vision of restoration.[^s17][^s19]
- **Chapters 36–39:** narratives of Hezekiah's reign and Sennacherib's invasion largely parallel to 2 Kings 18–20.[^s19]
- **Chapters 40–55:** comfort for the exiles, Cyrus as the Lord's instrument and the Servant of the Lord, often called the Book of Consolation.[^s18]
- **Chapters 56–66:** life in the restored community and the promise of new heavens and a new earth.[^s17][^s11]

## Key themes

**The Holy One.** Isaiah's call is the book's keynote: seraphim cry "Holy, holy, holy, is Yahweh of Armies!", Isaiah confesses he is a man of unclean lips, a coal from the altar takes away his sin, and he offers to be sent (Isaiah 6:3-8, WEB).[^s2]

**Trust in God, not in empires.** When Ahaz fears his neighbours, Isaiah tells him to stay calm and offers a sign: a young woman, "the virgin" in the WEB, will bear a son called Immanuel, "God with us" (Isaiah 7:14).[^s3] The royal child of Isaiah 9:6-7 is named "Wonderful Counselor, Mighty God, Everlasting Father, Prince of Peace", and his peaceful rule on David's throne will have no end, upheld with justice and righteousness.[^s4]

**Comfort and return.** Chapter 40 opens with "Comfort, comfort my people" and a voice calling to prepare the way of the Lord in the wilderness, and it reminds frail people that "the word of our God stands forever" (Isaiah 40:1-8, WEB).[^s6]

**The Servant of the Lord.** Bernhard Duhm (1892) identified four "Servant Songs" in chapters 40–55.[^s20] The last begins "Behold, my servant will deal wisely" (Isaiah 52:13, WEB)[^s8] and describes one "despised and rejected by men", "pierced for our transgressions", by whose wounds others are healed (Isaiah 53:3-5, WEB).[^s9]

**Good news and new creation.** The anointed messenger of Isaiah 61:1-2 brings good news to the humble, liberty to captives and "the year of Yahweh's favor".[^s10] The book ends looking toward new heavens and a new earth, where the wolf and the lamb feed together and nothing harms on God's holy mountain (Isaiah 65:17-25).[^s11]

## Scholarly views

**One Isaiah.** The *New Catholic Encyclopedia* notes that until the nineteenth century Isaiah was accepted as author of the whole book, and that some Protestant, Jewish and Catholic scholars still defend its unity.[^s18] The ESV Global Study Bible treats the book as Isaiah's while recognising that its parts address the Assyrian age, the exile and the time beyond.[^s17] The New Testament cites every part of the book under Isaiah's name, though the *New Catholic Encyclopedia* observes that such citations follow the usage of the day rather than settling authorship.[^s18]

**Two or three Isaiahs.** From J. G. Eichhorn and later Bernhard Duhm, many scholars have assigned chapters 40–55 to an anonymous prophet near the end of the Babylonian exile ("Second Isaiah") and chapters 56–66 to the period after the return ("Third Isaiah").[^s18][^s16] They point to Cyrus named in 45:1, the exile assumed rather than predicted, and changes of style and vocabulary.[^s18][^s7] The bishops' introduction describes the whole as an anthology composed chiefly by the great prophet and also by later disciples.[^s16]

**The Servant.** Interpreters have seen the Servant as Israel or a faithful remnant, or as an individual: the prophet, another historical figure or the coming Messiah; the New Testament writers identify him with Jesus.[^s20] In ancient Jewish interpretation, the Aramaic Targum on Isaiah 52:13–53:12 calls the Servant the Messiah while applying the sufferings to others, such as Israel or the nations.[^s20]

**Isaiah 7:14.** The Hebrew word *ʿalmah* means a young woman of marriageable age; the Greek Septuagint rendered it *parthenos*, "virgin", and Matthew cites that Greek text.[^s21] Hallvard Hagelia notes that the focus of Isaiah 7:14 falls on the child's name, Immanuel, which Matthew and Luke read as pointing to Jesus.[^s21][^s13]

**The text.** Parts of about twenty copies of Isaiah were found at Qumran, including one complete scroll (1QIsa-a); among biblical books only Psalms and Deuteronomy are more numerous there.[^s22] Adolfo Roitman of the Israel Museum notes that one of them, 1QIsa-b, is close to the medieval Masoretic Text, "but not as close as appeared at first".[^s22] The WEB, for example, follows the scrolls and the Septuagint in reading "light" in Isaiah 53:11.[^s9]

## Connections

The book is **attributed to** the prophet **Isaiah**, whom it **features** in his Temple call.[^s1][^s2] It **features** King **Ahaz** in the Syro-Ephraimite crisis,[^s3] King **Hezekiah** under Assyrian siege,[^s5] **Cyrus the Great** as the Lord's anointed,[^s7] **Jerusalem**, the subject of its opening vision,[^s1] and the **Babylonian Exile**, the setting of its later chapters.[^s18] It **teaches** God's **holiness** through the seraphim's "Holy, holy, holy".[^s2] Its chapters 36–39 **parallel 2 Kings**.[^s19] It belongs to the genre of **prophecy**, beside the **Book of Jeremiah**.[^s16] Its text is **preserved in** the **Dead Sea Scrolls** and the **Masoretic Text**,[^s22] and in the **Septuagint**, whose wording of 7:14 Matthew follows.[^s21] It lies behind Jesus's **Nazareth sermon**,[^s12] the ministry of **John the Baptist**,[^s14] and the early Christian preaching about Jesus in Acts.[^s15]
