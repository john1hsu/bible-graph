# Synagogue

A synagogue (from the Greek *synagōgē*, "a gathering") was the Jewish local assembly and, increasingly, the building where it met.[^s10][^s11] In the Gospels and Acts it is everywhere: Jesus teaches "in their synagogues" across Galilee, and Paul, arriving in a new city, goes first to the synagogue on the Sabbath.[^s1][^s8] Knowing what happened there helps readers picture these scenes and see why so much early Christian preaching began from Israel's Scriptures.

## Why it matters

The synagogue was where ordinary Jews heard and learned the Law week by week. Josephus boasted that Moses had the people set aside their work to gather and hear the law, and this "not once or twice, or oftener, but every week."[^s17] James, speaking at the Jerusalem council, could assume the same: Moses is "read in the synagogues every Sabbath" in every city (Acts 15:21, WEB).[^s7] Jesus grew up inside that custom, and the first Christians carried a similar pattern of reading, teaching and prayer into their own gatherings.[^s2][^s18]

## Geography

Synagogues were not confined to Jerusalem. Ancient sources and excavations place them in rural villages of Judea and Galilee as well as in Diaspora cities, and archaeologists have identified at least ten first-century buildings as synagogues, typically with tiered benches around a central floor where a reader could be seen and heard.[^s14] At Magdala on the Sea of Galilee, a synagogue found in 2009 contained the carved Magdala Stone, with one of the earliest known images of a menorah; a second, plainer synagogue was announced there in 2021.[^s15] In Jerusalem, a Greek inscription found in 1913 in a cistern in the City of David records a synagogue built by Theodotus, a priest and synagogue leader.[^s19][^s12] Outside the land, inscriptions from Egypt as early as the third century BC speak of a *proseuchē*, a "prayer house."[^s11][^s16] In the New Testament we meet synagogues at Nazareth and Capernaum, at Antioch of Pisidia and at Thessalonica.[^s1][^s3][^s6][^s8]

## In the biblical story

**Roots in the Old Testament.** The word does not appear in the Hebrew Bible, but a scene in Nehemiah anticipates what the synagogue would do. The people gathered at the Water Gate, Ezra the scribe stood "on a pulpit of wood" and read the law from morning until midday, and the readers gave the sense so that the people understood (Nehemiah 8:1-8).[^s9]

**Jesus.** Luke presents Jesus' Nazareth sermon as the opening of his ministry. He entered the synagogue on the Sabbath "as was his custom," stood to read, was handed the scroll of Isaiah, and after reading sat down and said that the Scripture was fulfilled that day (Luke 4:16-21).[^s2][^s1] At Capernaum he taught in the synagogue with an authority that astonished his hearers (Mark 1:21-22; Luke 4:31-33), and he went on preaching in synagogues throughout Galilee (Mark 1:39).[^s3][^s1] The Gospels also show local life around the synagogue: Jairus, who fell at Jesus' feet, was one of its rulers (Mark 5:22), and Luke tells of a centurion praised because "he built our synagogue for us" (Luke 7:5, WEB).[^s4][^s5]

**Paul and the early church.** At Antioch of Pisidia, Paul and Barnabas sat in the synagogue; after the reading of the law and the prophets, its rulers invited them to speak, and Paul addressed both the men of Israel and those who feared God (Acts 13:14-16).[^s6] At Thessalonica he reasoned there from the Scriptures for three Sabbaths, "as was his custom" (Acts 17:1-3).[^s8] Even the word passed into Christian use: James 2:2 speaks of a rich and a poor man entering "your synagogue," which the KJV renders "assembly."[^s10] Around AD 150, Justin Martyr described Christians meeting on Sunday to hear the memoirs of the apostles or the writings of the prophets read, followed by instruction and prayers, a pattern that echoes Acts 13.[^s18][^s6]

## Scholarly views

Historians agree that the synagogue was a creation of the Second Temple period and that it grew far more central to Jewish life after the Temple's destruction in AD 70.[^s11][^s16] They disagree about how it began.

- **The exile.** A long-standing view holds that synagogues arose among exiles in Babylon as a substitute for the lost Temple. Shaye Cohen calls this plausible but notes that no ancient source supports it.[^s11]
- **The Diaspora.** Others start with the Egyptian *proseuchē* inscriptions, the earliest hard evidence, and see the institution taking shape among Jews living far from Jerusalem.[^s16][^s11]
- **Hasmonean Judea.** Yonatan Adler finds no sign of synagogues in Judea before the Hasmonean era, and links their rise to the spread of everyday Torah observance among ordinary people.[^s14]

A second question concerns what the early synagogue was for. Cohen and Adler both judge that the earliest Judean synagogues were chiefly places to read and teach the Law, with communal prayer becoming central later; the Diaspora name *proseuchē*, "prayer house", shows that prayer belonged to the institution early in some places.[^s11][^s14][^s16] The Theodotus inscription names its purposes as reading the Law and teaching the commandments, together with lodging for travellers from abroad.[^s12][^s13] Its date has been debated, but Kloppenborg's study and current editions place it before AD 70, roughly between 50 BC and AD 50.[^s12][^s13]

## Common misreadings

Christians sometimes picture the synagogue as a rival "church" opposed to Jesus, or as a mini-Temple. Neither fits. Sacrifice belonged to the Temple, while the synagogue gathered people around Scripture.[^s11] Its leaders were usually local notables and benefactors, not a body of rabbis, and the Gospels show Jesus as a regular participant who chose it as the place to announce his mission.[^s16][^s2]

## Connections

- It is **explained by** Second Temple Judaism, the world whose Sabbath reading and Torah study gave rise to it.[^s11]
- It **complements** the Temple: Jerusalem kept sacrifice and priesthood, while the synagogue brought the reading of the Law into every town.[^s11]
- It is **corroborated by** the Theodotus inscription from Jerusalem and by Josephus's *Against Apion*, which describes weekly gatherings to hear the Law.[^s12][^s17]
- It **illustrates** worship, as Acts 13 shows reading, exhortation and response gathered on the Sabbath.[^s6]
- It is **studied in** biblical archaeology, which has uncovered first-century synagogues at Magdala and elsewhere.[^s14][^s15]
