# Rabbinic Judaism

Rabbinic Judaism is the form of Jewish faith and life that took shape under the sages, or rabbis, after the Romans destroyed Jerusalem and its Temple in AD 70. Without Temple, priesthood or political independence, the sages built a Judaism that could be lived anywhere: centred on the Torah, read together with an inherited oral tradition, and expressed in study, prayer and acts of kindness.[^s13][^s14] Its great writings — the Mishnah, the Talmuds and the midrash collections — remain the foundation of Jewish reading of the Hebrew Bible.[^s12][^s15]

## Why it matters

Christians and Jews read many of the same Scriptures, and the Christian Old Testament is also Jewish Scripture, ordered in Jewish tradition as Torah, Prophets and Writings.[^s15] Knowing how the rabbis read them helps Christians see the Jewish world of Jesus and Paul, notice Jewish habits of interpretation in the New Testament, and speak about Jewish neighbours fairly.[^s15] The Catholic Church's Pontifical Biblical Commission, for example, calls the Jewish reading of the Bible "a possible one," continuous with the Scriptures of the Second Temple period, and says Christians can learn much from Jewish exegesis while still reading the same texts in the light of Christ.[^s15] Paul's own insistence that God has not rejected his people, and that "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:29, WEB), gives every Christian tradition a reason for that respect.[^s6]

## Key teachings

**One Torah, written and oral.** The rabbis taught that the revelation at Sinai had two complementary parts: the written Torah and an oral Torah that applies it to every age.[^s12] The Mishnah tractate *Avot* opens with a chain of transmission: Moses received the Torah at Sinai and handed it to Joshua, Joshua to the elders, and the elders to the prophets, down to the "Men of the Great Assembly," who urged teachers to raise many students and to make a protective fence around the Torah.[^s9] Rabbinic tradition read Exodus 34:27, where God tells Moses to write "in accordance with these words," as a hint of teaching given by mouth as well as in writing.[^s7][^s10] The 1906 *Jewish Encyclopedia* notes that later sayings claiming all rabbinic teaching was given at Sinai were not meant literally; many laws were understood as developed by deduction or enacted by the sages.[^s10]

**Study, prayer and kindness in place of sacrifice.** Britannica's account credits Yohanan ben Zakkai and his circle with a programme in which the study of Scripture and prayer took the place of sacrifice and pilgrimage, so faith no longer depended on one sanctuary.[^s14] A famous story preserved in *Avot de Rabbi Natan* has Yohanan comfort a grieving disciple at the Temple ruins with Hosea 6:6, "I desire mercy, and not sacrifice" (WEB): deeds of loving-kindness atone as the altar once did.[^s11][^s3] The historian Jacob Neusner summed up the shift this way: the rabbi became the new priest, and Torah study the new form of worship.[^s11]

**A written legacy.** Around AD 200 Judah the Prince edited the Mishnah, organising earlier tradition, mostly legal, into six orders.[^s12][^s14] Later generations of sages commented on it, producing the Jerusalem (Palestinian) Talmud around 400 and the Babylonian Talmud around 550.[^s12] Alongside these grew midrash, verse-by-verse interpretation of Scripture, and rabbinic material is often divided into *halakhah* (law) and *aggadah* (story and teaching).[^s12][^s14]

## How it reads Scripture

Rabbinic reading treats Scripture as a living word to be studied in community, argued over and applied. The Mishnah records disputed opinions alongside accepted ones, so learning means entering a conversation across generations.[^s14] Synagogue readings were translated into Aramaic and explained in sermons.[^s14] Roots of this habit appear in the Bible itself: when Ezra read the law to the returned exiles, the Levites helped the people understand what was read (Nehemiah 8:1-8),[^s1] and by the first century, Acts 15:21 can say that Moses was "read in the synagogues every Sabbath" (WEB).[^s5] The Pontifical Biblical Commission points out that the New Testament itself uses Jewish methods of interpretation — rules traditionally linked to Hillel, and techniques resembling rabbinic midrash — though always with Jesus as the decisive key.[^s15]

## Scholarly views

Historians agree that the sages saw themselves as spiritual, and sometimes physical, heirs of the Pharisees, and that the Pharisaic "tradition of the elders" (Mark 7:3) is an ancestor of the oral Torah.[^s13][^s2] They differ on how direct the line is. Neusner argued that rabbinic Judaism at Yavneh was a new synthesis, joining the Pharisees' practice of holiness with the scribes' ideal of learning.[^s11] Britannica's account dates the full shape of rabbinic Judaism to the period after the failed Bar Kokhba revolt in the 130s, when the rabbis became the only cohesive Jewish leadership; by the late second century they were widely recognised as the religious elite of the land of Israel.[^s14][^s13]

## Common misreadings

Christians sometimes read Mark 7 as Jesus rejecting all tradition. But his complaint is specific: a human rule (the *Corban* vow) was "making void the word of God" by excusing someone from honouring father and mother (Mark 7:13, WEB).[^s2] In Matthew 23:2 he acknowledges that the scribes and Pharisees "sat on Moses' seat," and in Matthew 9:13 he, like the later sages, reaches for Hosea 6:6.[^s8][^s4] It is also a mistake to treat the Judaism of the Talmud as identical to the Judaism of Jesus's day; it developed over centuries after the Temple fell.[^s12][^s13]

## Connections

Rabbinic Judaism was **influenced by** the Pharisees, whose heirs the sages considered themselves.[^s13] Its oral tradition was **set out in** the Mishnah, redacted by Judah the Prince around 200.[^s12] Its Temple-free programme is credited to Yohanan ben Zakkai, by whom it was **introduced**, at least in Neusner's account of Yavneh.[^s11] It is **explained by** its background in Second Temple Judaism and the crisis of AD 70.[^s13] It is closely related to the synagogue, where Moses was read every Sabbath,[^s5] and to the Jewish reading of the Scriptures Christians share, including the Masoretic Text, the Shema and the Jewish background of Jesus's parables.[^s15]
