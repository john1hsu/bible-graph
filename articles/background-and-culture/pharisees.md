# Pharisees

The Pharisees were a Jewish movement of the Second Temple period devoted to studying and living out the Torah in everyday life. Most readers meet them in the Gospels, where they question Jesus about handwashing, the Sabbath and the company he keeps, and where Jesus warns sharply against their hypocrisy.[^s1][^s3] Yet the same New Testament shows Pharisees inviting Jesus to dinner, warning him of danger and defending his apostles, and the apostle Paul calls himself "a Pharisee, a son of Pharisees" (Acts 23:6, WEB).[^s2][^s8] Knowing who they actually were helps us hear both Jesus's warnings and his common ground with them more clearly.

## Why it matters

The Pharisees appear on almost every page of the Gospels, so how we picture them shapes how we read Jesus. If they are cartoon villains, his warnings become comfortable criticism of *other* people. If they are seen as earnest, Scripture-loving believers, those same words become a mirror for devout readers today.[^s7] The Catholic Church's 1985 guidance on teaching about Judaism warns that an exclusively negative picture of the Pharisees is likely to be inaccurate and unjust, and it notes how much Jesus shared with them.[^s8]

## Who they were

The name probably comes from a Hebrew verb meaning "to separate" (*perushim*, "separated ones"), though other explanations have been proposed.[^s6] The movement likely grew out of pious Jews who resisted Greek cultural pressure in the second century BC, and it appears in history under the Hasmonean kings.[^s6] Josephus, the first-century Jewish historian, says the Pharisees handed down to the people many observances "by succession from their fathers" that were not written in the laws of Moses, which the Sadducees rejected; the Pharisees, he adds, had the common people on their side.[^s5]

Their convictions included the resurrection of the dead, a future judgment, angels, and a middle way between God's sovereignty and human freedom.[^s6] They paid close attention to tithing, ritual purity and shared meals, perhaps seeking to bring something of the Temple's holiness into ordinary homes.[^s7] Josephus counts "above six thousand" of them in Herod's day, a small but respected group; schools led by Hillel and Shammai debated how strictly to apply the law.[^s6] After Jerusalem fell in AD 70, Pharisaic teachers helped shape rabbinic Judaism, and the Mishnah (about AD 200) preserves much of their legal discussion.[^s6][^s7]

## In the biblical story

**Disputes with Jesus.** In Mark 7:1-13, Pharisees and scribes ask why Jesus's disciples eat without the ritual handwashing required by "the tradition of the elders"; Jesus answers with Isaiah and the example of *Corban*, warning against letting human tradition set aside God's command to honour father and mother.[^s3] Matthew 23 gathers his fiercest words: they "sit on Moses' seat," so the crowds should do what they teach but not imitate their works, and they tithe herbs while neglecting "justice, mercy, and faith" (Matthew 23:2-3, 23, WEB).[^s1]

**Friends and seekers.** Luke records Pharisees hosting Jesus at their tables (Luke 7:36) and warning him that Herod wanted to kill him (Luke 13:31).[^s8] Nicodemus, "a man of the Pharisees," comes to Jesus by night (John 3:1-2), and Gamaliel, a respected Pharisee and teacher of the law, persuades the council to leave the apostles alone (Acts 5:34-39).[^s6]

**Paul the Pharisee.** Paul lists being "concerning the law, a Pharisee" among his former credentials (Philippians 3:5, WEB), and before the council he sides with the Pharisees on the hope of the resurrection, splitting them from the Sadducees, who deny resurrection, angels and spirits (Acts 23:6-9).[^s4][^s2]

## Scholarly views

Historians agree the Pharisees were influential teachers of the law and forerunners of the rabbis, but they debate what kind of group they were — a religious sect, a political party, a philosophical school or a scholarly class — because the sources (Josephus, the New Testament and later rabbinic writings) are each partial and written with their own concerns.[^s7] Many Christian interpreters read Matthew 23 as Jesus's own prophetic rebuke, aimed not at every Pharisee but at hypocrisy within a movement that should have been righteous, much as the prophets confronted Israel; rabbinic writings themselves condemn hypocrisy.[^s6] Other scholars think the Gospels' sharpest language also reflects tensions between Jesus's followers and Pharisaic leaders after AD 70.[^s7] Across these views there is wide agreement that "legalistic" and "hypocrite" are not fair labels for the whole group.[^s7][^s8]

## Common misreadings

A common mistake is to treat "Pharisee" as a synonym for "hypocrite" or to imagine the Pharisees teaching that people earn God's favour by rule-keeping. The Gospels themselves name sincere Pharisees, Jesus tells the crowds to heed their teaching, and he shared their belief in the resurrection and their practices of prayer, almsgiving and fasting.[^s1][^s8] The warning of Matthew 23 lands best when Christians hear it as addressed to religious people like themselves.[^s6]

## Connections

The Pharisees **contrast with** the Sadducees, as Acts 23:6-9 shows in their dispute over the resurrection.[^s2] Their existence and teaching are **attested outside the Bible** by Josephus's *Antiquities of the Jews*, which describes their "tradition of the fathers."[^s5] They are **explained by** their setting in Second Temple Judaism, from the Hasmonean era to the Temple's destruction.[^s6] They are closely tied to Paul, Nicodemus and the Gospel of Matthew, and to rabbinic Judaism, which grew from their teaching.[^s6][^s7]
