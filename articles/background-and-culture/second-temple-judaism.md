# Second Temple Judaism

"Second Temple Judaism" is the name historians give to the faith and life of the Jewish people between the rebuilding of the Jerusalem Temple, dedicated about 515 BC, and its destruction by Rome in AD 70.[^s9] It is the world in which the last books of the Old Testament were written and in which Jesus was born, taught, died and rose. Jesus, his mother, the Twelve and Paul all belonged to it: Paul could say he had once "advanced in the Jews' religion beyond many of my own age" (Galatians 1:14, WEB).[^s3] Knowing this world helps Christians hear the New Testament as its first readers did.

## Why it matters

Much of the New Testament takes this world for granted: synagogues and Sabbaths, Temple feasts, debates about purity and resurrection, hopes for a Messiah, Scripture read in Hebrew, Aramaic and Greek.[^s12] Without some picture of it, readers tend to fill the gaps with guesses, or to imagine Judaism as a dull backdrop to Jesus. Scholarly introductions present Jesus instead as one Jew among many, proclaimed Messiah and worshipped first by fellow Jews.[^s12] Seeing the real variety of Jewish life also guards against the old picture of a single, monolithic Judaism.[^s11]

## Who they were

**Return and rebuilding.** After Babylon destroyed Jerusalem in 587 BC, exiles returned under Persian rule following Cyrus's decree of 538 BC.[^s9] Ezra 6:14-18 records the Temple finished in the sixth year of Darius, encouraged by the prophets Haggai and Zechariah, and dedicated with joy, with sacrifices offered for all Israel and the priests and Levites set in their divisions according to the book of Moses.[^s1] In Nehemiah 8:1-8 Ezra reads the Law aloud to the gathered people from morning until midday while Levites help them understand it.[^s2] Because Ezra taught the Torah as a guide for all of life, older reference works call him the "father of Judaism."[^s7]

**Under empires.** Alexander the Great's conquest (about 333–331 BC) brought Greek rule and Greek culture. When the Seleucid king Antiochus IV turned the Temple into a pagan shrine about 167 BC, the Maccabees revolted and rededicated it in 164 BC, an event later remembered at Hanukkah.[^s9] John 10:22-23 places Jesus in the Temple at "the Feast of the Dedication," in winter.[^s5] The Hasmonean dynasty that followed was itself contested, and Rome took control in 63 BC, ruling through Herod and his sons and later through Roman governors until the Temple fell in AD 70.[^s9]

**What held them together.** Jews of this era worshipped the one God of Israel, revered the Torah of Moses, practised circumcision and the Sabbath, and looked to the Jerusalem Temple as the centre of worship.[^s11][^s12] Synagogues became local centres of prayer and of reading and explaining Scripture; Jesus's reading of Isaiah in Nazareth fits that custom.[^s8][^s4] Many Jews lived far from the land, in the Diaspora, and read their Scriptures in the Greek Septuagint.[^s7][^s12]

**Where they differed.** No single council or leader defined belief and practice.[^s12] Josephus describes three "sects of philosophy" — Pharisees, Sadducees and Essenes — and a fourth, militant philosophy founded by Judas the Galilean, differing over fate and freedom, life after death and the role of tradition.[^s10] Groups also disagreed over which writings beyond the Torah were Scripture, over the Messiah and over how to live under Rome; Samaritans worshipped the same God but at Mount Gerizim rather than Jerusalem.[^s12][^s11] The Dead Sea Scrolls, including the oldest surviving copies of biblical books and the rule of a community calling itself the *Yahad*, show this variety first-hand.[^s13]

## In the biblical story

The story opens in Ezra and Nehemiah, with the Temple rebuilt and the Law read to the people.[^s1][^s2] It forms the setting of every Gospel: Jesus teaches in synagogues on the Sabbath (Luke 4:16-21) and walks in Solomon's porch at the feast of Hanukkah (John 10:22-23).[^s4][^s5] In Acts, Paul uses the Pharisees' hope of resurrection, which the Sadducees denied, to divide the council (Acts 23:6-8).[^s6] Galatians 1:13-14 shows Paul looking back on his zeal "for the traditions of my fathers."[^s3] Belief in the resurrection, held by the Pharisees but rejected by the Sadducees, was itself a live question within Judaism.[^s7]

## Scholarly views

Historians agree that Judaism in this period was varied, but they describe that variety differently. Some, following Jacob Neusner, speak of "Judaisms" in the plural; others accept the diversity but stress a shared core of one God, Torah, Temple and covenant identity.[^s11] Scholars also use different labels for the era — "Second Temple," "early" or "middle" Judaism — each with its own emphasis.[^s12] Some reference works date Judaism's beginnings to the exile itself, when Sabbath-keeping and gatherings for prayer and teaching took on new weight.[^s7] The origins of the synagogue are debated too: many date it to the exile, while an older traditional view traces it back to Moses.[^s8] Josephus's neat list of sects is now often read as a simplification of a richer landscape.[^s11]

## Common misreadings

One mistake is to picture first-century Judaism as a single legalistic system that Jesus opposed from outside. The New Testament itself shows Jesus keeping the Sabbath synagogue custom and Paul still calling himself a Pharisee.[^s4][^s6] Another is to read later rabbinic Judaism straight back into Jesus's day; rabbinic leadership was not widely recognised until well after AD 70.[^s11]

## Connections

Second Temple Judaism flourished **during** the Second Temple period, from the Temple's dedication in 515 BC to its destruction in AD 70; that article covers the era's political history.[^s9] It is **explained by** the Babylonian exile, where its Torah-centred faith and synagogue gatherings took shape.[^s7] It is **attested outside the Bible** by Josephus's *Antiquities of the Jews*, with its account of the Jewish philosophies, and by the Dead Sea Scrolls.[^s10][^s13] It is the wider setting of the Pharisees, Sadducees and Samaritans, of the synagogue and the Septuagint, and of Paul; rabbinic Judaism grew from it after AD 70.[^s7][^s11]
