# The Lord's Prayer

The Lord's Prayer — the "Our Father", or *Pater Noster* in Latin — is the prayer Jesus gave his disciples. Matthew places it at the heart of the Sermon on the Mount: "Pray like this: 'Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy'" (Matthew 6:9, WEB).[^s1] Luke gives a shorter form in answer to a disciple's request (Luke 11:1-4).[^s2] The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* counts five petitions in Luke and seven in Matthew, whose text the liturgy kept.[^s6]

## Why it matters

No prayer is shared more widely among Christians. Tertullian called it a summary of the whole gospel, and the *Catechism* adopts his phrase; Cyprian of Carthage saw in it a compact summary of heavenly teaching with nothing missing.[^s7][^s5] Praying it slowly is one of the surest ways to learn what Jesus thought prayer is for.[^s3]

## Context

In Matthew the prayer sits within Jesus's teaching on giving, praying and fasting in secret. He warns against praying to be seen and against "vain repetitions", for "your Father knows what things you need, before you ask him" (Matthew 6:5-8, WEB).[^s1] The prayer follows: short, direct, trusting.

In Luke, Jesus has just finished praying when a disciple asks, "Lord, teach us to pray, just as John also taught his disciples" (Luke 11:1, WEB). Then come the friend at midnight, the call to ask, seek and knock, and the promise that the Father will "give the Holy Spirit to those who ask him" (11:5-13).[^s2] The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* suggests Jesus taught the prayer on more than one occasion.[^s3]

## Walking through the text

**The address.** The Old Testament already calls God the Father of his people, but Jesus makes "Our Father" the ordinary way to begin.[^s3] Paul says the Spirit of adoption teaches believers to cry, "Abba! Father!" (Romans 8:15, WEB).[^s15]

**God's name, kingdom and will.** "May your name be kept holy. Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done on earth as it is in heaven" (6:9-10, WEB).[^s1] These petitions ask God to do what he has promised: in Ezekiel he says, "I will sanctify my great name" (Ezekiel 36:23, WEB).[^s13] The kingdom is the reign Jesus proclaimed, begun in him and awaiting completion.[^s3] In Gethsemane he prays, "My Father, … your desire be done" (Matthew 26:42, WEB).[^s14]

**Our needs.** "Give us today our daily bread" (6:11).[^s1] The Greek word behind "daily", *epiousios*, is almost unknown elsewhere, and has been read as bread needed for life, for today, or for the coming day.[^s3] Cyprian heard both Christ, the bread of life, and food for one day at a time.[^s5]

**Forgiveness.** "Forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors" (6:12, WEB). Luke has "Forgive us our sins" (11:4).[^s1][^s2] This is the only petition Jesus comments on afterwards: "if you forgive men their trespasses, your heavenly Father will also forgive you" (Matthew 6:14-15, WEB).[^s1] The familiar liturgical word "trespasses" comes from that comment rather than from verse 12.

**Testing and deliverance.** "Bring us not into temptation, but deliver us from the evil one" (6:13, WEB).[^s1] The Greek *peirasmos* means trial or testing, and the last phrase can be read as "evil" or "the evil one".[^s3] God does not lure anyone into sin: "God can't be tempted by evil, and he himself tempts no one" (James 1:13, WEB).[^s16]

## Interpretation

Two features shape the whole prayer. It is plural: Cyprian stressed that "our prayer is public and common", offered for the whole people.[^s5] And it puts God's name and kingdom before our bread and our sins, setting personal needs within a longing for God's reign.[^s3]

**The doxology.** Many Christians end with "For yours is the Kingdom, the power, and the glory forever. Amen." The World English Bible notes that the critical Greek text omits these words.[^s1] Scholars broadly regard them as an early liturgical addition: they are missing from the oldest manuscripts, such as Sinaiticus and Vaticanus, while the early church manual known as the *Didache* already closes with "for Yours is the power and the glory forever".[^s3][^s4][^s10] The encyclopedia still welcomes it as part of the Church's tradition of prayer, provided it is recognised as a later addition.[^s3]

**Common misreadings.** The warning against "vain repetitions" (6:7) is not a ban on praying the same words often: Jesus gives words to say, and the *Didache* told believers to pray them three times a day.[^s1][^s4] Nor does the forgiveness petition make grace a bargain; like the parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:33), it ties mercy received to mercy shown.[^s17][^s5]

**Jewish roots.** The *Jewish Encyclopedia* notes that the prayer's opening follows an early form of the doxology later called the Kaddish, which asks that God's great name be magnified and sanctified.[^s12] Jesus taught his disciples to pray as Jews longing for God's kingdom.[^s3]

## Views across traditions

All traditions pray it; they differ mainly in its setting in worship and how its ending is said.[^s6][^s9][^s11]

**Catholic.** At Mass the final petition is extended by a prayer, the embolism, that looks to "our blessed hope" and Christ's coming, after which the assembly responds with the doxology.[^s6] The doxology, the *Catechism* says, takes up the first three petitions as adoration and thanksgiving, and the closing "Amen" means "so be it" (CCC 2855-2856).[^s8]

**Orthodox.** In the Divine Liturgy the faithful pray the Our Father just before Holy Communion, daring as God's children through baptism to call him Father.[^s9]

**Lutheran and other Protestant.** Luther's *Small Catechism* teaches that "Our Father" invites believers to pray boldly, as children to a loving father, and that in the fifth petition they ask for undeserved grace and gladly forgive those who wrong them; it ends with the doxology and an "Amen" meaning "yes, yes, it shall be so".[^s11] The prayer also anchors Reformed liturgies and the Heidelberg Catechism.[^s3]

## Connections

- **Part of** the Sermon on the Mount and of the Gospel of Luke.[^s1][^s2]
- **Teaches** the Kingdom of God ("Let your Kingdom come").[^s3]
- **Speaks to** forgiveness in the Bible, the one petition Jesus expands on (Matthew 6:14-15).[^s1]
- **Explained by** the Kaddish, the Jewish doxology its opening resembles.[^s12]
- **Related** to the *Didache*, which quotes it, and to the parable of the unforgiving servant (Matthew 18:21-35).[^s4][^s17]
