# Calling of the First Disciples

The calling of the first disciples is the moment, early in Jesus's ministry, when he finds two pairs of fishermen brothers at work by the Sea of Galilee — Simon and Andrew, then James and John the sons of Zebedee — and summons them: "Come after me, and I will make you into fishers for men" (Mark 1:17, WEB).[^s1] Mark and Matthew tell it briefly, Luke around a great catch of fish, and John describes an earlier meeting near the Jordan.[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s4]

## Why it matters

These four fishermen head the lists of the apostles in the first three Gospels.[^s10] The scene shows how discipleship begins in the Gospels: not with a person choosing a teacher, but with Jesus taking the initiative and calling ordinary working people to share his life and his mission.[^s1][^s9]

## What happened

**Mark and Matthew.** After John the Baptist is arrested, Jesus comes into Galilee proclaiming that "the time is fulfilled, and God's Kingdom is at hand" and calling people to repent and believe the good news (Mark 1:14-15, WEB).[^s1] Passing along the lake, he sees Simon and Andrew casting a net and calls them; "immediately they left their nets, and followed him" (Mark 1:18, WEB). A little further on he finds James and John mending nets in their boat and calls them too; they leave their father Zebedee in the boat "with the hired servants" (Mark 1:19-20, WEB).[^s1] Matthew's account runs closely parallel and adds that the second pair left "the boat and their father" (Matthew 4:22, WEB).[^s2] They go on together to Capernaum and "the house of Simon and Andrew" (Mark 1:21, 29, WEB).[^s1]

**Luke.** Luke sets the call by "the lake of Gennesaret", another name for the same lake. Jesus teaches from Simon's boat, then tells him to let down the nets in deep water. Simon says they "worked all night" for nothing, yet obeys; the catch is so large the nets begin to break. Overwhelmed, he falls down before Jesus: "Depart from me, for I am a sinful man, Lord." Jesus tells him not to be afraid — "From now on you will be catching people alive" — and Simon and his partners James and John "left everything" to follow him (Luke 5:1-11, WEB).[^s3]

**John.** John's Gospel records an earlier encounter at "Bethany beyond the Jordan" where John the Baptist was baptizing. Two of the Baptist's disciples hear him call Jesus "the Lamb of God" and follow; Jesus asks, "What are you looking for?" and invites them, "Come, and see." One of them, Andrew, finds his brother Simon — "We have found the Messiah!" — and Jesus names Simon "Cephas". Setting out for Galilee, Jesus then calls Philip, who brings Nathanael (John 1:28, 35-51, WEB).[^s4]

## Significance

**A prophet's call, intensified.** Readers have long heard an echo of Elijah calling Elisha from his plough in 1 Kings 19:19-21. Elisha asks first to kiss his father and mother goodbye; he goes back, slaughters his oxen to feed the people, and only then follows Elijah and serves him.[^s5] The fishermen do not ask even that, and elsewhere Jesus tells a would-be follower who wants first to bid his household farewell that "no one, having put his hand to the plow, and looking back, is fit for the Kingdom of God" (Luke 9:61-62, WEB).[^s6] John Chrysostom drew the lesson for his congregation: the disciples obeyed without delay, believing Jesus's promise before they had seen a single sign.[^s8]

**A new kind of disciple.** In the first century, teachers such as the Pharisees and John the Baptist had disciples — learners who took on their teacher's way of life.[^s9] Here Jesus does the choosing, and he calls people to himself ("come after me"), not only to a body of teaching.[^s1][^s9]

**Fishers of people.** The image fits men who already fish for a living, but it also has Old Testament roots. In Jeremiah 16:16 the LORD says he will "send for many fishermen" to "fish them up", in a passage that sets judgment on sin beside a promise to bring Israel home from exile (Jeremiah 16:14-18, WEB).[^s7] Charles W. F. Smith argued that the phrase therefore first carried an urgent, end-time sense — gathering people before God's coming kingdom — and that later Christians tended to soften it into a general picture of preaching.[^s11]

**Ordinary work, real cost.** Galilean fishing families worked in a heavily taxed, licensed economy, sometimes hiring day labourers for the catch.[^s13] Mark's mention of Zebedee and the hired servants shows a working family business — and a real loss when two sons leave it.[^s1][^s13]

## Reading the accounts together

Christians have long read John 1 and the Synoptic accounts as successive stages rather than rival versions. Chrysostom argued that Matthew describes a second call, distinct from the meeting in John 1, pointing out that John's scene comes before the Baptist's imprisonment and Matthew's after it.[^s8] The Catholic Encyclopedia likewise presents Andrew as first following Jesus on the Baptist's testimony and later being summoned to leave everything (Luke 5:11; Mark 1:17-18).[^s10] The Orthodox Church honours Andrew as "the First-Called" for that earlier meeting, keeping his feast on 30 November.[^s12] Some modern scholars instead see Luke's catch and the resurrection scene in John 21 as tellings shaped at different stages of the tradition around a common core: Jesus calling fishermen by the lake.[^s11]

A common misreading treats "fishers of men" as a slogan about technique or numbers. The text stresses Jesus's call: he will *make* them fishers, and it begins with following him.[^s1][^s2]

## Connections

The event **features** Jesus, Peter and his brother Andrew, and James and John the sons of Zebedee (Mark 1:16-20), and in John's account John the Baptist, whose disciples first follow Jesus (John 1:35-40).[^s1][^s4] It **occurred at** Galilee, beside its lake, just after Jesus began preaching there.[^s1] It **illustrates** the kingdom of God, since the call follows directly on Jesus's announcement that the kingdom is at hand,[^s1] and it **illustrates** discipleship, the learner's life of following a teacher.[^s9] It is **explained by** Second Temple Judaism, where Pharisees and John the Baptist also had disciples.[^s9] It is also related to Elijah's call of Elisha, Jeremiah's fishermen, Capernaum, and the Great Commission that later sends the disciples out.[^s5][^s7][^s2]
