# The Nazareth Sermon

The Nazareth Sermon is the name often given to Luke 4:16-30, where Jesus returns to Nazareth, "where he had been brought up" (Luke 4:16, WEB), goes to the synagogue on the Sabbath as was his custom, and stands up to read from the scroll of Isaiah. He then sits down and tells the watching congregation, "Today, this Scripture has been fulfilled in your hearing" (4:21, WEB). Admiration soon turns to fury, and the townspeople try to throw him from the brow of the hill on which their town was built (4:22-30).[^s1] Enter the Bible, from Luther Seminary, notes that this is the first scene of Jesus's public ministry that Luke narrates at length.[^s9]

## Why it matters

Luke uses this scene as a doorway. Enter the Bible calls it a programmatic passage for the whole of Luke and Acts, and a recent study in *Cuestiones Teológicas* describes it as inaugural, programmatic and prophetic.[^s9][^s10] In one Sabbath morning the reader hears what Jesus came to do, the Spirit who empowers him, the claim that God's promises are being kept now, and a warning that God's mercy reaches beyond those who think they own it. Judith Jones, writing for Working Preacher, observes that what Jesus preaches here becomes the theme of his whole ministry.[^s11]

## Context

Luke has just told of Jesus's baptism, his genealogy and his testing in the wilderness. Jesus then returns to Galilee "in the power of the Spirit" and teaches in its synagogues, praised by everyone (4:14-15).[^s1][^s11] The IVP New Testament Commentary sketches the shape of a synagogue Sabbath service, drawing on the Mishnah: the Shema and prayers, a reading from the Law, a reading from the Prophets, then instruction and a blessing. Jesus speaks at the point of the Prophets reading.[^s8]

Mark tells of a hometown visit in which Jesus teaches in the synagogue, the people take offence ("Isn't this the carpenter?"), and Jesus says a prophet is not without honour except in his own country (Mark 6:1-6).[^s4] Luke's scene shares that saying but sets it at the very start of the ministry. Jesus even anticipates a demand to repeat his Capernaum works (Luke 4:23), though Luke narrates Capernaum only afterwards (4:31-32).[^s1]

## Walking through the text

**The reading (4:16-20).** The scroll of Isaiah is handed to Jesus; he finds the place and reads: "The Spirit of the Lord is on me, because he has anointed me to preach good news to the poor." He is sent to heal the broken-hearted, "to proclaim release to the captives, recovering of sight to the blind, to deliver those who are crushed, and to proclaim the acceptable year of the Lord" (4:18-19, WEB).[^s1] The words come from Isaiah 61:1-2, with a line, "to let the oppressed go free", drawn from Isaiah 58:6, where God describes the fast he truly wants.[^s2][^s3][^s11] Enter the Bible suggests the two texts were joined because the same Greek word for release (*aphesis*) appears in both.[^s9] The Mishnah, a later rabbinic collection, allows a reader to skip from place to place in the Prophets, though not in the Torah.[^s12] Jesus stops in the middle of Isaiah 61:2, before "the day of vengeance of our God".[^s1][^s2] He rolls up the scroll, hands it to the attendant and sits down, and every eye in the synagogue is fixed on him (4:20).[^s1]

**The claim (4:21-22).** "Today" is the key word. The IVP commentary hears it as a moment of decision: Jesus is not only explaining the promise but presenting himself as its fulfilment.[^s8] The hearers wonder at his gracious words, yet ask, "Isn't this Joseph's son?" (4:22).[^s1]

**The challenge (4:23-27).** Jesus anticipates the proverb "Physician, heal yourself!" and their wish to see in their own town what was done at Capernaum. He says that "no prophet is acceptable in his hometown" (4:24, WEB) and reminds them of two stories from Kings: in a long famine Elijah was sent to a widow of Zarephath in the land of Sidon, and of the many lepers in Elisha's day only "Naaman, the Syrian" was cleansed (4:25-27, WEB).[^s1][^s5][^s6]

**The rejection (4:28-30).** The whole synagogue is filled with rage. They drive Jesus to the brow of the hill to throw him down, but he passes through the middle of them and goes his way.[^s1]

## Interpretation

"The acceptable year of the Lord" echoes the jubilee of Leviticus 25, when every fiftieth year liberty was proclaimed throughout the land and each family returned to its own property (Leviticus 25:10, KJV).[^s7] The IVP commentary pictures the jubilee as a year when debts were cancelled and slaves set free, an image of what Jesus brings; Enter the Bible and Working Preacher add that this release reaches social and economic bondage as well as sin.[^s8][^s9][^s11] Moreno Sanz highlights three keys: the good news of the Kingdom, the forgiveness and commitment that flow from the jubilee Jesus proclaims, and hope fulfilled in salvation's "today".[^s10]

Why the fury? The commentaries agree that it is Elijah and Elisha that turn the room. Jesus points out that God's prophets once brought blessing to a Sidonian widow and a Syrian general, which implies that Nazareth should expect no special privilege. Their attempt to kill him then proves his words about prophets and their hometowns.[^s8][^s9][^s11] Careful readers resist turning this into a story of "the Jews" rejecting Jesus. Everyone in it is Jewish, Jesus included, and he is worshipping in his own synagogue and reading Israel's Scriptures. The warning concerns any community, the church included, that wants God's salvation to serve its own purposes.[^s1][^s11]

## Connections

The passage is **part of** the Gospel of Luke, opening its account of Jesus's public ministry.[^s1][^s9] It **features** Jesus, the synagogue at Nazareth where the scene takes place, and the town of Nazareth itself.[^s1] It **quotes** the Book of Isaiah (61:1-2 and 58:6) and **alludes to** the jubilee law of Leviticus 25.[^s2][^s3][^s7][^s9] Through Jesus's examples it **alludes to** 1 Kings (Elijah and the widow of Zarephath) and 2 Kings (Elisha and Naaman).[^s1][^s5][^s6] It **teaches** the kingdom of God, whose good news it announces, and the work of the Holy Spirit, who anoints Jesus for his mission.[^s1][^s10]
