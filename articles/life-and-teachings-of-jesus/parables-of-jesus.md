# Parables of Jesus

A parable is a short comparison or story from ordinary life (sowing seed, losing a coin, a family quarrel) that points beyond itself to the ways of God. The Greek *parabolē* means something "thrown alongside" for comparison; it renders the Hebrew *mashal*, a broad word that also covers proverbs, riddles and fables.[^s7][^s8] One scholar defines the Gospel parable as "a figurative narrative that is true to life."[^s10] Parables appear in Matthew, Mark and Luke; the *Catholic Encyclopedia* counts about thirty-three (up to sixty with short comparisons) and finds none in John in the strict sense.[^s7]

They were central to Jesus's teaching. Mark says he taught "many things in parables" and that "without a parable he didn't speak to them" (Mark 4:2, 4:34, WEB); Matthew presents this as fulfilling a prophetic word, "I will open my mouth in parables" (Matthew 13:34-35, WEB).[^s1][^s2]

## Why it matters

Most parables are about the Kingdom of God. "How will we liken God's Kingdom?" Jesus asks, and answers with a mustard seed that becomes a shelter for birds (Mark 4:30-32, WEB); elsewhere the Kingdom is a hidden treasure or a pearl for which a man sells everything (Matthew 13:44-46).[^s1][^s2][^s10] Reading the parables well means hearing what Jesus said about God's reign, and being asked, like his first hearers, how we will respond.

They also show how Jesus taught. He spoke "as they were able to hear it" and explained everything privately to his disciples (Mark 4:33-34, WEB).[^s1] Asked why he uses parables, he says the mysteries of the Kingdom are given to the disciples, and applies Isaiah's words about hearing without understanding to those who refuse his message (Matthew 13:10-17; Mark 4:10-12).[^s1][^s2] A parable both reveals and conceals: it opens to those who come closer and ask, and leaves the indifferent with only a story.[^s7]

## Roots in the Old Testament and Jewish teaching

Jesus did not invent the form. In Nathan's story a rich man takes a poor man's only lamb; David declares that the man deserves to die, and Nathan replies, "You are the man!" (2 Samuel 12:1-7, WEB).[^s4][^s8] Isaiah sings of a vineyard that yields worthless fruit, then reveals that "the vineyard of Yahweh of Armies is the house of Israel" (Isaiah 5:1-7, WEB).[^s5][^s8] Jesus's parable of the vineyard tenants echoes that song, with its tower and winepress, and his hearers "perceived that he spoke the parable against them" (Mark 12:1-12, WEB).[^s5][^s6] Like Nathan, Jesus lets listeners judge a story before they recognise themselves in it.

Later Jewish teachers also taught through parables, often about a king and his household, to explain the Law and God's bond with Israel; the *Jewish Encyclopedia* notes close parallels to Gospel parables such as the banquet.[^s8] Jesus taught within this shared Jewish tradition.[^s8]

## How to read it

- **Find the setting and audience.** Luke opens the parables of the lost sheep, coin and son by noting that tax collectors and sinners drew near while Pharisees and scribes murmured, "This man welcomes sinners, and eats with them" (Luke 15:1-3, WEB).[^s3] The parables answer that complaint.[^s3]
- **Study the structure and details.** Notice the main characters, how the story is built and where it ends, as with the four soils in the parable of the sower (Mark 4:3-8).[^s1][^s10]
- **Ask for the central truth.** Bailey compares a parable to a wheel: the main point is the hub, and the details are spokes that serve it.[^s10]
- **Notice when Jesus interprets.** He explains the weeds among the wheat himself: the sower is the Son of Man, the field the world, the harvest the end of the age (Matthew 13:36-43).[^s2] Such explanations guide us without licensing us to decode every detail of every parable.[^s7][^s10]
- **Hear the call to respond.** The parables challenged their hearers rather than offering general maxims.[^s9]

## Common mistakes

- **Turning every detail into a code.** For centuries the Good Samaritan was read with the Samaritan as Christ and the inn as the Church.[^s9] Such readings can nourish devotion, but they can make a parable say anything; Tertullian warned that heretics bend parables "whither they will."[^s7]
- **Reducing a parable to a moral.** Treating parables as timeless lessons in niceness loses their urgency about the Kingdom.[^s9]
- **Building doctrine on a detail.** Doctrine should rest on clearer texts, with parables illustrating it.[^s7]
- **Making Jesus's opponents stock villains.** The elder brother is begged to come in, and Luke never says how he answered (Luke 15:28-32), leaving the story open for the listening Pharisees and scribes.[^s3]

## Scholarly views

The main debate is how much a parable's details mean. Origen and the Alexandrians read them allegorically and Christologically; Antiochenes such as John Chrysostom preferred sober exposition, and Augustine allowed that not every detail carries meaning.[^s7][^s9] Luther and Calvin rejected allegory as a general method, though Luther still allegorised.[^s9] In 1888 Adolf Jülicher argued that each parable makes a single point; C. H. Dodd and Joachim Jeremias then set the parables in Jesus's ministry and stressed the Kingdom's coming, and later scholars studied each evangelist's shaping or read the parables as open-ended literature.[^s9]

Stein faults Jülicher for denying Jesus any allegorical details and for flattening the parables into moral teaching, and ties meaning to the intention of Jesus and the evangelists.[^s9] Craig Blomberg proposes a "controlled" allegory with one point per main character, while Bailey prefers one central truth with subordinate points.[^s10]

## Connections

The parables belong to **the Gospels** and are **explained by the Kingdom of God**, the subject most of them illustrate (Mark 4:26-32).[^s1][^s7] They **contrast with allegory**: a parable draws on real life and is interpreted from outside the story, while an allegory carries its meaning in its invented figures.[^s7] They are **explained by the parables of the Hebrew Bible**, such as Nathan's and Isaiah's, and by **rabbinic Judaism**, whose teachers used the same form.[^s4][^s5][^s8] **The Good Samaritan** and **the Prodigal Son** are narrower articles, and **genre in the Bible** gives the wider habit of reading each kind of writing on its own terms.[^s3][^s10]
