# The Sadducees' Question about the Resurrection

In the last days before his death, while Jesus was teaching in the Temple courts, a group of Sadducees brought him a puzzle meant to make the resurrection of the dead look absurd. Matthew, Mark and Luke all tell the story (Mark 12:18-27; Matthew 22:23-33; Luke 20:27-40).[^s1][^s2][^s3] Jesus's reply is short, but it contains one of his clearest teachings on what lies beyond death, and one of the few places where we watch him argue a doctrine straight from the Books of Moses.[^s12][^s15]

## Why it matters

The passage shows how Jesus read the Old Testament: not only for its commands but for what it reveals about who God is.[^s12] It grounds the Christian hope of resurrection in God's own faithfulness, since he names himself the God of people who had long since died.[^s15] The Catechism of the Catholic Church, for example, draws on these very verses when it explains that Jesus taught the resurrection firmly.[^s16]

## Context

Mark places the scene in a run of encounters in the Temple: Jesus is questioned about his authority (Mark 11:27-33), about paying taxes to Caesar (Mark 12:13-17), about the resurrection, and about the greatest commandment, before he asks his own question about David's son (Mark 12:35-37).[^s4][^s1] Sloan treats Mark 11-13 as a single unit.[^s12]

The questioners matter. Mark introduces them as people "who say that there is no resurrection" (Mark 12:18, WEB), and Acts adds that they denied angels and spirits as well (Acts 23:8).[^s1][^s11] Their riddle rests on the law of levirate marriage: when a man died without an heir, his brother was to marry the widow so that the firstborn would carry on the dead man's name in Israel (Deuteronomy 25:5-10).[^s6][^s13] The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that the Hebrew speaks of a man with no "son," while the Septuagint, and the Sadducees in Mark 12:19, speak of "children."[^s13][^s1] A story with the same shape appears in the deuterocanonical Book of Tobit, where young Sarah has lost seven husbands (Tobit 3:8).[^s7] Tobit is in the Catholic and Orthodox Old Testaments; Protestant Bibles treat it as Apocrypha, and Judaism does not count it as Scripture.[^s17]

## Walking through the text

**The riddle (Mark 12:19-23).** Seven brothers in turn marry the same woman, each dies childless, and at last she dies too. "In the resurrection, when they rise, whose wife will she be of them?" (Mark 12:23, WEB).[^s1] Chrysostom thought the case invented, with seven brothers chosen to make resurrection look ridiculous.[^s14]

**The diagnosis (Mark 12:24).** Jesus says they are wrong because they know neither the Scriptures nor the power of God.[^s1] His answer then takes up both failures in turn.[^s15]

**The power of God (Mark 12:25).** When the dead rise, "they neither marry nor are given in marriage, but are like angels in heaven" (WEB).[^s1] The Sadducees assumed the age to come would simply continue this one. Luke draws the contrast sharply: "the children of this age" marry, but those counted worthy of "that age" cannot die any more and are "children of God, being children of the resurrection" (Luke 20:34-36, WEB).[^s3] Marriage, which in the levirate law kept a family name alive against death, is not needed where death no longer reigns.[^s3][^s6]

**The Scriptures (Mark 12:26-27).** Jesus then turns to "the book of Moses about the Bush," the very part of Scripture his questioners prized most.[^s1][^s14] There God tells Moses, "I am the God of your father, the God of Abraham, the God of Isaac, and the God of Jacob" (Exodus 3:6, WEB).[^s5] "He is not the God of the dead, but of the living," Jesus concludes; Luke adds, "for all are alive to him" (Luke 20:38, WEB).[^s1][^s3]

**The response.** Matthew says the crowds were astonished, Luke that some scribes said "Teacher, you speak well," and no one dared ask any more questions (Matthew 22:33; Luke 20:39-40).[^s2][^s3]

## Interpretation

How does Exodus 3:6 prove the resurrection? Interpreters have answered in several ways. Chrysostom stressed the wording: God said "I am," not "I was," so the patriarchs still live in the promise of resurrection.[^s14] Others, as Sloan's survey records, rest the argument on the present tense or on later revelation adding a meaning Moses did not know; Sloan himself argues that Jesus's reading does not go beyond what the Old Testament text says.[^s12] A common reading today focuses on relationship: God bound himself by covenant to Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, and death cannot cancel that bond, so the life they have with God will be completed in the resurrection.[^s15] An ancient Jewish writing, 4 Maccabees, uses similar language, saying the faithful, like Abraham, Isaac and Jacob, "live to God" (4 Maccabees 7:19, WEB).[^s8]

Two misreadings are common. First, Jesus does not say that people become angels; he says the risen are *like* angels in not marrying, and Luke ties the likeness to no longer dying.[^s1][^s3] Chrysostom read it the same way: they do not marry because they are like angels.[^s14] Second, the passage is not a dismissal of marriage; it answers whether earthly arrangements carry over into the age to come, and its focus is the living God, not the details of heavenly life.[^s1][^s15]

The hope Jesus defends was already voiced in Daniel 12:2, where many who sleep in the dust of the earth awake, "some to everlasting life" (WEB).[^s9] After Easter, Paul takes up the question the Sadducees mocked, "With what kind of body do they come?", and answers that what is sown perishable is raised imperishable (1 Corinthians 15:35-44).[^s10]

## Connections

This scene is **part of** the Gospels of Mark, Matthew and Luke, which all record it.[^s1][^s2][^s3] It **features** Jesus and the Sadducees, the Temple party that denied the resurrection.[^s1][^s11] It **teaches** the resurrection of the dead, as the Catholic Catechism's use of it shows.[^s16] It **quotes** Exodus, God's self-naming at the burning bush (Exodus 3:6), and **alludes to** Deuteronomy's levirate law (Deuteronomy 25:5-6).[^s5][^s6] Its riddle is **explained by** the practice of levirate marriage.[^s13] It belongs beside Paul's preaching of the resurrection at the Areopagus and his teaching in 1 Corinthians 15.[^s10]
