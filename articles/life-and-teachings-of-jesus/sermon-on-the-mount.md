# Sermon on the Mount

The Sermon on the Mount is the name Christians give to Matthew 5–7, the longest stretch of Jesus's teaching in the Gospels. It opens as Jesus sees the crowds, goes up "onto the mountain" and sits down to teach his disciples (Matthew 5:1, WEB), and it closes with the crowds "astonished at his teaching" because he taught "with authority, and not like the scribes" (Matthew 7:28-29, WEB).[^s1][^s3] The title is not in the Bible: Augustine used it for his Latin commentary around AD 400, and English usage goes back to a notation in the Coverdale Bible of 1535.[^s5][^s6]

## Why it matters

Few passages have shaped Christian life as deeply. It contains the Beatitudes, the Lord's Prayer and the Golden Rule, and Augustine, opening his commentary, told readers they would find in it a perfect standard of the Christian life.[^s6] It also gathers up the question that runs through the whole Bible — what it means to belong to God's people — and answers it from the heart outward. Christians of every tradition treasure it, even where they differ on how its hardest commands should be lived.[^s5][^s7]

## Context

Matthew sets the scene carefully. Jesus has begun preaching "Repent! For the Kingdom of Heaven is at hand" (Matthew 4:17, WEB) and has gone through Galilee "preaching the Good News of the Kingdom" and healing the sick, so that great crowds follow him from Galilee, the Decapolis, Jerusalem, Judea and beyond the Jordan (4:23-25).[^s11] When the sermon ends, Jesus comes down the mountain and the crowds follow him again (8:1).[^s13] The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* suggests that the "gospel of the kingdom" announced in 4:23 is the thread that ties the sermon together, and notes that here Jesus appears above all as a teacher.[^s5]

Luke has a shorter, closely related discourse, often called the Sermon on the Plain, where Jesus "stood on a level place" with his disciples and a great crowd (Luke 6:17, WEB). It begins with blessings and woes, includes love of enemies and the Golden Rule, and also ends with two builders (Luke 6:20-49).[^s4] Luke's version has about thirty verses to Matthew's 107, and nearly all of Luke's have a parallel in Matthew.[^s5]

## Walking through the text

**The Beatitudes and salt and light (5:1-16).** Jesus begins by pronouncing God's blessing on unlikely people: "Blessed are the poor in spirit, for theirs is the Kingdom of Heaven" (5:3, WEB), the mourners, the meek, the merciful and "the peacemakers" (5:9). He then tells his disciples they are "the salt of the earth" and "the light of the world" (5:13-14).[^s1]

**Jesus and the Law (5:17-48).** "I didn't come to destroy, but to fulfill" (5:17, WEB), Jesus says, and he calls for a righteousness that exceeds that of the scribes and Pharisees (5:20).[^s1] Again and again he recalls what "was said" and adds "but I tell you". He takes up commandments from the Decalogue — "You shall not murder" and "You shall not commit adultery" (Exodus 20:13-14) — and traces them back to anger and lustful looking (5:21-28).[^s1][^s12] He speaks about divorce, oaths, retaliation and love of enemies, and ends: "you shall be perfect, just as your Father in heaven is perfect" (5:48, WEB).[^s1]

**Worship before the Father (6:1-18).** Giving, prayer and fasting are to be done not "to be seen" but before the Father "who sees in secret" (6:1, 6:6, WEB). At the centre stands the Lord's Prayer, "Our Father in heaven, may your name be kept holy" (6:9, WEB).[^s2]

**Treasure and trust (6:19-34).** "Where your treasure is, there your heart will be also" (6:21, WEB); no one can serve both God and money; disciples are not to be anxious but to "seek first God's Kingdom and his righteousness" (6:33, WEB).[^s2]

**Judging, asking and the two ways (7:1-29).** Jesus warns against judging others while ignoring "the beam" in one's own eye (7:1-5), invites prayer ("Ask, and it will be given you", 7:7), and sums up the Law and the Prophets in the Golden Rule (7:12). He closes with two gates, two kinds of fruit, and two builders: the one who "hears these words of mine and does them" builds on rock (7:24, WEB).[^s3]

## Interpretation

It matters that the sermon begins with blessing before it gives any command — God's Kingdom belongs to the poor in spirit — and ends with a call to build one's life on Jesus's own words.[^s1][^s3][^s5] The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* reads Jesus's "but I tell you" as deepening the Law rather than setting it aside, and observes that Jesus himself embodied the sermon.[^s5]

Two common misreadings deserve care. First, "Don't judge" (7:1) is often quoted to rule out any moral discernment, yet the same chapter asks disciples to recognise false prophets "by their fruits" (7:15-16); the warning is against hypocritical faultfinding.[^s3][^s5] Second, the sermon's vivid sayings are proverbial: the encyclopedia notes that the principle beneath a saying matters more than a wooden literal reading.[^s5] Readers should also remember that Jesus affirms Israel's Law and Prophets here (5:17-19); the sermon is not an attack on Judaism or its Scriptures.[^s1][^s5]

## Views across traditions

**Catholic.** The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* calls the sermon the chief expression of the "New Law" or Law of the Gospel: the Holy Spirit writes it on the heart, and the sacraments give the grace to live it. It does not abolish the moral commands of the Old Law but reforms the heart, the root of human acts (CCC 1965–1968). The Catechism also distinguishes precepts, binding on all, from evangelical counsels, which each person follows according to his or her vocation (CCC 1973–1974).[^s7]

**Orthodox.** In most Orthodox churches the Beatitudes (Matthew 5:3-12) are sung on ordinary Sundays as the third antiphon of the Divine Liturgy, with the good thief's prayer "Remember us, O Lord" as a refrain, so that the sermon prepares worshippers to hear the Gospel and receive Communion.[^s10]

**Lutheran.** The Apology of the Augsburg Confession teaches that the Gospel does not create new civil laws but brings forgiveness and new life. It treats "resist not evil" (Matthew 5:39) as forbidding private revenge, while holding that Christians may serve as magistrates, soldiers or judges and may swear oaths when the authorities require them.[^s8]

**Anabaptist.** The Schleitheim Confession (1527) of the Swiss Brethren reads the sermon's commands as binding on the church as they stand: believers do not take up the sword, which belongs to rulers "outside the perfection of Christ", and they swear no oaths but let their yes be yes and their no be no.[^s9]

Each tradition reads the same chapters as Jesus's word to his disciples; they differ on how its commands relate to grace, vocation and public life.[^s7][^s8][^s9]

## Connections

The sermon is **part of** the Gospel of Matthew, opening its account of Jesus's teaching.[^s5] It **teaches** the kingdom of God, the "gospel of the kingdom" that frames it.[^s5] It **parallels** Luke's Sermon on the Plain, which shares much of its material.[^s4][^s5] It **quotes** the Ten Commandments when Jesus takes up the commands against murder and adultery.[^s1][^s12] It is closely related to the Beatitudes and the Lord's Prayer, which it contains, and to the Pharisees, whose righteousness Jesus says his disciples must exceed.[^s1][^s2]
