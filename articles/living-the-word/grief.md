# Grief

Grief is the sorrow that follows the death of someone we love, and the Bible is full of it. Jacob tears his clothes and refuses to be comforted when he thinks Joseph is dead (Genesis 37:34-35); David cries out for his son, "I wish I had died instead of you, Absalom" (2 Samuel 18:33, World English Bible); and at the tomb of his friend Lazarus, "Jesus wept" (John 11:35, WEB).[^s1][^s4][^s9] Scripture never tells mourners to hide their tears. It gives them company, words and hope.

## Why it matters

Many Christians quietly fear that grieving shows weak faith. New Testament scholar Jeffrey Weima tells of a widow who felt guilty for her tears, and argues that Christians are not only allowed to grieve but that their tears are a sign of love; what sets them apart is not fewer tears but grief held together with hope.[^s15] Augustine felt the same pull. At his mother Monica's funeral he forced back his tears, thinking lament unfitting for someone who had died in faith, and only later let himself weep before God, asking readers not to mock him for it.[^s16] The Bible frees believers from that false choice between faith and sorrow.

## What Scripture says

**Mourning is a normal, honoured part of life.** Ecclesiastes says there is "a time to weep" and "a time to mourn" (Ecclesiastes 3:4, WEB).[^s6] Israel mourned in visible, communal ways: tearing garments, wearing sackcloth, fasting, sitting silent with the bereaved, and keeping set periods of mourning.[^s13][^s14] When Jacob died, Joseph wept over his father's face, the Egyptians wept for seventy days, and the family held "a very great and severe lamentation," mourning seven days at the threshing floor of Atad (Genesis 50:1-3, 10, WEB).[^s2] The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that seven days of mourning seems to have been usual, and later Jewish practice kept a seven-day period of mourning at home in which neighbours bring the first meal and visitors come to comfort the family.[^s14] Christians can learn from this unhurried, shared way of mourning.

**Grief can be given words.** David and his men tore their clothes, wept and fasted until evening when they heard of Saul's and Jonathan's deaths, and David composed a lament for them both: "How the mighty have fallen!" (2 Samuel 1:11-12, 17-27, WEB).[^s3] He grieves Jonathan as "my brother" and honours Saul as well, calling the two of them "lovely and pleasant in their lives" (1:23, 26).[^s3] The Jewish Encyclopedia calls this lament one of the gems of Hebrew poetry.[^s14]

**God draws near to the brokenhearted.** "Yahweh is near to those who have a broken heart" (Psalm 34:18, WEB), and Jesus blesses "those who mourn, for they shall be comforted" (Matthew 5:4, WEB).[^s5][^s8]

**Jesus weeps.** In John 11, Martha and Mary each tell Jesus that their brother would not have died if he had been there (11:21, 32).[^s9] Jesus declares, "I am the resurrection and the life" (11:25), yet when he sees Mary and the mourners weeping he is deeply troubled and weeps, and the crowd says, "See how much affection he had for him!" (11:33-36, WEB).[^s9] Weima notes that the onlookers took his tears as love, not as a lack of faith.[^s15] Then Jesus calls Lazarus out of the tomb (11:43-44), a sign of the life he gives.[^s9]

**The church grieves together.** Paul tells believers to "weep with those who weep" (Romans 12:15, WEB).[^s10] Grief is meant to be carried in company, as Jacob's sons and daughters "rose up to comfort him" even when he could not yet accept comfort (Genesis 37:35).[^s1]

**Christians grieve with hope.** Paul writes so that the Thessalonians will not grieve "like the rest, who have no hope," because those who have "fallen asleep in Jesus" will rise when the Lord comes, and "we will be with the Lord forever" (1 Thessalonians 4:13-17, WEB).[^s11] He ends, "comfort one another with these words" (4:18).[^s11] The Bible's last picture is of God wiping away every tear, when death and mourning will be no more (Revelation 21:4).[^s12]

## Pitfalls

**Reading 1 Thessalonians 4:13 as "don't grieve."** Weima points out that the verse is often misread this way. Paul does not forbid grief; he contrasts grief with hope and grief without it.[^s15][^s11]

**Hurrying the mourner.** Scripture's mourners take days and weeks; Jacob mourned "many days" and refused to be comforted (Genesis 37:34-35).[^s1][^s2] Friends do better to sit, listen and help than to press for a quick recovery.[^s14]

**Using verses as a bandage.** A hope-filled verse offered too fast can sound like a denial that the loss is real. Jesus wept before he raised Lazarus.[^s9][^s15]

**Grieving alone.** Biblical mourning is shared. This article is not medical, psychological or crisis advice: if grief stays overwhelming, keeps you from daily life, or brings thoughts of harming yourself, talk with a pastor, a grief counsellor or a doctor, and in danger contact local emergency or crisis support right away.[^s10]

## Connections

- **Suffering and lament** (`broader`): grief is one of the most common forms of suffering Christians bring to Scripture, and lament is its prayer.[^s3]
- **Raising of Lazarus** (`supportedBy`): Jesus's tears at the tomb and his words to Martha are the heart of the New Testament's teaching on grief.[^s9]
- **David's lament for Saul and Jonathan** (`supportedBy`): a model of grief put into words, even for a complicated relationship.[^s3]
- **1 Thessalonians** (`supportedBy`): Paul's comfort to a grieving church, grief held together with hope.[^s11]
- **Resurrection of the dead** (`supportedBy`): the hope that, Weima argues, makes Christian grief different.[^s15]
- It also links to **Jesus**, **David**, the **Sermon on the Mount** (Matthew 5:4), **loving your neighbour** (Romans 12:15), the mixed joy and tears at the **laying of the foundation of the Second Temple**, the mourning practices of **rabbinic Judaism**, and the hope of the **Kingdom of God**.[^s8][^s10][^s14][^s12]
