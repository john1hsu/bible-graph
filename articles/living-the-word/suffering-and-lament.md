# Suffering and Lament

Sooner or later every Christian brings pain to Scripture: illness, loss, injustice, or a long season when God seems silent. The Bible does not hand the sufferer a neat explanation. What it gives, again and again, is a way to pray: lament, the honest cry of God's people that begins "How long, Yahweh? Will you forget me forever?" (Psalm 13:1, World English Bible).[^s1] Pope John Paul II called Scripture "a great book about suffering," pointing to psalms of abandonment such as Psalms 22 and 88.[^s17]

## Why it matters

Lament is one of the most common kinds of prayer in the Psalter.[^s13] Yet Walter Brueggemann argued that churches have largely let it fall silent, and that the loss is costly: without lament, believers are left with only praise and polite agreement, and the honest, two-way character of the covenant relationship is lost.[^s12] Lament teaches that bringing complaint *to* God is itself an act of faith, not a failure of it.

## What Scripture says

**The shape of a lament.** Psalm 13 shows the pattern in six verses: address and complaint ("How long?"), petition ("answer me... give light to my eyes"), and then a turn to trust ("But I trust in your loving kindness") and a vow to sing (Psalm 13:3-6, WEB).[^s1] Most laments end in praise, but that praise does not cancel the complaint; it grows out of it and sometimes comes before anything outward has changed.[^s13] Some laments do not turn at all. Psalm 88 cries "day and night," asks "why do you hide your face from me?", and closes with friends gone "into darkness" (Psalm 88:1, 14, 18, WEB).[^s3] Its place in Scripture tells sufferers that even prayer with no resolution is still prayer addressed to "the God of my salvation" (88:1).[^s3]

**Suffering is not always punishment.** Job's friends sit with him in silence for seven days, which is wise (Job 2:11-13), and then spend chapters insisting his suffering must be a penalty for sin.[^s5][^s14] At the end God says the friends have not spoken rightly of him "as my servant Job has," and Job prays for them (Job 42:7-10, WEB).[^s6] The Baker's dictionary notes that God never explains Job's suffering, and that Job's story overturns the idea that pain is always deserved; John Paul II likewise reads Job as rejecting the claim that every suffering follows a fault.[^s14][^s17] The prophet Habakkuk asks the same "how long?" about violence and injustice around him (Habakkuk 1:2-4).[^s7]

**A whole book of lament.** Lamentations is five poems mourning the fall of Jerusalem to Babylon in 587 BC, the first four of them alphabetic acrostics, written in the three-beat-then-two-beat "qinah" rhythm of lament and drawing on funeral dirges.[^s15] At its centre, a sufferer who has walked "in darkness, and not in light" remembers that God's "mercies don't fail. They are new every morning" (Lamentations 3:2, 22-23, WEB), and that "the Lord will not cast off forever" (3:31).[^s4] Jewish communities still read Lamentations on the Ninth of Av, the fast that mourns the destruction of the First and Second Temples, and churches use chapter 3 in Holy Week.[^s16][^s15]

**Jesus laments.** On the cross Jesus cries in the words of Psalm 22:1, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Matthew 27:46, WEB).[^s8] Psalm 22 moves from that cry to praise in the assembly, confessing that God "has not despised nor abhorred the affliction of the afflicted" (Psalm 22:24, WEB).[^s2] John Paul II sees in this cry the depth of the Son's suffering, borne in union with the Father.[^s17]

**Hope in the groaning.** Paul says creation "groans" and that believers "groan within ourselves" while they wait, and that when we do not know how to pray, the Spirit intercedes "with groanings which can't be uttered" (Romans 8:22-23, 26, WEB).[^s9] He blesses "the God of all comfort" who comforts us so that we can comfort others, and admits he once "despaired even of life" (2 Corinthians 1:3-4, 8, WEB).[^s10] The Bible's last word is not lament: God will dwell with his people and "wipe away from them every tear" (Revelation 21:3-4, WEB).[^s11][^s14]

## Pitfalls

**Rushing past the pain.** Quoting Romans 8:28 ("all things work together for good") at a funeral can sound like denying that the loss is real. Paul says it in a chapter full of groaning, after admitting that present sufferings are real and waiting is hard (Romans 8:18-26).[^s9] Job's friends were best when they were silent.[^s5]

**Assuming every sorrow is a punishment.** Scripture sometimes links suffering to sin, but the book of Job and Jesus's own suffering show that the innocent suffer too.[^s14][^s6]

**Thinking complaint is unfaithful.** God rebuked Job's tidy-minded friends, not the man who argued with him.[^s6] Lament speaks *to* God and holds him to his promises.[^s13][^s12]

**Lamenting alone.** Lamentations was probably used by survivors to mourn together, and the Good Samaritan, John Paul II writes, is the one who stops beside the sufferer instead of passing by.[^s15][^s17] This article is not medical, psychological or crisis advice: if sorrow, despair or anxiety persists or overwhelms you, talk with a pastor, a counsellor or a doctor, and if you are in danger or thinking of harming yourself, contact local emergency or crisis support now.

## Connections

- **Psalm 22** (`supportedBy`): the lament Jesus prayed on the cross, moving from abandonment to praise.[^s2][^s8]
- **Book of Lamentations** (`supportedBy`): an entire book of communal lament over Jerusalem's fall.[^s15]
- **Book of Job** (`supportedBy`): the Bible's longest wrestling with innocent suffering, ending with God vindicating Job's honest speech.[^s6]
- **Grief** (`complements`): lament is the biblical language for mourning; Lamentations draws on funeral dirges.[^s15]
- **Hebrew poetry** (`explainedBy`): acrostics and the qinah rhythm show how carefully crafted Israel's laments are.[^s15]
- It also connects to the **crucifixion of Jesus**, the **Babylonian exile**, the **Book of Jeremiah**, to whom Lamentations is traditionally ascribed, and the hope of the **Kingdom of God**.[^s15][^s8]
