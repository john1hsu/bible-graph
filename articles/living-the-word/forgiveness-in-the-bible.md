# Forgiveness in the Bible

Christians bring two needs to Scripture here: to be forgiven, and to forgive someone else. The Bible ties them tightly together. It begins with who God is, a God who forgives, and it ends with a community of forgiven people learning to forgive one another "just as God also in Christ forgave you" (Ephesians 4:32, World English Bible).[^s8]

## Why it matters

Every time Christians pray the Lord's Prayer they ask God to "forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors" (Matthew 6:12, WEB), and Jesus immediately underlines that clause above all the others (Matthew 6:14-15).[^s2] The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* notes that this is the only petition Jesus returns to and develops, and Luther's Small Catechism likewise pairs asking God's pardon with a promise to forgive and do good to those who wrong us.[^s14][^s15]

## What Scripture says

**God forgives.** When God passes before Moses on Sinai, he proclaims himself "merciful and gracious... slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness," one who goes on "forgiving iniquity and disobedience and sin," yet does not simply wave away guilt (Exodus 34:6-7, WEB); Moses at once asks God to pardon the people (34:9).[^s6] Psalm 103 sings of the same God, who has not repaid us according to our sins and has removed our transgressions "as far as the east is from the west" (Psalm 103:10-12, WEB).[^s7] Jewish tradition likewise treats forgiveness as an attribute of God, received through genuine repentance and the intention not to repeat the wrong.[^s13]

**How Israel received it.** Under the law of Moses, sacrifices dealt with many unintentional sins, and the yearly Day of Atonement dealt with the nation's sin as a whole; the prophets Jeremiah and Ezekiel looked ahead to a new covenant in which God would forgive and transform his people.[^s12] The New Testament presents Jesus as the one who brings that promise to fulfilment: he forgives sins in his own ministry and speaks of his coming death as given for many.[^s12]

**Forgiving one another.** The law already forbade hating a brother in one's heart, taking vengeance or bearing a grudge, joining this to the command to love one's neighbour as oneself (Leviticus 19:17-18).[^s11] Joseph lived it out: when his brothers feared revenge after Jacob's death, he wept, refused to put himself "in the place of God," named the evil they had intended, and promised to provide for them and their children (Genesis 50:15-21).[^s5] Paul tells believers to repay no one evil for evil, to leave vengeance to God, and to overcome evil with good (Romans 12:17-21), and to put away bitterness and forgive as they were forgiven (Ephesians 4:31-32; Colossians 3:12-13).[^s10][^s8][^s9]

**Jesus's teaching.** When Peter asks whether forgiving seven times is enough, Jesus answers "until seventy times seven" and tells the parable of a servant forgiven an unpayable debt of ten thousand talents who then throttles a fellow servant over a hundred denarii (Matthew 18:21-35).[^s1] The point is the proportion: those forgiven so much cannot refuse mercy over so little; the parable closes with a call to forgive "from your hearts" (18:35).[^s1] In Luke, Jesus says to rebuke a brother who sins and to forgive him when he repents, even seven times in a day (Luke 17:3-4).[^s3] On the cross he prays, "Father, forgive them, for they don't know what they are doing" (Luke 23:34, WEB).[^s4]

**Forgiveness happens in the heart.** The Catholic Catechism observes that we cannot simply stop feeling or remembering an offence, but a heart open to the Holy Spirit can turn hurt into compassion and prayer for the one who caused it, so forgiveness often begins as a decision and a prayer that the heart grows into.[^s14] Luther's catechism grounds the same practice in grace: we ask God for what we do not deserve, and so we in turn forgive and do good to those who wrong us.[^s15]

## Pitfalls

**"Forgive and forget."** Scripture does not ask Christians to pretend that harm did not happen. Joseph named the evil done to him, and Jesus's teaching in Matthew 18 begins with going to the one who sinned and showing him his fault (Matthew 18:15-17).[^s5][^s1] Elaine Ramshaw argues that lifting "seventy times seven" out of its chapter has sometimes been used to press injured people, including people suffering abuse, to restore relationships on the wrongdoer's terms; read in context, Matthew 18 is concerned to protect the vulnerable "little ones."[^s16]

**Forgiveness is not the same as reconciliation.** Forgiving releases the debt and the desire for revenge; reconciliation is the restoration of a relationship, which also involves the other person's repentance. Luke 17:3-4 links forgiveness with repentance, and Ramshaw warns against making forgiveness mean ignoring harm or restoring a relationship on the wrongdoer's terms.[^s3][^s16] Someone who is being harmed should seek safety and help, and if anger, grief or memories keep overwhelming you, talk with a pastor, a counsellor or a doctor; forgiveness never requires staying in harm's way.[^s16]

**Treating God's forgiveness as earned.** The servant in the parable is released because the king had compassion, not because he could repay (Matthew 18:27); our forgiving others responds to grace and does not purchase it.[^s1][^s15]

## Connections

- **Lord's Prayer** (`supportedBy`): the petition "forgive us our debts, as we also forgive our debtors" and Jesus's comment on it in Matthew 6:14-15 are the clearest everyday anchor for this teaching.[^s2]
- **Parable of the unforgiving servant** (`supportedBy`): Matthew 18:21-35 is Jesus's fullest story about forgiving others because we have been forgiven.[^s1]
- **Loving your neighbour** (`complements`): Leviticus 19:17-18 sets the refusal of grudges and vengeance alongside the command to love one's neighbour as oneself.[^s11]
- **Reconciliation** (`confusedWith`): forgiveness and the restoring of a relationship are often run together, but Scripture and careful readers distinguish them.[^s16]
- **Day of Atonement** (`explainedBy`): Israel's sacrifices and yearly day of atonement form the background for how the New Testament speaks of God's forgiveness through Christ.[^s12]
- It also touches the **Sermon on the Mount**, **grace**, the **atonement** and the **Kingdom of God**, which the unforgiving servant parable illustrates (Matthew 18:23).[^s1]
