# Loving Your Neighbor

Most of the Christian life happens among other people: family, coworkers, church members, strangers, even those who have wronged us. Scripture's word for all of them is "neighbor," and its instruction is short enough to memorise: "you shall love your neighbor as yourself" (Leviticus 19:18, World English Bible).[^s1] The command begins in the law of Moses, stands at the centre of Jesus's teaching, and runs through the letters of the apostles.[^s13]

## Why it matters

When a scribe asked Jesus which commandment was first of all, Jesus answered with two: to love God with all one's heart, soul, mind and strength (quoting Deuteronomy 6:4-5), and to love one's neighbor as oneself (Mark 12:28-31).[^s3][^s11] In Matthew he adds that the whole law and the prophets "depend on these two commandments" (Matthew 22:40, WEB).[^s4] Christians across traditions have kept the two together. The Westminster Shorter Catechism sums up the Ten Commandments as love for God and for neighbor, and the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* asks believers to regard every neighbor, without exception, as "another self."[^s17][^s16]

## What Scripture says

**Love is concrete.** Leviticus 19:18 closes a run of practical laws: leave the edges of the harvest for the poor and the sojourner, do not steal or lie, pay a hired worker before nightfall, do not curse the deaf or trip the blind, judge without favouring rich or poor, do not slander, and do not nurse hatred or a grudge (Leviticus 19:9-18).[^s1] Love of neighbor, in other words, is spelled out as fair wages, honest speech and care for the vulnerable before it is ever a feeling.[^s13]

**Love reaches the outsider.** The same chapter tells Israel to love the foreigner living among them "as yourself," because Israel had been foreigners in Egypt (Leviticus 19:33-34), and Deuteronomy grounds the same command in God himself, who loves the foreigner and gives him food and clothing (Deuteronomy 10:17-19).[^s1][^s10] Scot McKnight notes that "neighbor" in Leviticus 19:18 first meant a fellow member of the covenant people, while these laws already pressed Israel's compassion beyond its own.[^s12] Jewish teachers of the period also treated the command as central: Hillel summed up the Torah as not doing to your fellow what is hateful to you, and the *Jewish Encyclopedia* argues that the context of Leviticus 19 shows "neighbor" embraces the stranger too.[^s14]

**Jesus widens the circle.** When a lawyer, having quoted both great commandments, asked "Who is my neighbor?" Jesus told of a man beaten on the Jericho road, passed by a priest and a Levite and rescued by a Samaritan who bandaged him, carried him to an inn and paid for his care (Luke 10:25-35).[^s2] Jesus then asked which of the three proved to be a neighbor, and told the lawyer to "go and do likewise" (Luke 10:36-37, WEB).[^s2] The question shifts from "whom must I love?" to "whose neighbor will I become?"[^s13] Augustine drew the same lesson: our neighbor is anyone we have a duty to help in need, and no one, not even an enemy, is excluded.[^s15] In the Sermon on the Mount Jesus goes further still: love your enemies and pray for those who persecute you, because the Father sends sun and rain on the just and the unjust (Matthew 5:43-48).[^s5]

**The apostles call it the fulfilment of the law.** Paul writes that every commandment is "summed up" in loving one's neighbor, for love does no harm to a neighbor (Romans 13:8-10), and that Christian freedom is meant for serving one another in love (Galatians 5:13-14).[^s6][^s7] James calls the command "the royal law" and warns that favouring the rich over the poor breaks it (James 2:1-9).[^s8] John puts it bluntly: anyone who claims to love God while hating a brother is a liar, since we cannot love the God we have not seen while refusing the brother we have seen (1 John 4:19-21).[^s9]

## Pitfalls

**Shrinking the circle.** The lawyer's question was an attempt to find the boundary of his duty, and Jesus refused to draw one. McKnight observes that loyalty to one's own people becomes wrong when it turns into neglect of outsiders in need.[^s12] Churches can do the same by caring for members while overlooking the newcomer or the poor.[^s8]

**Reading "hate your enemy" into the Old Testament.** Jesus quotes the saying "love your neighbor and hate your enemy" (Matthew 5:43), but Leviticus 19:18 itself contains no command to hate; the ISBE notes that the second half was an inference some drew from the first.[^s13][^s1]

**Turning the parable into a caricature.** The story does not teach that priests, Levites or Jewish teachers were heartless as a class. In Mark the scribe who asks about the greatest commandment agrees warmly with Jesus and is told he is not far from the Kingdom of God (Mark 12:32-34), and Hillel's summary shows Jewish teachers prizing the same command.[^s3][^s14]

**Love as sentiment only.** James asks what good it is to wish a hungry brother well without giving him what he needs (James 2:15-16), and Leviticus pairs love with honest rebuke rather than silent resentment (Leviticus 19:17).[^s8][^s1] Love does not mean ignoring harm or staying in a situation where someone is hurting you; when a relationship is unsafe or conflict keeps overwhelming you, seek out a pastor or a counsellor, and in danger, contact emergency or crisis support.

## Connections

- **The Great Commandment** (`supportedBy`): Matthew 22:34-40 and Mark 12:28-34 set love of neighbor beside love of God as the summary of the law.[^s4][^s3]
- **Parable of the Good Samaritan** (`supportedBy`): Luke 10:25-37 is Jesus's own answer to "Who is my neighbor?"[^s2]
- **Sermon on the Mount** (`supportedBy`): Matthew 5:43-48 extends neighbor love to enemies.[^s5]
- **Second Temple Judaism** (`explainedBy`): Jewish teachers of the period, such as Hillel, also treated Leviticus 19:18 as a summary of the Torah, the background to the lawyer's and the scribe's questions.[^s14]
- It sits close to **forgiveness in the Bible**, since Leviticus 19:17-18 joins the refusal of grudges to this command.[^s1]
