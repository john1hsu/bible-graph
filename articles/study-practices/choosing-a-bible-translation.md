  - Read the translation's preface to learn its approach (more word-for-word or more meaning-based), which Hebrew and Greek texts it translates, and how it uses footnotes.

# Choosing a Bible Translation

Most Christians meet the Bible in translation. The Old Testament was written mainly in Hebrew, with a little Aramaic, and the New Testament in Greek, so a reader without those languages depends on the careful work of scholars who render them into English.[^s4] Choosing a translation is therefore not a matter of brand loyalty but a small act of stewardship: knowing what kind of Bible you are holding, so that you can hear what the text itself says.

Translating Scripture so that people understand it is older than any English Bible. When Ezra read the law to the people who had returned from exile, the Levites "gave the sense, so that they understood the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8, ASV).[^s1] At Pentecost the crowd in Jerusalem marvelled that each heard the apostles "in our own native language" (Acts 2:8, WEB).[^s2] And Paul told the Corinthians that in the assembly he would rather speak a few words with understanding than many in a language no one could follow (1 Corinthians 14:9-11, 19).[^s3]

## Why it matters

English readers have an unusual wealth of choice: dozens of complete versions exist, and new ones keep appearing.[^s4] That variety is a gift, but it also means two people in the same small group may read noticeably different wording. Knowing how translations are made helps a reader treat those differences as an aid to understanding rather than a cause for suspicion, and keeps faith from resting on the exact phrasing of one modern version.[^s4][^s5]

## How to apply it

**Know the spectrum.** Translations sit along a line. *Formal-equivalence* or "word-for-word" versions try to keep close to the original words and sentence patterns; Tyndale House's guide places the KJV, NASB, ESV and NRSV in this group. *Functional* or *dynamic-equivalence* versions aim to express the meaning in natural modern English, as the NIV, NET and NLT do, while the CSB aims at a balance between the two. *Paraphrases* such as The Message restate the main idea in contemporary idiom.[^s4] The ESV's own preface describes itself as "essentially literal" and admits that every translation is a trade-off between precision and readability.[^s6] An older Bible encyclopedia puts the same point simply: the ideal is neither too literal nor too free.[^s5]

**Remember that every version interprets.** No translation is purely word-for-word, because a single Hebrew or Greek word can carry several meanings and translators must choose; in practice the line between "literal" and "dynamic" is blurred, and a more meaning-based version is sometimes closer to the original at a given verse.[^s4] Read the preface to learn which approach a version takes.

**Ask what text and which books.** Modern translations work from critical editions of the original languages. The ESV, for example, translates the Masoretic Hebrew text as printed in *Biblia Hebraica Stuttgartensia* and the Greek New Testament of the United Bible Societies and Nestle-Aland, consulting the Dead Sea Scrolls, the Septuagint and other witnesses in difficult places, and its footnotes flag where manuscripts differ.[^s6] Manuscript discoveries since the King James Version of 1611, from Codex Sinaiticus to the Dead Sea Scrolls, have given translators older and better texts than were then available, which is one reason modern versions differ from the KJV in places.[^s5]

**Use your church's guidance.** For Catholics, the 1983 Code of Canon Law gives the Apostolic See and bishops' conferences authority to approve translations (canon 825), and the U.S. bishops list approved versions for private prayer and study, among them the New American Bible Revised Edition and Catholic editions of the NRSV.[^s7] The Orthodox Church counts among the genuine books of the Old Testament writings that some other Christians place in a secondary position or do not accept as inspired, so the books a Bible contains matter as well as its wording.[^s8] Protestant readers often follow the version their congregation reads aloud. These differences are best respected, not argued away.

**Have a main Bible and a second one.** Tyndale House's guide recommends choosing one translation you can stick with and get to know, while comparing versions with different approaches when you study, since each closes off some possible meanings; where they differ sharply, a commentary can show why.[^s4]

## Common mistakes

- **Treating a paraphrase as a study Bible.** Paraphrases can be fresh and vivid, but they can obscure what the original says and are not a good base for regular study.[^s4]
- **Assuming one English word equals one Hebrew or Greek word.** Because original words carry ranges of meaning, building an argument on the exact wording of a single version can mislead.[^s4]
- **Assuming the oldest English version is the most accurate.** Beauty and long use are real strengths of older versions, but translators now have access to older manuscripts than earlier translators did.[^s5]
- **Ignoring the footnotes and preface.** They tell you where the text is uncertain and how the translators decided.[^s6]
- **Choosing the wording that suits my point.** Comparing versions is meant to find what the text says, not to shop for support.[^s4]

## Connections

Comparing versions with different approaches is a natural part of the close observation practised in **inductive Bible study**, so the two work well together.[^s4] Seeing one original word rendered in several ways also guards, in a modest way, against **word-study fallacies** that assume each word has a single meaning.[^s4] The practice is explained by **textual criticism** and the **Masoretic Text**, since translations rest on critical editions of the Hebrew and Greek and note manuscript differences in their footnotes.[^s6] The **Catholic Church** gives it a distinctive, regulated form through its approval of translations.[^s7] Related articles on the **Septuagint**, the **deuterocanonical books** and the **biblical canon** explain why Bibles from different churches contain different books,[^s8] and the history of English versions runs from **William Tyndale**, who translated the New Testament directly from Greek, to the King James Version and beyond.[^s5]
