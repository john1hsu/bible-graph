steps:
  - Choose a short passage of a few verses, such as a Gospel scene or a psalm, settle into quiet, and ask God to speak to you through his word.
  - Lectio - read the passage slowly several times, aloud if you can, and first ask what the text says in itself and where it sits in its chapter.
  - Meditatio - stay with a word or phrase that stands out, turn it over, and ask what that meaning says to your life today.
  - Oratio - answer God in your own words, with thanks, confession, questions or requests drawn from the passage.
  - Contemplatio - rest quietly before God for a few minutes without trying to produce more thoughts.
  - Actio - choose one concrete way to live out what you received, and test any strong impression against the wider Bible and with your church or small group.

  situation: A member of a small group prays through Luke 10:38-42, the visit of Jesus to Martha and Mary, during a busy season of church volunteering.
  before: "Jesus' words to Martha about the one thing needed jumped out at me, so God is telling me to quit the hospitality team."
  after: "In the scene, Jesus defends Mary for sitting and listening to him while Martha is pulled apart by her serving; he invites me to put listening to him first and to notice where worry crowds it out, and my serving can grow from that."
  why: Lectio begins by asking what the text says in itself before asking what it says to me, so a striking phrase is heard within its scene instead of as a free-floating private message.

# Lectio Divina

Lectio divina (Latin for "divine" or "sacred reading") is an ancient Christian way of reading Scripture slowly and prayerfully, so that reading becomes a conversation with God. Pope Benedict XVI summed up the classic stages as questions: lectio asks what the text says in itself; meditatio, what it says to us; oratio, what we say to the Lord in response; and contemplatio, what conversion of mind, heart and life the Lord is asking of us. He added that the process is not complete until it leads to actio, a life given to others in love.[^s5]

The habit it cultivates is rooted in the Bible itself. The psalmist calls blessed the person whose delight is in the Lord's law: "On his law he meditates day and night" (Psalm 1:2, WEB), and pictures that person as a fruitful tree planted by streams of water (Psalm 1:3).[^s1] Joshua is told that the book of the law shall not depart out of his mouth, and "thou shalt meditate therein day and night" (Joshua 1:8, KJV): meditation that is spoken, not only silent, and that aims at doing what is written.[^s2] Luke says Mary "kept all these sayings, pondering them in her heart" (Luke 2:19, WEB).[^s3] Another Mary sat listening to Jesus while her sister Martha was "distracted with much serving", and Jesus said she had chosen the good part, which would not be taken from her (Luke 10:38-42, WEB).[^s4]

## Where it comes from

Benedict XVI places lectio divina in the tradition of the early church fathers and names Origen as one of its masters.[^s5] In his *Letter to Gregory*, Origen urged his reader to apply himself to the divine Scriptures and insisted that prayer is indispensable for understanding them, citing Jesus' promise that those who ask, seek and knock will receive.[^s11] The Rule of Saint Benedict built set times for reading into every season of the monastic year alongside manual labour, gave each monk a book from the library to read straight through in Lent, and had senior monks check that no one was idle.[^s10] Allan Bouley notes that this came to roughly three hours a day, mostly with Scripture, and that "reading" then meant reading aloud, so that tongue and ear carried the words to the mind.[^s9]

Around 1150 the Carthusian prior Guigo II described four "rungs" of a ladder that became the classic pattern. Reading looks carefully at Scripture, meditation searches out what was hidden, prayer is the heart's longing for what is good, and contemplation lifts the heart to God; reading, he says, puts food in the mouth and meditation chews it.[^s12] The US Catholic bishops note that the practice continued through medieval religious orders such as the Benedictines and Carmelites and is enjoying a revival today.[^s7]

## Why it matters

Lectio divina joins knowing the Bible with praying it. The Catechism of the Catholic Church names lectio divina as one way meditation engages mind, imagination, emotion and desire to deepen faith and dwell on the mysteries of Christ, while insisting that any method is only a guide: the goal is to know and love the Lord Jesus himself.[^s6] Benedict XVI called prayerful reading a fundamental element in the spiritual life of every believer, not only of monks or clergy.[^s5] James Swetnam of the Pontifical Biblical Institute treats the four steps as aspects of a single act of faith, in which, at the end, "God has the last word."[^s8]

## How to apply it

Choose a short passage and read it more than once; Swetnam stresses reading the text again and again and seeking its original meaning in its historical, cultural and religious setting.[^s8] In meditatio, let a word or phrase that stands out lead you to ask how that meaning touches your life now.[^s8][^s7] In oratio, answer God honestly with thanks, confession, questions and requests; in contemplatio, remain quietly before him with an open heart; then take one concrete step of actio.[^s5][^s7] Teachers order the movements differently (the US bishops' guide puts contemplation before prayer), so they are aspects of one prayerful reading, not a rigid recipe.[^s7][^s8]

## Across traditions

Lectio divina grew up in monasteries and is warmly commended in official Catholic teaching.[^s5][^s6] A study in the *Journal of Spiritual Formation and Soul Care* shows that devotional reading of Scripture was an accepted practice throughout evangelical history and examines it under the classic headings of reading, meditation, prayer, contemplation and action.[^s14] Martin Luther inherited the monastic pattern and reshaped it as *oratio, meditatio, tentatio* (prayer, meditation and trial). John Kleinig explains that Luther tied meditation to the spoken word heard in the church's worship, and replaced the climb toward contemplation with the testing of faith, which drives the believer back to God's word.[^s13] Some evangelical teachers add a caution: Timothy Ralston of Dallas Theological Seminary argues that personal impressions from devotional reading carry no authority over others and can even contradict what the text means.[^s15] Benedict XVI makes a related point from the Catholic side, warning against individualism and insisting that Scripture is always read in the communion of the Church.[^s5]

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the reading.** Jumping straight to "what does this say to me?" invites reading our own ideas into the text; both Benedict XVI and Swetnam begin with what the passage says in itself.[^s5][^s8]
- **Treating an impression as revelation.** A phrase that strikes you invites prayer; it is not a message with authority over others.[^s15]
- **Making the method the goal.** A method is only a guide; the aim is union with Christ.[^s6]
- **Reading in isolation.** Prayerful reading belongs within the life of the church, not apart from it.[^s5]
- **Stopping before action.** Joshua meditated in order to obey, and lectio is incomplete without charity.[^s2][^s5]

## Connections

Lectio divina is a form of **prayer**. It pairs naturally with **inductive Bible study**: both begin by asking what the text itself says, though lectio moves from there into prayer and rest before God.[^s5][^s8] Its first step seeks the passage's original meaning in its setting, linking it to **reading in context** and guarding against **eisegesis**.[^s8] It is commended in the teaching of the **Catholic Church**,[^s5][^s6] traced back to **Origen**,[^s5][^s11] and given its classic four-step form by **Guigo II** in the **Ladder of Monks** (*Scala Claustralium*).[^s12]
