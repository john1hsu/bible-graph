steps:
  - Choose a psalm, either one that fits your day or the next one in a regular cycle (your church's daily office, the whole Psalter in a month, or the Byzantine weekly order), and read it aloud slowly.
  - Notice what kind of psalm it is (praise, lament, thanksgiving, trust or wisdom) and look for the place where it turns, such as the move from complaint to trust in Psalm 13.
  - Pray it in the first person, letting its words become your own speech to God; where its mood is not yours today, pray it with and for those who do feel it.
  - Pause after the psalm to name your own situation before God in a sentence or two, using a word or image the psalm gave you.
  - Treat the hard lines honestly, handing anger and the cry for justice over to God rather than turning them into curses on anyone.
  - End with praise, using the traditional "Glory be to the Father" or a sentence of your own, and come back to the same psalm on other days or pray it with others.
example:
  situation: After a hard week, a member of a small group reads Psalm 13 and feels uneasy praying its opening words, "How long, Yahweh? Will you forget me forever?"
  before: "Christians shouldn't complain to God, so I'll skip the first verses and just claim verse 5 about trusting his loving kindness."
  after: "David says 'How long?' to God, not about God behind his back; I can pray the complaint honestly, ask God to answer, and keep praying until I can say with verse 5 that I trust his loving kindness, even if that takes longer than six verses."
  why: The lament is itself addressed to God and is part of the prayer the psalm teaches; skipping to the happy line removes the very path from distress to trust that the psalm walks.

  - Colossians 3:16 and James 5:13 link psalms and songs with teaching, suffering and joy. How could your small group pray a psalm together this week?

# Praying the Psalms

Praying the Psalms means using the Bible's hymnbook as words to say to God, not only as a text to study. The Psalms are poems and songs, and the Catholic *General Instruction of the Liturgy of the Hours* stresses that they are songs of praise rather than readings, so they keep their musical character even when said quietly.[^s11] The fourth-century bishop Athanasius of Alexandria told his friend Marcellinus that while other books of the Bible tell us about holy people, in the Psalms readers speak the words themselves, as if they were their own songs; he wrote that "these words become like a mirror to the person singing them."[^s10]

Scripture itself shows the practice. Psalm 119 says, "Seven times a day, I praise you, because of your righteous ordinances" (Psalm 119:164, WEB).[^s5] After the Last Supper, Jesus and his disciples "had sung a hymn" before going out to the Mount of Olives (Mark 14:26, WEB);[^s6] the Gospel does not say which hymn, but Jewish tradition still sings the Hallel, Psalms 113-118, at the Passover Seder and on festivals.[^s19] On the cross, Luke records Jesus' last words as "Father, into your hands I commit my spirit!" (Luke 23:46, WEB),[^s4] echoing Psalm 31:5, "Into your hand I commend my spirit" (WEB).[^s3] In Acts the believers' prayer addresses God as the one who spoke "by the mouth of your servant, David," and prays a psalm's words back to him (Acts 4:25, WEB),[^s7] and the apostles urge churches to teach one another "with psalms, hymns, and spiritual songs" (Colossians 3:16, WEB) and the cheerful to "sing praises" (James 5:13, WEB).[^s8][^s9]

## Why it matters

The Psalms teach us how to pray by giving us words for every condition of the heart. John Calvin called the book "An Anatomy of all the Parts of the Soul," because every grief, fear, doubt and hope appears in it as in a mirror, and he held it to be the best guide to calling on God, showing believers how to keep praying through doubt until faith prevails.[^s13] Walter Brueggemann describes the Psalms as following the movement of life itself: psalms of *orientation* when the world seems good and secure, of *disorientation* when it falls apart (the laments), and of *new orientation* when God gives a "new song."[^s17] Psalm 13 walks that whole road in six verses, from "How long, Yahweh? Will you forget me forever?" to "I trust in your loving kindness" (Psalm 13:1, 5, WEB).[^s1] Psalm 88 does not even reach the turn: it ends with friends far away and darkness, yet it is still spoken to "the God of my salvation" (Psalm 88:1, 18, WEB).[^s2] Such psalms show that no experience is too dark to bring to God.[^s17]

## How to apply it

Begin with one psalm, read aloud slowly, and pray it in the first person.[^s10] Notice its kind and where it turns, as the move from plea to praise in many laments.[^s17][^s1] The Catholic *General Instruction* asks that the mind be in accord with the voice, and it addresses a common difficulty: when you must pray a joyful psalm while sad, or a lament while happy, you pray it in the name of the whole Church, finding reason to rejoice with those who rejoice and weep with those who weep.[^s11] Add a sentence of your own prayer, and close with praise; the traditional "Glory be to the Father" at the end of each psalm gives it a Trinitarian and Christ-centred meaning.[^s11] Over time, follow a cycle so that you meet the psalms you would not have chosen.[^s14][^s15]

## Across traditions

Christian traditions organise the practice differently. In the Catholic Liturgy of the Hours, the Second Vatican Council describes the Divine Office as the prayer Christ himself, together with his body, addresses to the Father; the psalms are spread over a four-week cycle, and those who pray them do so "in the person of Christ himself," even when praying alone.[^s12][^s11] In the Byzantine tradition shared by Orthodox and Eastern Catholic churches, the Psalter is divided into twenty *kathismata* ("sittings"), read at services such as Vespers, so that the whole book is heard each week and twice a week in Great Lent.[^s16][^s15] The Anglican *Book of Common Prayer* of 1662 directs that "the Psalter shall be read through once every Month" at Morning and Evening Prayer.[^s14] Calvin's preface shows the Reformed love for the Psalms as the school of prayer.[^s13] Jewish worship, in which the Psalms first lived, continues to pray them, as in the Hallel of the festivals.[^s19]

## Common mistakes

- **Skipping the laments.** A reviewer of Brueggemann's work notes his warning that many churches avoid the laments and sing only psalms of orientation, though honest faith needs them.[^s18]
- **Lifting a verse out of its psalm.** Brueggemann warns that, read as conventional language, the psalms of extremity are reduced to clichés; pray the whole psalm and its turn.[^s17][^s1]
- **Using the cursing psalms as curses.** The *General Instruction* says that such psalms, though quoted in the New Testament, are in no way meant to be used as curses.[^s11]
- **Rattling them off.** Athanasius asked that the psalms be recited simply as written, without artificial embellishment, and the *General Instruction* asks for the mind to match the voice.[^s10][^s11]
- **Mixing up the numbers.** Byzantine books use the Greek numbering of the Psalms, which differs from most modern English Bibles, so check a cross-reference when following a traditional cycle.[^s15]

## Connections

Praying the Psalms is a form of **prayer** and is used for the **Book of Psalms**, whose Hebrew poetry it voices aloud.[^s10][^s11] It speaks especially to **suffering and lament**, giving words for disorientation as well as praise.[^s17] It is practised in the **Catholic Church**, **Eastern Orthodoxy**, **Anglicanism** and the **Reformed tradition**.[^s11][^s16][^s14][^s13] It pairs with **lectio divina** (slow, prayerful reading) and **Scripture memorisation**. Psalm 31:5 on Jesus' lips in Luke 23:46 shows the Psalms shaping Jesus' own prayer at the crucifixion; the article on **Psalm 22** follows another psalm in the passion story.[^s3][^s4]
