# Gospel of Mark

The Gospel of Mark is the second and shortest of the four Gospels, and most scholars think it was the first to be written.[^s2] There is no birth story: it opens with a headline, "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1, ASV), and moves straight to John the Baptist.[^s1] Its favourite word, "immediately" or "straightway", appears dozens of times, and its scenes are often more vivid than the parallels in Matthew and Luke.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Mark shows who Jesus is by what he does: he teaches with authority, drives out demons, heals, calms the storm (4:39) and forgives sins (2:5), yet he heads deliberately toward the cross.[^s1][^s2] At the turning point he says that "the Son of man also came not to be ministered unto, but to minister, and to give his life a ransom for many" (10:45, ASV).[^s1] Mark is honest about the disciples' slowness and failure, which makes it a Gospel for ordinary, struggling followers; the call to "take up his cross, and follow me" (8:34, ASV) is for everyone.[^s1][^s3][^s5]

## Background

**Author.** The Gospel does not name its author; the heading "According to Mark" was attached in the manuscripts.[^s3] Papias, quoted by Eusebius, reported that Mark, "the interpreter of Peter", wrote down Peter's teaching accurately, though "not, however, in exact order".[^s4] Irenaeus also calls Mark Peter's disciple and interpreter.[^s6] Tradition identifies him with John Mark of Acts: his mother's Jerusalem house was a place of prayer (Acts 12:12); his leaving Paul and Barnabas mid-journey later caused a sharp quarrel (Acts 13:13; 15:37-39); yet he became Paul's valued fellow worker (Colossians 4:10; 2 Timothy 4:11), and 1 Peter 5:13 sends greetings from "my son" Mark.[^s7][^s2]

**Date and place.** Irenaeus places the writing after the "departure" of Peter and Paul; the traditional setting is Rome, with a church facing persecution.[^s6][^s3] Proposals range from the 50s to just after AD 70 (see *Scholarly views*).[^s2][^s3][^s5]

**Audience.** Mark explains Jewish washing customs (7:3-4), translates Aramaic words and uses many Latin terms, so his first readers were probably Gentile Christians.[^s1][^s2][^s3] Jesus' warning that his followers would be "hated of all men for my name's sake" (13:13, ASV) spoke to a church under pressure.[^s1][^s3]

## Outline

The *New American Bible* and the *ESV Global Study Bible* both make Peter's confession the hinge:[^s3][^s5]

1. **Preparation (1:1-15):** John the Baptist, the baptism, the testing, and Jesus' first proclamation.[^s1][^s5]
2. **Authority in Galilee and beyond (1:16–8:26):** disciples called, healings and exorcisms, controversies, parables and feedings.[^s5]
3. **The road to Jerusalem (8:27–10:52):** Peter's confession and three passion predictions (8:31; 9:31; 10:33-34).[^s5][^s1]
4. **Jerusalem (11:1–13:37):** the entry, the temple, debates, and the discourse on the future.[^s5]
5. **Passion and empty tomb (14:1–16:8).**[^s5][^s3]
6. **The longer ending (16:9-20),** which the NABRE presents as attached by other hands.[^s3][^s5]

## Key themes

- **Jesus the Son of God.** The title frames the book: 1:1, the Father's voice at the baptism (1:11) and transfiguration (9:7), and the centurion at the cross: "Truly this man was the Son of God" (15:39, ASV).[^s1][^s3]
- **The kingdom of God.** Jesus' first words are "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand" (1:15, ASV); his parables reveal its "mystery" (4:11).[^s1]
- **The suffering Son of Man.** Jesus often commands silence about his deeds and identity; when Peter confesses "Thou art the Christ", Jesus tells them to tell no one and teaches that the Son of Man "must suffer many things" (8:29-31, ASV).[^s1][^s3] He corrects mistaken hopes about the Messiah.[^s5]
- **Discipleship and failure.** At the arrest all flee, Peter denies Jesus, and a young man runs off without his linen cloth (14:51-52), whom some identify as Mark himself.[^s1][^s3][^s2]
- **Fulfilment of Scripture.** Mark opens with words "written in Isaiah the prophet" (1:2, ASV), including Isaiah 40:3, and from the cross Jesus cries the first line of Psalm 22, "My God, my God, why have you forsaken me?" (Mark 15:34; Psalm 22:1, WEB).[^s1][^s8][^s9]

## Scholarly views

All Christian traditions receive Mark as Scripture; the debates concern its author, date and ending.[^s2][^s3] The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* and *ESV Global Study Bible* hold the traditional view that John Mark recorded Peter's preaching at Rome, in the 50s or 60s.[^s2][^s5] The Catholic NABRE introduction reports that modern research often proposes an unknown Hellenistic Jewish Christian, perhaps in Syria, shortly after 70, and cautions against exaggerating Peter's influence.[^s3] Most scholars hold that Matthew and Luke used Mark, though a minority, such as W. R. Farmer, dissent.[^s2] The two oldest Greek manuscripts, Codex Sinaiticus and Codex Vaticanus, end at 16:8; most scholars regard 16:9-20 as a later compilation, and whether Mark's original ending was lost is debated.[^s2][^s3]

## Common misreadings

Mark is sometimes treated as a rough draft that Matthew and Luke improved, but its brisk style keeps pressing the question "who say ye that I am?" (8:29, ASV).[^s1][^s5] The ending at 16:8, with the frightened women, can sound like defeat, yet the young man has already announced "he is risen" and promised that "his disciples and Peter" will see Jesus in Galilee (16:6-7, ASV).[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

Mark is **part of** the Gospels.[^s2] Papias **attributes** it to John Mark.[^s4] It **parallels** Matthew and Luke, which most scholars think used it.[^s2] It **teaches** the kingdom of God (1:15).[^s1] It **quotes** the Book of Isaiah (40:3) and Psalm 22.[^s8][^s9] It **features** Peter and narrates the crucifixion of Jesus.[^s1] It is **preserved in** Codex Sinaiticus, which ends at 16:8.[^s2] It includes the Sadducees' question about the resurrection (12:18-27) and Jesus' citing of the Shema (12:28-34).[^s1]
