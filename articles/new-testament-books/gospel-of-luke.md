# Gospel of Luke

The Gospel of Luke is the third of the four Gospels and the first half of a two-part work that continues in the Acts of the Apostles.[^s3] It tells the story of Jesus from the announcements of his birth to his resurrection and ascension, and opens with a rare personal preface: having "traced the course of all things accurately from the first", Luke writes to "most excellent Theophilus" so that he may "know the certainty" of what he was taught (Luke 1:3-4, ASV).[^s1] Many of its parables are found nowhere else.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Luke shows how God's promises to Israel are fulfilled in Jesus and then opened to the whole world.[^s3] The angel's news is "good tidings of great joy which shall be to all the people": a Saviour, "Christ the Lord", is born in the city of David (Luke 2:10-11, ASV).[^s1] The genealogy runs back to "the son of Adam, the son of God" (3:38, ASV), and the heart of the book is Jesus' word to Zacchaeus: "the Son of man came to seek and to save that which was lost" (19:10, ASV).[^s1][^s2] Jesus here is tender toward the poor, outcasts and sinners, and severe toward pride and the love of wealth.[^s3]

## Background

**Author.** The Gospel never names its author, and its title was added later.[^s6] From the late second century the church named Luke, Paul's companion; Irenaeus says Luke "recorded in a book the Gospel preached by" Paul.[^s4][^s3] Paul sends greetings from "Luke, the beloved physician" (Colossians 4:14, WEB), and the "we" passages of Acts suggest the author travelled with Paul.[^s9][^s2] Luke drew on those who were "eyewitnesses and ministers of the word" (1:2, ASV), not claiming to be one himself.[^s1][^s3]

**Date and place.** Proposals range from the late 50s to the 80s or later (see *Scholarly views*); the place of writing is unknown.[^s2][^s3][^s5]

**Audience.** Theophilus, also addressed in Acts, may have been a patron or an official; through him Luke writes for a largely Gentile church, explaining Jewish customs and highlighting God's concern for Gentiles.[^s2][^s3]

## Outline

The *New American Bible* and *ESV Global Study Bible* outlines agree:[^s3][^s5]

1. **Prologue (1:1-4).**
2. **Infancy narrative (1:5–2:52),** with Mary's song, the Magnificat (1:46-55).[^s1]
3. **Preparation for the ministry (3:1–4:13).**
4. **Ministry in Galilee (4:14–9:50):** beginning with the sermon at Nazareth (4:16-21) and including the Sermon on the Plain (6:17-49).[^s1][^s2]
5. **Journey to Jerusalem (9:51–19:27):** Jesus "stedfastly set his face to go to Jerusalem" (9:51, ASV); most of Luke's unique parables fall here.[^s1][^s2]
6. **Teaching in Jerusalem (19:28–21:38).**
7. **Passion (22:1–23:56).**
8. **Resurrection and ascension (24:1-53),** including the walk to Emmaus (24:13-35).[^s1]

## Key themes

- **Good news to the poor, fulfilled "today."** At Nazareth Jesus reads that he is anointed "to preach good tidings to the poor" and announces, "To-day hath this scripture been fulfilled in your ears" (Luke 4:18, 21, ASV).[^s1][^s8] In the Sermon on the Plain he blesses the poor and warns the rich (6:20, 24).[^s1]
- **The kingdom of God.** Jesus says, "I must preach the good tidings of the kingdom of God to the other cities also" (4:43, ASV).[^s1]
- **The Holy Spirit and prayer.** Luke speaks of the Spirit more than any other Gospel, from Jesus' conception (1:35) to the promised "power from on high" (24:49, ASV), and often shows Jesus at prayer.[^s3][^s1]
- **Mercy and the lost.** Luke 15 answers the complaint that Jesus welcomes sinners (15:1-2) with the lost sheep, coin and son, and only Luke tells of the Good Samaritan.[^s7][^s3] On the cross Jesus prays, "Father, forgive them; for they know not what they do" (23:34, ASV), and promises the penitent criminal Paradise "to-day" (23:43).[^s1]
- **Women and the overlooked.** Luke gives unusual attention to women: Mary and Elizabeth in the infancy story, the women who supported Jesus' ministry, and the sisters Martha and Mary (10:38-42).[^s2][^s3]
- **The great reversal and joy.** The humble are lifted up and the proud brought low, and the Gospel closes with the disciples praising God in the temple "with great joy" (24:52-53, ASV).[^s5][^s1]

## Scholarly views

Christians of every tradition receive Luke as Scripture; scholars debate its author and date.[^s2][^s3] The traditional view, defended by the *Encyclopedia of the Bible* and the *ESV Global Study Bible*, holds that Luke the physician, Paul's companion, wrote Luke and Acts, around AD 58 by the *Encyclopedia*'s reckoning or from about AD 62 by the ESV's.[^s2][^s5] The Catholic *New American Bible* introduction reports that most scholars date it after the Roman destruction of Jerusalem in AD 70, many between 80 and 90, reading passages such as 19:43-44 and 21:20 in that light, and that Luke drew on Mark, a sayings source shared with Matthew ("Q") and material of his own ("L").[^s3] Mikeal Parsons notes broad agreement that one author wrote Luke and Acts, while scholars debate how unified the two volumes are; the old argument that Luke's vocabulary proves a doctor's hand was challenged by Henry Cadbury.[^s6][^s2]

## Common misreadings

The Good Samaritan is often reduced to "be nice to strangers." But the lawyer asks "who is my neighbor?" to set limits (10:29), and Jesus replies with a story in which a Samaritan "proved neighbor" (10:36, ASV): the question becomes not whom I must love but whether I will be a neighbour.[^s1] Luke 15 is sometimes read as good sinners against bad religious people, yet the father goes out to plead with the elder son too, and the story ends before we hear his answer.[^s7][^s1]

## Connections

Luke is **part of** the Gospels.[^s2] Tradition from Irenaeus on **attributes** it to Luke the evangelist.[^s4] It **complements** Acts as the first of two volumes.[^s3] It **parallels** Mark, widely seen as one of its sources.[^s3] It **teaches** the kingdom of God (4:43) and **quotes** Isaiah 61:1-2 in Jesus' first sermon (4:18-19).[^s1][^s8] It **features** Jerusalem, his journey's goal, and the walk to Emmaus, where the risen Jesus explains "the things concerning himself" in all the Scriptures (24:27, ASV).[^s3][^s1] It **speaks to** forgiveness: no Gospel dwells more on Jesus' mercy.[^s3]
