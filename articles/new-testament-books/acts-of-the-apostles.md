# Acts of the Apostles

The Acts of the Apostles, usually just called Acts, stands between the four Gospels and the letters of the New Testament. It opens by looking back: "The former treatise I made, O Theophilus, concerning all that Jesus began both to do and to teach" (Acts 1:1, ASV).[^s1] That earlier volume is the Gospel of Luke, addressed to the same "most excellent Theophilus" (Luke 1:3), and Acts carries the story on from Jesus' ascension to Paul's arrival in Rome.[^s6][^s3] Across 28 chapters it tells how a small company of Jewish disciples in Jerusalem became a movement of Jews and Gentiles spread across the Roman world.[^s1][^s3]

## Why it matters

Without Acts, we would not know how the churches Paul writes to came to exist. Acts tells that story: Pentecost, the first church in Jerusalem, Saul's conversion, the welcome of Gentiles, and the journeys behind many of Paul's letters.[^s2][^s3] It also shows the church learning to pray, share, decide and suffer together; the first believers "continued stedfastly in the apostles' teaching and fellowship, in the breaking of bread and the prayers" (2:42, ASV).[^s1] The Catholic *New American Bible* introduction sums up its picture of the early church as a "Spirit-guided community" carrying Jesus' word outward.[^s3]

## Background

**Author.** Like the Gospels, Acts does not name its author. From the second century the church attributed both Luke and Acts to Luke, the companion of Paul whom Colossians 4:14 calls "the beloved physician" (WEB).[^s7][^s2] The second-century bishop Irenaeus argued that Luke was "inseparable from Paul," pointing to the passages in Acts where the narrator says "we".[^s4] These "we" sections (16:10-17; 20:5-15; 21:1-18; 27:1–28:16) begin when Paul answers the vision of the man of Macedonia: "straightway we sought to go forth into Macedonia" (16:10, ASV).[^s1][^s2]

**Date.** Proposals range from the early 60s, when the story ends, to the early second century (see *Scholarly views*).[^s2][^s5]

**Audience and purpose.** Acts is written to Theophilus so that he might "know the certainty" of what he had been taught (Luke 1:4, WEB), and through him to the wider church.[^s6] Scholars see several overlapping aims: to record a movement Luke believed was God's work, to show that the welcome of Gentiles was part of God's plan from the start, and to show Roman officials repeatedly finding the Christians innocent of sedition.[^s2][^s3]

## Outline

The risen Jesus' words in Acts 1:8 work as a map of the book: "ye shall be my witnesses both in Jerusalem, and in all Judaea and Samaria, and unto the uttermost part of the earth" (ASV).[^s1] The *New American Bible* introduction divides it accordingly:[^s3]

1. **Preparation for the mission (1:1–2:13):** Jesus' ascension (1:9-11) and the coming of the Spirit at Pentecost (2:1-4).[^s1]
2. **The mission in Jerusalem (2:14–8:3):** Peter's preaching, the life of the first community, and the martyrdom of Stephen, where "a young man named Saul" appears (7:58).[^s1]
3. **The mission in Judea and Samaria (8:4–9:43):** Philip preaches in Samaria (8:5), and Saul meets the risen Jesus on the Damascus road (9:3-4).[^s1]
4. **The Gentile mission begins (10:1–15:35):** the Spirit falls on the centurion Cornelius' household (10:44-45); believers are first called "Christians" at Antioch (11:26); Barnabas and Saul are sent out (13:2-3); and the Jerusalem council meets (15).[^s1]
5. **Paul's mission to the ends of the earth (15:36–28:31):** journeys through Asia Minor and Greece, arrest in Jerusalem, and the voyage to Rome.[^s3][^s1]

## Key themes

- **The Holy Spirit.** The Spirit empowers the witnesses (1:8; 2:4), sends out missionaries (13:2) and guides the church's decisions (15:28).[^s1][^s3]
- **Witness to the risen Jesus.** The apostles are witnesses commissioned by the risen Lord, and their speeches proclaim Jesus as the fulfilment of Scripture and call hearers to repentance.[^s2][^s3]
- **Jews and Gentiles together.** The good news moves from Jews to Samaritans to Gentiles, and the council of Acts 15 settles that Gentile believers need not be circumcised (15:1-6, 28).[^s1][^s3]
- **The kingdom of God.** The book begins with Jesus "speaking the things concerning the kingdom of God" (1:3) and ends with Paul "preaching the kingdom of God" in Rome (28:31).[^s1]

## Scholarly views

Christians across traditions receive Acts as inspired Scripture; scholars disagree about its author, date and historical method.[^s2][^s5] The traditional view, defended in the *Encyclopedia of the Bible*, holds that Luke the physician wrote it in the early 60s: the book ends before Paul's trial and does not mention Nero's persecution or the fall of Jerusalem, and the "we" sections read as an eyewitness's travel notes.[^s2] Joseph Tyson of Southern Methodist University reports that most modern scholars prefer a date around 80–90, while a growing number argue for about 110–120, partly because Acts may draw on Josephus' *Antiquities* (93–94) and on Galatians; Tyson himself reads Acts partly as a response to Marcion.[^s5] On historical reliability, the *Encyclopedia* points to details confirmed by inscriptions, such as the "politarchs" of Thessalonica, while the Catholic introduction counsels neither exaggerating Luke's literal accuracy nor underestimating its factual worth.[^s2][^s3]

## Common misreadings

The title can mislead: most of the Twelve barely appear, and the story centres on Peter and then on Paul.[^s2] Acts is also sometimes read as the story of Christians leaving Judaism behind. In fact its first believers are Jews in Jerusalem, and the great debate of chapter 15 is whether Gentile believers must be circumcised (15:1); Luke presents their inclusion as part of God's plan from the start, not as a rejection of Israel.[^s1][^s3]

## Connections

Acts **features** Paul, whose call and journeys fill its second half, and Barnabas, who is sent out with him from Antioch (13:2-3).[^s1] It **features** Peter, whose sermons and visit to Cornelius open the first half.[^s2] It **features** the outpouring of the Spirit at Pentecost (2:1-4).[^s1] Church tradition **attributes** it to Luke, Paul's fellow worker, as Irenaeus argued from the "we" passages.[^s4] It **teaches** the kingdom of God, which frames the whole book from 1:3 to 28:31.[^s1] It continues the **Gospel of Luke**, to which it is the sequel.[^s6][^s3]
