# The Four Gospels

The New Testament opens with four books, Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, that each tell the story of Jesus: what he did and taught, how he died and how he rose.[^s9] The church has read them from early times as four records of one Gospel, each giving a portrait of Christ from its writer's own perspective.[^s8] Mark begins with a heading for the whole: "The beginning of the gospel of Jesus Christ, the Son of God" (Mark 1:1, ASV).[^s1]

## Why it matters

Everything Christians know first-hand about Jesus' ministry comes to them chiefly through these four books. The Catholic Church's Second Vatican Council gives the Gospels "a special preeminence" among the Scriptures as the principal witness to the life and teaching of the incarnate Word, and the New Testament itself places them first.[^s12][^s9] Knowing how the four fit together and differ helps a reader hear each one clearly.[^s8]

## What "gospel" means

The Greek word *euangelion* means "good news".[^s8][^s9] Its roots lie in Isaiah, where a herald runs ahead of God's people with a joyful message: "How beautiful on the mountains are the feet of him who brings good news" (Isaiah 52:7, WEB), and Zion is told to "go up on a high mountain" and announce "Behold, your God!" (Isaiah 40:9, WEB).[^s5][^s4][^s8] Mark's opening quotes the same chapter of Isaiah, the voice crying in the wilderness (Mark 1:2-3; Isaiah 40:3), and Jesus begins preaching that "the kingdom of God is at hand: repent ye, and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:15, ASV).[^s1][^s4]

Before it named a book, "gospel" meant the message the apostles preached.[^s9] Paul reminds the Corinthians of "the gospel which I preached unto you": that Christ died for our sins, was buried, was raised on the third day and appeared to Cephas and the Twelve (1 Corinthians 15:1-5, ASV).[^s6] The four Gospels tell that same message as narrative, which is why each gives so much space to Jesus' last days and resurrection.[^s8] The early titles say "according to Matthew" or "according to John": one gospel, told by four witnesses. Justin Martyr is the first writer known to call the books themselves "Gospels", and the usage spread from the second century.[^s8][^s9]

## Books included

1. **Matthew**, which the *Encyclopedia of the Bible* links to an early Jewish-Christian setting.[^s8] Papias, as quoted by Eusebius, says Matthew compiled Jesus' sayings "in the Hebrew language".[^s11]
2. **Mark**, probably the earliest. Papias calls Mark Peter's interpreter, who "wrote down accurately whatsoever he remembered", though not in strict order.[^s11] The *Encyclopedia of the Bible* thinks it likely that Mark wrote in Rome for Gentile readers.[^s8]
3. **Luke**, the first half of a two-volume work completed by Acts. Its preface says that "many have undertaken to set in order a narrative" and that Luke traced everything carefully so that Theophilus might know "the certainty" of what he had been taught (Luke 1:1-4, WEB).[^s2][^s9]
4. **John**, which goes largely its own way, with different episodes, long discourses and its own chronology.[^s8] It states its purpose plainly: "these are written, that ye may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God" (John 20:31, ASV), adding that many other signs went unwritten (20:30; 21:25).[^s3][^s7]

Matthew, Mark and Luke have been called the **Synoptic Gospels** since the 1780s, because they can be "seen together" in parallel columns, sharing much of their order, content and wording; John differs in outline and approach.[^s14][^s9][^s8]

## How the four became one collection

The four circulated together early. When Marcion rejected Matthew, Mark and John, the church's answer treated the four as a single collection, and around AD 170 Tatian wove them into one continuous harmony, the *Diatessaron*.[^s8] Yet the church kept the four separate books. Irenaeus of Lyons held the number fixed at four, rebuked those who would add or remove Gospels, compared them to the four winds and to the four living creatures of Revelation, and said the Word gave the Gospel "under four aspects, but bound together by one Spirit".[^s10] The Second Vatican Council likewise speaks of "the fourfold Gospel", whose writers selected, summarised and explained what had been handed down, in view of their churches' needs.[^s12]

## Scholarly views

Christians across traditions receive all four Gospels as Scripture; what scholars debate is how they were written and how they relate.[^s8][^s9] The tradition passed on by Papias and Irenaeus links them with apostles and their companions, and the *Encyclopedia of the Bible* holds that internal evidence places all four in the first century.[^s11][^s10][^s8] Most scholars accept **Markan priority**, the view that Mark came first and was used by Matthew and Luke.[^s8][^s13] The widely taught **Two-Source hypothesis** adds a lost sayings source, "Q", to explain roughly 250 verses that Matthew and Luke share beyond Mark, which the *Encyclopedia* thinks likely but not certain.[^s8] Mark Goodacre defends Markan priority while arguing for the **Farrer hypothesis**, in which Luke used Matthew and Q is unnecessary; the **Griesbach hypothesis** instead puts Matthew first and has Mark use both Matthew and Luke.[^s13] Historical-critical teachers such as Dale Martin at Yale stress that the Gospels are not modern biographies, reading Mark, for example, as a skilfully crafted narrative with its own message; the *Encyclopedia* agrees that they are historical in form while aiming above all to proclaim the good news.[^s15][^s8]

## Common misreadings

One habit is to merge the four into a single blended story, as if the differences were noise. Tatian tried this, but the church kept four books, and each Evangelist's choices, such as John's stated aim of leading readers to faith, are part of what Scripture says.[^s8][^s10][^s3] The opposite habit treats every difference in order or wording as an error, though even Papias said Mark did not write in strict order.[^s11]

## Connections

The four Gospels are **part of** the New Testament, standing at its head.[^s9] They **feature** Jesus, as the principal witness to his life and teaching.[^s12] They **teach** the kingdom of God, the theme of Jesus' first preaching (Mark 1:14-15).[^s1] They **quote** the Book of Isaiah, beginning with Mark's opening (Mark 1:2-3).[^s1] Their fourfold shape is **corroborated by** Irenaeus's *Against Heresies*, the earliest full defence of exactly four Gospels.[^s10]
