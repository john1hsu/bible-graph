# Gospel of Matthew

The Gospel of Matthew stands first among the four Gospels and opens the New Testament. It tells the story of Jesus from his genealogy and birth to his death, resurrection and final commission, beginning with the words "The book of the generation of Jesus Christ, the son of David, the son of Abraham" (Matthew 1:1, ASV).[^s1] More than any other Gospel it shows how Jesus fulfils the Scriptures of Israel, and it gathers his teaching into five long discourses, including the Sermon on the Mount.[^s2] In the first two centuries it was the Gospel Christians quoted most often, which helps explain its place at the head of the canon.[^s2]

## Why it matters

Matthew is the bridge between the Testaments. Again and again it pauses to say that something happened "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken by the Lord through the prophet" (Matthew 1:22, ASV), and it quotes the Old Testament more than fifty times, so Matthew is best read with Israel's Scriptures open.[^s1][^s2] It also gives the church much of its most familiar teaching: the Sermon on the Mount, Peter's confession of Jesus as Messiah, and the Great Commission.[^s5] Above all, it is framed by a promise: the child is named Immanuel, "God with us" (1:23), and the risen Jesus ends the book by saying "I am with you always" (28:20).[^s1]

## Background

**Author.** The Gospel itself names no author. From the second century the church attributed it to Matthew, the tax collector whom Jesus called from "the place of toll" (Matthew 9:9, ASV).[^s1][^s2] The earliest witness is Papias, an early church leader quoted by the historian Eusebius, who says Matthew put together "the oracles in the Hebrew language".[^s3] Irenaeus, Origen, Eusebius and Jerome repeat the attribution.[^s2]

**Date and place.** Those who hold apostolic authorship often date the book to the late 50s or early 60s AD.[^s5] Many other scholars date it after the fall of Jerusalem in AD 70, often a decade or more later, partly because Matthew draws on Mark and partly because of the burned city in the parable of Matthew 22:7.[^s4][^s1] Antioch in Syria, a city with both Jewish and Gentile believers, is a common suggestion for where it was written or first read.[^s4][^s5]

**Audience.** Matthew writes for a community steeped in the Jewish Scriptures, probably made up of Jewish Christians who were learning to welcome Gentiles; the mission that begins with "the lost sheep of the house of Israel" (10:6) ends with "all the nations" (28:19).[^s1][^s4][^s5]

## Outline

Matthew alternates narrative with long blocks of teaching. Each of the five teaching blocks closes with a similar sentence, "when Jesus had finished these words" or its like (7:28; 11:1; 13:53; 19:1; 26:1).[^s1][^s2]

1. **Prologue (1–2):** genealogy, birth and infancy of the Messiah.
2. **Ministry begins (3–4)**, then **Discourse 1, the Sermon on the Mount (5–7):** life in the kingdom.
3. **Mighty works (8–9)**, then **Discourse 2 (10):** the mission of the Twelve.
4. **Growing opposition (11–12)**, then **Discourse 3 (13):** parables of the kingdom.
5. **Jesus' identity revealed (14–17)**, including Peter's confession, then **Discourse 4 (18):** life together in the community.
6. **Toward Jerusalem (19–22)**, then **Discourse 5 (23–25):** woes against hypocrisy and teaching about the end and judgement (some outlines count only 24–25).
7. **Passion and resurrection (26–28):** the Last Supper, the cross, the empty tomb and the Great Commission.[^s2][^s4][^s5]

Some readers see in the five discourses an echo of the five books of Moses, though Matthew never says so directly.[^s2]

## Key themes

- **Jesus the Messiah.** He is Son of David, Son of Abraham, Son of God and Immanuel; Peter confesses, "Thou art the Christ, the Son of the living God" (16:16, ASV).[^s1][^s4]
- **Fulfilment.** Jesus says he came "not to destroy, but to fulfil" the Law and the Prophets (5:17, ASV), and his disciples' righteousness must exceed that of the scribes and Pharisees (5:20).[^s1][^s4]
- **The kingdom of heaven.** Matthew's distinctive phrase appears over thirty times; Jesus' first preaching is "Repent ye; for the kingdom of heaven is at hand" (4:17, ASV).[^s1][^s2]
- **The church.** Matthew alone among the Gospels uses the word "church" (16:18; 18:17).[^s1][^s2]
- **Discipleship and mission.** Disciples learn to follow the way of the cross and are sent to make disciples of all nations (28:18-20).[^s4][^s5]

## Scholarly views

Christians agree that Matthew is inspired Scripture; scholars disagree about who wrote it and when.[^s2] The traditional view, still defended by a number of scholars, holds that the apostle Matthew wrote it, perhaps before AD 70; the *Encyclopedia of the Bible* argues the early testimony should not be lightly set aside.[^s2][^s5] The majority of modern scholars, including the introduction to the Catholic *New American Bible*, regard the author as an unknown Jewish Christian teacher writing after AD 70 who used Mark's Gospel, a collection of sayings shared with Luke (often called "Q") and material of his own; that introduction keeps the name Matthew for convenience and allows that the apostle may stand behind some of its traditions.[^s4] Scholars also debate what Papias meant by Matthew's "oracles": a collection of sayings, a Hebrew Gospel, or Old Testament proof texts.[^s2][^s3]

## Common misreadings

Matthew 18:20, "where two or three are gathered together in my name", is often quoted as if Jesus were present only in small prayer meetings. In context it follows instructions about a believer who refuses to listen even to "the church" (18:17), and it promises Christ's presence to a community seeking to restore and discern together.[^s1] The sharp words of Matthew 23 have also been misused against Jewish people; the Catholic introduction notes that Matthew's warnings apply equally to disciples who fail to do God's will.[^s4]

## Connections

Matthew is **part of** the Gospels, standing at their head.[^s2] Church tradition **attributes** it to the apostle Matthew, beginning with Papias.[^s3] It **teaches** the kingdom of God, which it calls the kingdom of heaven.[^s2] It **features** the Pharisees as Jesus' frequent opponents and conversation partners.[^s4] It **parallels** the Gospel of Mark, using nearly all of Mark's material, and shares sayings with Luke.[^s4]
