# The Story of the Bible

The Bible is a library of many books written over many centuries, yet Christians have always read it as more than an anthology. Running through law, poetry, prophecy, gospel and letter is one unfolding story of God and the world, which begins with creation and ends with a new creation, and has Jesus Christ at its centre.[^s10][^s11] Knowing its shape helps a reader understand any single passage better.

## The story in brief

**Creation.** The Bible opens with God making the heavens and the earth, creating humanity "in his own image", and seeing that everything he had made "was very good" (Genesis 1:1, 27, 31, WEB).[^s1]

**Rebellion and exile from Eden.** In Genesis 3 the first humans disobey; the ground is cursed, they are sent out of the garden, and cherubim guard the way to the tree of life (Genesis 3:17, 23-24).[^s2] Orthodox teaching calls this the fall, through which sin and death enter and creation itself needs restoring.[^s11]

**Promise to Abraham.** God calls Abram to leave his country and promises to make him a great nation, adding that "all of the families of the earth will be blessed in you" (Genesis 12:1-3, WEB).[^s3] From here the story narrows to one family, but for the sake of every family.

**Israel.** Through the exodus, the covenant and law given through Moses, the land, the kings and the prophets, God forms and disciplines a people; the same Orthodox guide describes this whole stretch as preparation for the Messiah.[^s11] It runs through the exile to Babylon and the return, still waiting for something greater.

**Jesus.** The letter to the Hebrews says that God, who spoke "at sundry times and in divers manners" through the prophets, has "in these last days spoken unto us by his Son" (Hebrews 1:1-2, KJV).[^s5] After the resurrection Jesus explains, "beginning from Moses and from all the prophets", what all the Scriptures say about himself, and later names the law of Moses, the prophets and the psalms as written "concerning me" (Luke 24:27, 44, WEB).[^s4]

**The church and the nations.** In the same chapter Jesus says repentance and forgiveness are to be preached in his name "to all the nations, beginning at Jerusalem" (Luke 24:47, WEB) — the promise to Abraham now reaching outward.[^s4][^s3]

**New creation.** The story closes with John's vision of a new heaven and a new earth, God dwelling with his people, and every tear wiped away (Revelation 21:1-5).[^s6] The river of life and the tree of life return, and "There will be no curse any more" (Revelation 22:1-3, WEB) — the end answering the beginning.[^s7][^s2]

## How Christians tell it

The traditions frame the story in different words, but they agree on its unity and its centre in Christ. The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* teaches that from apostolic times the church has read the two Testaments as one divine plan, using typology to see how events such as the call of the patriarchs and the exodus foreshadow what God accomplished in Christ, without losing their own value; it recalls Augustine's saying that the New Testament lies hidden in the Old and the Old is unveiled in the New.[^s10] The Orthodox Church in America presents the Old Testament as the Christian's own history with God, moving through creation, fall, promise and preparation to the Incarnation, and notes the saints' conviction that Jesus cannot be understood apart from what led up to him.[^s11]

Many Protestant teachers use the image of a drama. N. T. Wright likened Scripture to a play in five acts (creation, fall, Israel, Jesus, and the church), in which the church, steeped in the earlier acts, must perform the final act with both "innovation and consistency".[^s12] Craig Bartholomew and Michael Goheen's textbook *The Drama of Scripture* likewise presents the Bible as a grand narrative that grounds a Christian view of the world.[^s14]

## Where the Old Testament ends

Book order affects how readers hear the story. The Hebrew Bible, Jewish Scripture, ends with Chronicles and Cyrus's decree inviting God's people to rebuild the house in Jerusalem: "let him go up" (2 Chronicles 36:23, WEB).[^s8] English Protestant Bibles end the Old Testament with Malachi's promise, "I will send you Elijah the prophet" (Malachi 4:5, WEB).[^s9] Gregory Goswell argues that the last book has a real say in how the whole is read, that Malachi's final place is a sixteenth-century Protestant development rather than an ancient choice, and that every candidate ending looks forward to an unfinished hope of God's rule.[^s13] Jewish and Christian readers alike, then, receive these Scriptures as a story still reaching toward its goal.[^s13][^s8]

## Why it matters

The story gives every verse an address: a command, lament or promise means more once the reader knows which act it belongs to.[^s12] Wright argues that because so much of the Bible is narrative, its authority works through its story rather than as a rulebook or a list of propositions.[^s12] Seeing the whole also guards against neglecting the Old Testament: the Catechism notes that the church has always rejected the idea that the New Testament makes the Old obsolete.[^s10]

Common misreadings come from losing the plot: treating the Bible as unconnected moral tales, skipping the Old Testament, or lifting a promise made to Israel and applying it to oneself without asking where it sits in the story. The opposite mistake is to learn the outline and stop reading the texts; the outline is only a map.[^s12][^s10]

## Connections

The view of Scripture as one story centred on Christ is **held by** the Catholic Church, whose Catechism speaks of the two Testaments as one divine plan,[^s10] and by Eastern Orthodoxy, whose introduction to the Old Testament traces creation, fall, promise and preparation to the Incarnation.[^s11] It is **explained by** the biblical canon, because the arrangement of the books, and especially which book closes the Old Testament, shapes how the story is heard.[^s13] It is **supported by** the road to Emmaus in Luke 24, where the risen Jesus reads Moses and all the prophets as speaking about himself.[^s4] Its stages and parts are traced in related articles on the exodus, the exile, the kingdom of God and the two Testaments.[^s11][^s10]
