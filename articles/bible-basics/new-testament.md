# New Testament

The **New Testament** is the second and shorter part of the Christian Bible: 27 books that tell of Jesus Christ and the first generations of his followers.[^s7] Its central subject, as the *Catechism of the Catholic Church* puts it, is Jesus himself, his teaching, death and glorification, and the beginnings of his church under the Holy Spirit.[^s12] Christians read it together with the Old Testament as one story of God's saving purpose.[^s12][^s7]

## Why the name?

"Testament" here means covenant. At the Last Supper Jesus took the cup and said, "This cup is the new covenant in my blood, which is poured out for you" (Luke 22:20, WEB).[^s1] The Letter to the Hebrews quotes at length the promise in Jeremiah that God would "make a new covenant with the house of Israel" (Hebrews 8:8, WEB), and calls Jesus the mediator of "a better covenant" (Hebrews 8:6).[^s2] So the writings that bear witness to that covenant came to be called the books of the new covenant, or New Testament.[^s7]

## Books included

Catholic, Orthodox and Protestant Bibles share the same 27 books, though the Old Testament differs between them.[^s12][^s13][^s8] They are arranged by kind rather than by date of writing:[^s7]

- **Gospels (4):** Matthew, Mark, Luke and John, accounts of Jesus' life, teaching, death and resurrection.
- **History (1):** the Acts of the Apostles, the spread of the good news from Jerusalem across the Roman world.
- **Letters of Paul (13):** Romans, 1–2 Corinthians, Galatians, Ephesians, Philippians, Colossians, 1–2 Thessalonians, 1–2 Timothy, Titus and Philemon.
- **Hebrews (1):** placed after Paul's letters; older lists counted it among them, though its author is unnamed.
- **Catholic or general letters (7):** James, 1–2 Peter, 1–3 John and Jude.
- **Prophecy (1):** Revelation, an apocalyptic account of God's intervention in history.[^s11][^s13][^s9][^s7]

## How it was written and gathered

The New Testament was written in Greek, which the Westminster Confession notes was the language then most widely known among the nations.[^s13] More precisely it is *koine*, the everyday Greek of the eastern Roman world.[^s15] Many scholars think Paul's letters are the earliest of its writings, beginning with 1 Thessalonians around AD 50, and that the Gospels followed, with Mark often dated around 70.[^s10] Bible Odyssey places the whole collection, by various authors, between about AD 50 and 120; dating and authorship are discussed book by book, and traditional and scholarly views on them differ for some books.[^s11][^s7]

The texts themselves show how they began. Luke says he carefully traced events handed down by "eyewitnesses and servants of the word" so that Theophilus might know the certainty of what he had been taught (Luke 1:1-4, WEB).[^s3] Paul asked that his letter to Colossae also be read in the assembly at Laodicea (Colossians 4:16).[^s4] And 2 Peter already speaks of "all" Paul's letters and sets them alongside "the other Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:15-16, WEB).[^s5]

For several centuries the books circulated separately and churches discussed which belonged in the collection.[^s11][^s10] In his Easter letter of 367, Athanasius of Alexandria listed exactly the 27 books we have and called them "fountains of salvation."[^s9] Even then, Greek churches still debated Revelation for a time, and the Syriac Peshitta long used a shorter list of 22 books.[^s8] The Biblical Canon article tells that story more fully.

## Why it matters

The New Testament is the church's earliest witness to Jesus. John states its purpose plainly: "these are written, that you may believe that Jesus is the Christ, the Son of God, and that believing you may have life in his name" (John 20:31, WEB).[^s6] The *Catechism* calls the Gospels "the heart of all the Scriptures."[^s12] The Westminster Confession, a Reformed Protestant standard, likewise lists the 27 books among the Scriptures given by inspiration of God to be the rule of faith and life.[^s13]

## Common misreadings

**"The New Testament replaced the Old."** The New Testament constantly quotes Israel's Scriptures; Hebrews 8 builds its argument on Jeremiah's words.[^s2] Hebrews does say the first covenant is made "obsolete" (Hebrews 8:13, WEB), but the church has always read both Testaments together: in an old saying of Augustine's that the *Catechism* quotes, the New lies hidden in the Old and the Old is unveiled in the New.[^s2][^s12]

**"The books are in the order they were written."** They are not. The order is by kind of writing, and Paul's letters, printed after the Gospels and Acts, are generally thought to be older than them.[^s10][^s7]

**"A council invented the New Testament."** The books were in use and widely received long before any list was fixed; Athanasius's letter and later councils recognised a collection the churches were already reading.[^s9][^s11][^s8]

## Connections

The New Testament **complements** the Old Testament: the *Catechism* teaches that the two are one plan of God and must each be read in the light of the other.[^s12] It **teaches** the new covenant that Jesus announced at the Last Supper and Hebrews explains,[^s1][^s2] and it **quotes** the Old Testament, as Hebrews 8 quotes Jeremiah.[^s2] Its contents are **corroborated by** Athanasius's Festal Letter 39, the earliest surviving list of exactly the 27 books.[^s9][^s10] It is **preserved in** Codex Sinaiticus, copied in the mid-fourth century, which contains the earliest complete copy of the New Testament.[^s14][^s15]
