# Old Testament

The **Old Testament** is the first and much the larger part of the Christian Bible: the Scriptures of ancient Israel, from Genesis to Malachi. These were the Scriptures of Jesus and the apostles. On the road to Emmaus the risen Jesus, "beginning from Moses and from all the prophets," explained to two disciples what all the Scriptures said about him (Luke 24:27, WEB), and later he spoke of what was written of him in "the law of Moses," the prophets and the psalms (Luke 24:44).[^s1]

## Why the name?

"Testament" comes through Latin from the Hebrew *berit*, "covenant," so the two parts of the Christian Bible are named for God's covenant with Israel and the new covenant in Christ.[^s10] The language goes back to Jeremiah, where God promises "a new covenant with the house of Israel, and with the house of Judah," with his law written on their hearts (Jeremiah 31:31-33, WEB).[^s4] Paul speaks of the public "reading of the old covenant" in the synagogue (2 Corinthians 3:14, WEB), having just called himself a servant "of a new covenant."[^s2] Melito, a second-century bishop of Sardis, wrote that he had travelled east to learn exactly "the books of the Old Testament" and sent a list of them; the historian Eusebius preserved his letter.[^s14] Britannica credits Melito, around AD 170, with first using the name for the collection.[^s8]

"Old" here means first and foundational, not obsolete. Because the phrase can sound as if Israel's Bible had been set aside, many scholars and some churches also use "Hebrew Bible," "Hebrew Scriptures" or "First Testament."[^s10][^s7]

## Books included

Protestant Bibles contain 39 Old Testament books, the list given in the Westminster Confession.[^s12] They are usually grouped as:

- **Law (Pentateuch):** Genesis, Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers, Deuteronomy.
- **History:** Joshua, Judges, Ruth, 1–2 Samuel, 1–2 Kings, 1–2 Chronicles, Ezra, Nehemiah, Esther.
- **Poetry and wisdom:** Job, Psalms, Proverbs, Ecclesiastes, Song of Songs.
- **Prophets:** Isaiah, Jeremiah, Lamentations, Ezekiel, Daniel and the twelve shorter prophets, Hosea to Malachi.[^s12]

Catholic Bibles have 46 Old Testament books, counting Jeremiah and Lamentations separately, because they also include deuterocanonical books such as Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon and the Maccabees.[^s11][^s7] Orthodox Old Testaments follow the Greek Septuagint and include books such as Sirach and the Maccabees, along with additions to Daniel.[^s13]

The Jewish Bible, the **Tanakh**, contains the same books as the Protestant Old Testament but counts and orders them differently. Its name is an acronym of its three parts, Torah (Teaching), Nevi'im (Prophets) and Ketuvim (Writings). It counts 24 books, treating the twelve minor prophets as one "Book of the Twelve" and leaving Samuel, Kings, Chronicles and Ezra–Nehemiah undivided.[^s8][^s10] The Christian Old Testament places Ruth beside Judges, because her great-grandson is David, and ends with Malachi's promise that Elijah will return before the day of the Lord, which leads naturally into the Gospels. The Tanakh places Ruth among the Writings, with the scrolls read at Jewish festivals.[^s7]

## Languages and text

Most of the Old Testament was written in Hebrew; the Westminster Confession calls Hebrew its original, authentic language.[^s12] A few parts are in Aramaic: a long section of Daniel, two passages in Ezra and a single verse of Jeremiah.[^s9] Readers can see the switch in Daniel 2:4, where the Chaldeans answer the king "in the Syrian language" (WEB), that is, Aramaic, and the text continues in that language.[^s6][^s9] Christians read the Old Testament through two main textual streams. The Masoretic Text is the traditional Hebrew text, and the Septuagint is the ancient Greek translation that New Testament quotations usually follow.[^s7][^s8]

## Why it matters

The Old Testament was the Bible of the early church. Timothy had known "the sacred writings" since infancy, and Paul says they can make a person "wise for salvation through faith" (2 Timothy 3:15, WEB).[^s3] He also told the Romans that "whatever things were written before were written for our learning," so that believers might have hope (Romans 15:4, WEB).[^s5] Without it, New Testament words such as covenant, sacrifice, kingdom, Messiah and exile would make little sense.

## Views across traditions

All Christian traditions receive the Old Testament as God's word and reject any claim that the New Testament has made it void.[^s11][^s12]

**Catholic.** The *Catechism* calls the Old Testament an indispensable part of Scripture whose books keep a permanent value, because the old covenant has never been revoked. It teaches that the Old Testament prepares for Christ and contains a treasury of prayer.[^s11]

**Protestant.** The Westminster Confession lists 39 books as inspired, treats the Hebrew text as authentic and holds that the Apocrypha are not part of the canon.[^s12]

**Eastern Orthodox.** The Orthodox Church uses the Septuagint as its official Old Testament text, as the early Christians and the Fathers did, so its Old Testament includes the books found in that Greek tradition.[^s13]

Each tradition explains its list in its own terms, and the differences go back to the early church's use of both the Hebrew and the Greek forms of Israel's Scriptures; the Biblical Canon article traces how the lists took shape.[^s7][^s13]

## Common misreadings

Some readers skip the Old Testament as a stern prelude, or treat it as a book about a different God. The New Testament writers did not: they found Christ in Moses, the prophets and the psalms.[^s1] Christians should also remember that these books remain the living Scripture of the Jewish people, read in synagogues today in their own order.[^s7]

## Connections

The Old Testament is **preserved in** the Septuagint, the Greek translation made in the third and second centuries BC,[^s8] and in the Masoretic Text, the Hebrew text that differs from the Septuagint in wording and length in places.[^s7] It **contrasts with** the Tanakh, which shares much of its content but differs in order, emphasis and, for Catholic and Orthodox Christians, contents.[^s7] It **teaches** the new covenant, promised in Jeremiah 31.[^s4] It **complements** the New Testament, which the *Catechism* says it prepares for and which does not make it void.[^s11] It is **corroborated by** Eusebius's *Church History*, which preserves Melito's early list of its books.[^s14]
