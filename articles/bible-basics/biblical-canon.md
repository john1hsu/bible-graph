# Biblical Canon

The **canon** is the list of books a church receives as Holy Scripture. The word comes from the Greek *kanōn*, a rod or measuring stick, and came to mean a closed collection of writings set apart by a believing community as its standard.[^s5] When Christians open a Bible and find Genesis at the front and Revelation at the back, they are holding the result of a long, prayerful process of recognition that ran through Israel's history and the first centuries of the church.[^s5][^s6]

## Why it matters

Christians confess that "every Scripture is God-breathed" and is useful for teaching and correction (2 Timothy 3:16, WEB).[^s4] The canon answers the practical question that confession raises: *which* writings are Scripture? Knowing how the list came together helps readers trust the Bible they hold, understand why a Catholic, Orthodox or Protestant Bible may contain a different number of Old Testament books, and talk about those differences with accuracy and charity rather than suspicion.[^s5][^s9]

## In Scripture itself

The Bible never prints a table of contents, but it shows the canon taking shape. After his resurrection Jesus tells his disciples that everything written about him "in the law of Moses, the prophets, and the psalms" must be fulfilled (Luke 24:44, WEB) — a three-part description that echoes the way Israel's Scriptures were already grouped.[^s1][^s5] In the New Testament, 2 Peter mentions the letters of "our beloved brother Paul" and warns that some people twist them "as they also do to the other Scriptures" (2 Peter 3:15-16, WEB), placing Paul's letters alongside Israel's sacred writings.[^s3][^s2]

## How the canon took shape

**The Hebrew Bible.** The Jewish canon developed in Hebrew and is arranged as the Tanakh: Torah (Law), Nevi'im (Prophets) and Ketuvim (Writings). The Torah was probably the first collection to be treated as authoritative, and the prologue to Sirach in the second century BC already speaks of the Law and the Prophets alongside "the other books." Some books, including Esther, the Song of Songs, Ecclesiastes and Ezekiel, were discussed before their place was settled.[^s5]

**The Christian Old Testament.** The earliest Christians used the Septuagint, a Greek collection that also contained books such as Tobit, Judith and Baruch. These later came to be called *deuterocanonical* ("second canon"). Every Christian Old Testament still follows the Septuagint's order, grouping the books as history, poetry and prophecy.[^s5]

**The New Testament.** In the second century the teacher Marcion accepted only a form of Luke and ten letters of Paul and rejected the rest, including the Old Testament; the church judged his view heretical, and that controversy pushed Christians to say clearly which writings they received.[^s6][^s5] The Muratorian Fragment, probably from Rome near the end of that century, lists the Gospels, Acts, Paul's letters, Revelation and several shorter letters, while omitting Hebrews, James and Peter's letters.[^s6] The church historian Eusebius later still distinguished acknowledged books from disputed ones such as James, Jude, 2 Peter and 2 John, with Revelation uncertain.[^s6]

In his thirty-ninth Easter letter, Athanasius of Alexandria listed the 27 books of today's New Testament and called the canonical books "fountains of salvation." He counted 22 Old Testament books, matching the letters of the Hebrew alphabet, and named others — Wisdom, Sirach, Esther, Judith, Tobit, the *Didache* and the *Shepherd* — as worth reading but not canonical.[^s7][^s6] A council at Carthage in 397 issued a similar list and restricted public reading in church to canonical Scripture.[^s6]

## Views across traditions

All three great traditions receive the same 27 books of the New Testament and affirm, against Marcion, that the Old Testament is truly God's word.[^s6][^s9] They differ over the Old Testament.

**Catholic.** At its fourth session on 8 April 1546 the Council of Trent named the canonical books, including Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and 1–2 Maccabees, and received them "in their entirety and with all their parts."[^s8] The *Catechism* teaches that the Church discerned the canon by apostolic Tradition and counts 46 Old Testament and 27 New Testament books.[^s9]

**Eastern Orthodox.** The Orthodox Church in America describes a "longer canon" drawn from the Septuagint, accepted by the early church: Tobit, Judith, additions to Esther and Daniel, the Maccabees, Wisdom, Sirach, Baruch and the Prayer of Manasseh. It does not call these books Apocrypha, the usual Protestant term.[^s12]

**Protestant.** The sixteenth-century Reformers did not receive the deuterocanonical books as Scripture.[^s5] The Westminster Confession lists 39 Old and 27 New Testament books as inspired and "the rule of faith and life," and holds that the Apocrypha are no part of the canon.[^s10] The Anglican Articles of Religion take a middle path: the church reads those books "for example of life and instruction of manners" but does not use them to establish doctrine.[^s11]

Each tradition sees itself as receiving, not inventing, the books God gave; the differences concern which books belong to that gift.[^s9][^s10][^s12]

## Common misreadings

The canon was not drawn up by a single committee at a single meeting; it was recognised gradually as books proved themselves in the worship and teaching of the church over several centuries.[^s5] Nor do differences in Old Testament length mean that one side simply "added" or "removed" books on a whim: the variation goes back to the early church's use of the Greek Septuagint alongside the Hebrew canon.[^s5][^s12]

## Connections

The canon is **explained by** the Septuagint, the Greek Old Testament whose extra books underlie the longer Catholic and Orthodox canons.[^s5] It is **supported by** Athanasius's thirty-ninth festal letter, a landmark witness that names the 27 New Testament books.[^s7] Its formation is **explained** in part by the challenge of Marcion, whose shortened Scripture pushed the church to define its own.[^s6] It is closely related to the deuterocanonical books, the Old and New Testaments, and the inspiration of Scripture.[^s4][^s9]
