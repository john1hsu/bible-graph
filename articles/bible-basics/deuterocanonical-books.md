# Deuterocanonical Books

Open a Catholic or Orthodox Bible beside most Protestant ones and you will find extra pages in the Old Testament: Tobit, Judith, the Wisdom of Solomon, Sirach (also called Ecclesiasticus), Baruch, 1 and 2 Maccabees, and longer forms of Esther and Daniel.[^s6][^s13] These are the deuterocanonical books. They were written by Jews in the centuries just before Christ, came into the church through the Greek Old Testament, and are not part of the Hebrew Bible read in the synagogue.[^s6][^s11]

The names carry history. *Deuterocanonical* ("of the second canon") is a later, Catholic term; one scholarly account traces it to Sixtus of Siena in 1566.[^s10][^s9] *Apocrypha* ("hidden" or "set aside") was used by early church writers, and Protestants took it over for these books, where it gradually gained a negative ring.[^s10][^s11] Orthodox Christians often call them *anagignoskomena*, the books that are "readable", a description going back to Athanasius's thirty-ninth festal letter of 367.[^s8][^s9]

## Why it matters

These books are the reason Christian Bibles differ in length, so knowing what they are helps believers understand one another's Scriptures without suspicion.[^s11] They also open a window on the faith of Israel between Malachi and Matthew: love for the Law and the Temple, prayer for the dead, hope in the resurrection and wisdom about everyday life, in the world into which Jesus was born.[^s5][^s3][^s1][^s7]

## Books included

The Catholic Old Testament has 46 books, seven more than the Hebrew canon: **Tobit**, **Judith**, **Wisdom**, **Sirach**, **Baruch**, **1 Maccabees** and **2 Maccabees**, along with Greek additions to **Esther** and **Daniel** (such as the stories of Susanna and of Bel and the Dragon).[^s13][^s6] The Council of Trent named these books in 1546 and received them "with all their parts".[^s12]

Orthodox Bibles, which follow the Greek Septuagint, include all of these and a few more.[^s11][^s8] Greek Christian Bibles linked to the Septuagint also carried shorter works such as the Prayer of Manasseh, Psalm 151 and the Letter of Jeremiah.[^s10] The Anglican Articles list a similar wider set of "other books", including 3 and 4 Esdras and the Prayer of Manasses.[^s15]

The books differ in genre and origin. Tobit and Judith are stories, Wisdom and Sirach are wisdom books, and 1 and 2 Maccabees are histories of the Maccabean struggle led by Judas Maccabeus and his brothers.[^s6][^s7][^s1] Some were written in Hebrew or Aramaic, others in Greek: Wisdom and 2 Maccabees were composed in Greek, while Baruch and 1 Maccabees go back to Hebrew originals.[^s6]

**Sirach** is the best dated. Its author names himself "Jesus, the son of Sirach Eleazar, of Jerusalem" (Sirach 50:27, WEB), and he praises the high priest Simon son of Onias in chapter 50.[^s3] His grandson translated the book into Greek after coming to Egypt "in the eight and thirtieth year" of King Euergetes (KJV prologue), about 132 BC, and noted that his grandfather had studied "the law, and the prophets, and other books of our fathers".[^s5][^s7] Hebrew manuscripts of Sirach have been recovered from the Cairo Geniza and from Qumran.[^s7]

**1 Maccabees** describes a time of distress worse than any since prophets ceased to appear in Israel (1 Maccabees 9:27).[^s4] **2 Maccabees** tells how Judas Maccabeus, finding pagan tokens on fallen soldiers, prayed that their sin be blotted out and sent a collection to Jerusalem for a sin offering, because he expected the dead to rise (2 Maccabees 12:38-45).[^s1] The NABRE notes call this the earliest statement that prayer and sacrifice for the dead are effective; translations number the closing verse as 45 or 46.[^s2][^s1]

## Views across traditions

**Catholic.** The Catholic Church holds that apostolic Tradition guided the church in discerning which writings belong in the canon, and it lists the seven books among the 46 of the Old Testament.[^s13] The Council of Trent defined this list as sacred and canonical.[^s12] The Septuagint was the vehicle through which these books reached the church.[^s6]

**Orthodox.** Orthodoxy has no single clear conciliar statement on the Old Testament canon, but the Septuagint serves as its quasi-official form, so the *anagignoskomena* are read in church and printed in its Bibles.[^s8] The Synod of Jerusalem in 1672, in the Confession of Dositheus, expressly named books such as Wisdom, Judith, Tobit, Sirach and the Maccabees within Scripture.[^s16] Orthodox scholars still discuss exactly how these books relate to the core canon.[^s9]

**Lutheran.** Luther's German Bible kept these books but moved them into a separate section headed "Apocrypha", set between the Old and New Testaments rather than among the canonical books.[^s11][^s17]

**Anglican.** Article VI says the church reads these other books, as Jerome taught, for example of life and instruction of manners, but does not use them to establish doctrine.[^s15]

**Reformed and most other Protestants.** The Westminster Confession holds that the Apocrypha are not divinely inspired, are no part of the canon, and may be used only like other human writings.[^s14] English Bibles such as the Geneva Bible and the King James Version still printed them, but from the late 1600s Protestant English Bibles increasingly left them out.[^s11]

## Connections

These books are **preserved in** the [Septuagint](bg:septuagint), the Greek Old Testament that carried them into the church.[^s6] They are **held** as Scripture by the [Catholic Church](bg:catholic-church)[^s13] and by [Eastern Orthodoxy](bg:eastern-orthodoxy),[^s8] and they are **challenged** by the [Westminster Confession of Faith](bg:westminster-confession-of-faith), which excludes them from the canon.[^s14] 2 Maccabees **teaches** hope in the [resurrection of the dead](bg:resurrection-of-the-dead).[^s1] Books such as Sirach are **explained by** their setting in [Second Temple Judaism](bg:second-temple-judaism).[^s7] They belong under the larger question of the [biblical canon](bg:biblical-canon) and sit beside the [Old Testament](bg:old-testament), the [Masoretic Text](bg:masoretic-text) and the [Book of Wisdom](bg:book-of-wisdom).[^s6][^s13]
