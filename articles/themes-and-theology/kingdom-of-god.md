# Kingdom of God

The Kingdom of God is God's own reign as King: his saving rule breaking into a world that has resisted him, and the new order of life that rule creates. Jesus opened his ministry in Galilee "preaching the gospel of the kingdom of God" and announcing, "The time is fulfilled, and the kingdom of God is at hand! Repent, and believe in the gospel" (Mark 1:14-15, WEB).[^s1] Bible dictionaries agree that the phrase is central to Jesus's teaching in the first three Gospels, where it or an equivalent occurs roughly eighty times.[^s12][^s14][^s15]

The Greek *basileia* (like Hebrew and Aramaic *malkut*) means first of all kingship or reign, God's active ruling, rather than a territory on a map; "the rule of God" is often the clearest English.[^s12][^s14][^s15] "Kingdom of Heaven", Matthew's usual wording (about thirty-two times, against four uses of "Kingdom of God"), means the same thing: Jewish usage often said "Heaven" out of reverence instead of naming God.[^s12][^s13][^s14]

## Why it matters

The Kingdom is the frame for the whole gospel story. Jesus's healings, his welcome of sinners, his parables and his call to discipleship are all presented as the arrival of God's reign, and every Christian who prays the Lord's Prayer asks, "Let your Kingdom come. Let your will be done, as in heaven, so on earth" (Matthew 6:10, WEB).[^s5][^s12] Reference works across the traditions stress that the Kingdom is God's gift and God's work, received in repentance and faith, not a human achievement.[^s14][^s15][^s16]

## Through the Bible

**God the King in the Old Testament.** The exact phrase is rare in the Old Testament, but the idea is everywhere. God is King of all creation and of every nation: "Your kingdom is an everlasting kingdom. Your dominion endures throughout all generations" (Psalm 145:13, WEB).[^s7][^s12] He is also Israel's true King, and the prophets look ahead to the day his reign is made plain. Isaiah pictures a messenger bringing good news "who says to Zion, 'Your God reigns!'" (Isaiah 52:7, WEB).[^s8][^s12] Daniel, central to this hope, foresees that "the God of heaven" will "set up a kingdom which shall never be destroyed" (Daniel 2:44, WEB).[^s6][^s12]

**Jewish hope in Jesus's day.** By the time of Jesus, Jewish prayer and teaching spoke of "the Kingdom of Heaven" (*malkut shamayim*). The Jewish Encyclopedia explains that reciting the Shema each day was understood as taking on "the yoke of the Kingdom", and that prayers such as the Kaddish ask God to establish his Kingdom soon; under Roman rule that longing grew more intense.[^s13] Jesus did not reject this shared Jewish hope; he announced it as being fulfilled.[^s12][^s13]

**Jesus: already present.** Jesus taught that God's reign was arriving in his own ministry: "if I by the finger of God cast out demons, then the kingdom of God has come to you" (Luke 11:20, WEB).[^s4] His parables show it starting small and growing by God's power, like seed that sprouts while the farmer sleeps, "he doesn't know how", or a tiny mustard seed that becomes a shelter for birds (Mark 4:26-32, WEB).[^s2] To his disciples is given "the mystery of the kingdom of God" (Mark 4:11, WEB).[^s2] The Catholic Catechism likewise notes that Jesus taught the Kingdom chiefly through parables, which call for a radical choice.[^s16]

**Jesus: still to come.** Yet Jesus also taught his followers to pray for its coming (Matthew 6:10) and to live ready for it.[^s5][^s12] Before Pilate he said, "My Kingdom is not of this world" (John 18:36, WEB): its power does not come from earthly force.[^s10] Paul describes it as "righteousness, peace, and joy in the Holy Spirit" (Romans 14:17, WEB), and Revelation looks to the day when the kingdom of the world becomes "the kingdom of our Lord, and of his Christ" (Revelation 11:15, ASV).[^s9][^s11]

## How interpreters have read it

Scholars once argued over whether Jesus thought the Kingdom was wholly future and imminent (Johannes Weiss, Albert Schweitzer) or already fully present (C. H. Dodd). Most now hold the two sides together: the Kingdom has been inaugurated in Jesus but awaits its consummation, a pattern often called "already and not yet".[^s12][^s14][^s15] Robert Stein warns that stressing only the "already" breeds triumphalism, while stressing only the "not yet" breeds defeatism.[^s14] Christian traditions broadly see the church as the community that lives under God's reign without simply equating it with the Kingdom; the Catholic Catechism, for example, calls the church the "seed and the beginning" of the Kingdom, whose fulfilment comes only with Christ's return.[^s12][^s15][^s17]

## Common misreadings

- **"Kingdom of Heaven" means going to heaven when we die.** In Matthew it is simply a reverent way of saying "Kingdom of God": God's reign coming to earth, not only a place the soul goes.[^s12][^s13]
- **"The Kingdom of God is within you" means a private inner feeling.** Older versions such as the KJV and WEB read "within you" in Luke 17:21, but Jesus is speaking to Pharisees, and many modern translations render the Greek "in your midst" or "among you": the Kingdom was present in Jesus himself.[^s3][^s18][^s12]
- **We build the Kingdom by our own efforts.** The parables of growth stress that God gives the growth; disciples receive the Kingdom, pray for its coming and live by it.[^s2][^s14][^s15]

## Connections

The Kingdom of God belongs to **the story of the Bible** as a whole, running from God's kingship in the Psalms and Prophets to its fulfilment in Revelation.[^s7][^s11][^s12] It is **explained by Second Temple Judaism**, whose prayers and hopes for *malkut shamayim* form the setting of Jesus's announcement.[^s13] It is **explained by the Book of Daniel**, whose vision of an everlasting kingdom given by God shapes the New Testament's language.[^s6][^s12] It is **studied in biblical theology**, which traces it across both Testaments.[^s14] It is closely related to the **Sermon on the Mount** and the **Lord's Prayer**, which teach life under God's reign, to the **parables of Jesus**, his main way of teaching it, and to the **Messiah**, the King through whom it comes.[^s5][^s12][^s16]
