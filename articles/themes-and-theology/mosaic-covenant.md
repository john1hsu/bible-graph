# Mosaic Covenant

The Mosaic covenant is the covenant God made with the whole people of Israel through Moses after bringing them out of Egypt. It was mediated at Mount Sinai, where Israel received the law, including the Ten Commandments, and it is also called the "old" or "first" covenant in contrast with the new covenant Jeremiah promised.[^s16] The giving of the law at Sinai is the event; the Mosaic covenant is the lasting relationship that event created, with its terms, its sign, its blessings and curses, and its long history of renewal, breach and hope.[^s1][^s2][^s19]

## Why it matters

Much of the Old Testament is lived inside this covenant. The laws of Exodus, Leviticus, Numbers and Deuteronomy are its terms, the historical books measure kings and people by it, and the prophets call Israel back to it.[^s17][^s9] The New Testament cannot be understood without it either: Jesus says he came "not to destroy, but to fulfill" the law (Matthew 5:17, WEB), and Paul, 2 Corinthians and Hebrews all explain the gospel by setting the new covenant beside the old.[^s11][^s13][^s15] Reading this covenant well guards against two opposite errors: treating God's law as a way to earn his love, and treating the Old Testament as a dark background with nothing to say to Christians.[^s5][^s20]

## Biblical basis

**Rescue first, then covenant.** At Sinai God reminds Israel that he has already carried them out of Egypt, then offers that if they keep his covenant they will be his own possession, a kingdom of priests and a holy nation; the people answer, "All that Yahweh has spoken we will do" (Exodus 19:3-8, WEB).[^s1] Deuteronomy insists the choice rested on love, not merit: the Lord did not choose Israel for being numerous, "but because Yahweh loves you" and kept his oath to the fathers (Deuteronomy 7:7-8, WEB).[^s5]

**Sealed in blood.** Moses reads "the book of the covenant" aloud, the people pledge obedience, and he sprinkles them with "the blood of the covenant" (Exodus 24:3-8, WEB).[^s2]

**A sign.** The Sabbath is "a sign between me and you throughout your generations", kept "for a perpetual covenant", recalling both creation and the God who makes Israel holy (Exodus 31:13-17, WEB).[^s3] The Jewish Encyclopedia notes that Jewish tradition calls the Sabbath the everlasting sign of the Sinai covenant.[^s19]

**Blessings and curses.** Faithfulness brings rain, harvest and God's presence, "I will walk among you, and will be your God" (Leviticus 26:12, WEB); breaking the covenant brings judgment and exile (26:14-15).[^s4] Yet even in exile God will "remember my covenant with Jacob" and with Isaac and Abraham, and will not reject them (26:42-45, WEB).[^s4] Deuteronomy sets "life and death, the blessing and the curse" before the people and urges them to "choose life" (30:19, WEB), promising that after exile God will gather them and "circumcise your heart" (30:1-6, WEB).[^s7]

## Through the Bible

The covenant is renewed generation by generation. On the plains of Moab, Moses makes a covenant "besides the covenant which he made with them in Horeb", embracing children, foreigners and those "not here with us this day" (Deuteronomy 29:1, 10-15, WEB).[^s6] At Shechem, Joshua calls the tribes to choose whom they will serve, makes a covenant, and sets up a stone as witness (Joshua 24:14-27).[^s8] King Josiah reads "the book of the covenant" in the Temple and leads the people to stand to it (2 Kings 23:1-3, WEB).[^s9] The Jewish Encyclopedia counts four such renewals: under Moses, Joshua, the priest Jehoiada and Josiah.[^s19]

The prophets press its demands. Jeremiah grieves that Israel broke the covenant made at the Exodus, "although I was a husband to them", and promises a new covenant with the law written on the heart and sins remembered no more (Jeremiah 31:31-34, WEB).[^s10]

In the New Testament, Paul lists "the covenants, the giving of the law" among Israel's gifts (Romans 9:4, WEB).[^s12] He argues that the law, coming 430 years after the promise to Abraham, cannot cancel it, and served as "our tutor to bring us to Christ" (Galatians 3:17, 24, WEB).[^s14] In 2 Corinthians 3 he contrasts a ministry written on stone with the Spirit's ministry written on hearts, and speaks of reading "the old covenant".[^s13] Hebrews quotes Jeremiah in full and concludes that calling one covenant "new" has made "the first obsolete" (Hebrews 8:6-13, WEB).[^s15]

## Common misreadings

- **"It was salvation by works."** The covenant rests on God's prior rescue and love (Exodus 19:4; Deuteronomy 7:7-8).[^s1][^s5]
- **"Old covenant means a God without mercy."** Mercy after failure is written into the covenant itself (Leviticus 26:44-45; Deuteronomy 30:6).[^s4][^s7]
- **"Obsolete means God has rejected Israel."** Paul still names the covenants as belonging to the Israelites (Romans 9:4).[^s12]

## Scholarly views

George Mendenhall (1954) argued that Hittite vassal treaties share the covenant's pattern: a historical prologue, stipulations, witnesses, blessings and curses, and the deposit and reading of the text.[^s18] Moshe Weinfeld noted that Exodus 19-24 tells of making a covenant rather than reproducing a treaty, found the pattern clearest in Deuteronomy, and linked Deuteronomy 28's curses to Esarhaddon's treaty of 672 BCE.[^s18] He distinguished this "obligatory" covenant from the "promissory" ones with Abraham and David.[^s18] Kenneth Kitchen instead dates Deuteronomy's form to late second-millennium treaties, a view the *Zondervan Encyclopedia of the Bible* supports; it also sides with John Murray, against Mendenhall's reading of Sinai as purely conditional, that the covenant is grounded in grace.[^s17]

## Views across traditions

Christians agree that this covenant came from God, that its law is holy, and that it points to Christ; they differ on how its law binds believers.[^s20][^s23]

- **Catholic.** The *Catechism* calls the Old Law "the first stage of revealed Law", summed up in the Ten Commandments. It is holy but imperfect: like a tutor it shows what to do without giving the Spirit's strength, and it is "a pedagogy and a prophecy" preparing for the Gospel (CCC 1962-1964).[^s20]
- **Eastern Orthodox.** St Philaret's *Longer Catechism* teaches that the outward law given through Moses at Sinai recalled the law of conscience that people had stifled. Christians are bound by the Commandments, which Christ taught them to fulfil more perfectly (questions 487-491).[^s21]
- **Lutheran.** The *Formula of Concord* teaches that the law restrains, reveals sin and guides the regenerate. Believers are freed from its curse and coercion, but still need it as a rule for life (Epitome VI).[^s22]
- **Reformed.** The *Westminster Confession* teaches that the moral law given on Sinai binds everyone forever. The ceremonial laws are abrogated and the judicial laws expired with Israel's state, and believers are not under the law to be justified or condemned (chapter 19).[^s23]

**A Jewish reading.** For Judaism the Sinai covenant remains in force; the Jewish Encyclopedia states that Judaism knows no covenant other than this one.[^s19]

## Connections

It is an **instance of** biblical **covenant**, made through Moses with Israel.[^s16] Jeremiah **contrasts** it with the **New Covenant**, which is "not according to" it (Jeremiah 31:32).[^s10] Its form is **explained by** ancient Near Eastern vassal treaties.[^s18] It grows out of the giving of the law at Sinai, includes the Ten Commandments, is taught at length in Deuteronomy, and stands beside the earlier **Abrahamic covenant** (Galatians 3:17).[^s2][^s6][^s14]
