# Grace

Grace is God's free and undeserved favour toward people, and the gift that flows from it. Bible dictionaries define it as God's unmerited favour, freely given rather than earned, and Paul's letters use it so often for God's saving work in Christ that it becomes almost another word for salvation.[^s11][^s12] Its classic summary is Ephesians 2:8-9: "for by grace you have been saved through faith, and that not of yourselves; it is the gift of God, not of works" (WEB).[^s7]

The New Testament word is Greek *charis*, which first meant charm or attractiveness, then a favour or gift, and then thanks for a gift; the New Testament fills it with the meaning of what God has done in Christ.[^s11] The main Old Testament word is Hebrew *chen*, "favour", which the Greek translators usually rendered *charis*.[^s11][^s12]

## Why it matters

Grace answers a basic question: on what basis does a holy God welcome sinful people? Scripture's answer is that God takes the initiative. Christians of every major tradition confess that salvation begins with God's free gift, not with human merit, even where they explain the details differently.[^s13][^s14][^s15] Grace is pardon, but also the power by which God sustains his people.[^s8][^s9][^s12]

## Through the Bible

**Grace before the New Testament.** Grace is not a New Testament novelty. In the darkness before the Flood, "Noah found favor in Yahweh's eyes" (Genesis 6:8, WEB).[^s2][^s12] After Israel's worship of the golden calf, God reveals his name to Moses as "a merciful and gracious God, slow to anger, and abundant in loving kindness and truth" (Exodus 34:6, WEB).[^s1] Baker's dictionary stresses that God's favour in the Old Testament is just as free and sovereign as in the New.[^s12] These verses are equally treasured in Judaism: the "Thirteen Attributes of Mercy" drawn from this passage stand at the heart of the penitential *Selichot* prayers and are repeated on Yom Kippur.[^s20]

**Grace in Jesus.** John's Gospel says, "For the law was given through Moses; grace and truth came through Jesus Christ" (John 1:17, ASV).[^s3] Jesus is not recorded using *charis* as a technical term, but his ministry and parables, such as the workers in the vineyard and the great supper, picture God's undeserved generosity to people who could not earn it.[^s11][^s12]

**Grace in Paul.** Paul greets and blesses his readers with grace,[^s11][^s12] and teaches that people are "justified freely by his grace through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Romans 3:24, WEB), and that "where sin abounded, grace abounded more exceedingly" (Romans 5:20, WEB).[^s4][^s5] Grace for Paul is also strength: the risen Lord tells him, "My grace is sufficient for you, for my power is made perfect in weakness" (2 Corinthians 12:9, WEB).[^s9]

**Grace that changes lives.** Grace is never permission to keep sinning. Paul raises the question himself, "Shall we continue in sin, that grace may abound?", and answers, "May it never be!" (Romans 6:1-2, WEB).[^s6] Ephesians says believers are God's "workmanship, created in Christ Jesus for good works" (Ephesians 2:10, WEB), and Titus describes the grace of God "bringing salvation to all men" and teaching believers to live self-controlled, upright and devout lives now (Titus 2:11-14).[^s7][^s8][^s12]

## Common misreadings

- **"The Old Testament is law, the New Testament is grace."** God's favour runs through the Old Testament, and God names himself "gracious" at Sinai itself; John 1:17 contrasts the gift through Moses with its fulfilment in Christ, not a God of law with a God of grace.[^s1][^s3][^s12]
- **"Grace means it doesn't matter how I live."** Paul rejects that conclusion explicitly (Romans 6:1-2, 14-15); reference works describe grace as producing godliness, not excusing sin.[^s6][^s11]
- **"Grace is only forgiveness."** Scripture also speaks of grace as God's empowering presence and gifts (2 Corinthians 12:9; Titus 2:11-14).[^s8][^s9][^s12]

## Views across traditions

All the traditions below agree that grace is God's free gift and that no one earns salvation; they differ on how grace is received and how it works in a person.[^s13][^s17]

- **Catholic.** The *Catechism* describes grace as God's free and undeserved favour and help, given so we can respond to his call and share in his own life. It distinguishes sanctifying (habitual) grace, first received in Baptism, from actual graces, teaches that even our preparation for grace is God's work, and holds that God's initiative calls for a free human response.[^s14]
- **Eastern Orthodox.** Orthodox theology describes grace as God's uncreated energies, through which believers share God's life but not his essence, drawing on 2 Peter 1:4, "partakers of the divine nature" (KJV).[^s10][^s18][^s19] Salvation is understood as *theosis* (deification), a lifelong growth in which human freedom cooperates with God (synergy), especially through the sacraments.[^s18][^s19]
- **Lutheran and Reformed.** The Augsburg Confession teaches that people are not justified by their own strength, merits or works but freely for Christ's sake through faith, citing Romans 3 and 4.[^s15] Protestant traditions summarise this as justification by "grace alone", and Reformed and Free Church traditions generally tie grace less closely to the sacraments than Catholic and Orthodox Christians do.[^s13]
- **Wesleyan.** Methodist teaching speaks of prevenient grace that goes before any human response and draws people toward repentance, justifying grace that pardons, and sanctifying grace that grows believers in love of God and neighbour.[^s16]

There has also been real convergence: in 1999 Catholics and Lutherans signed the Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification, later affirmed by the World Methodist Council, the Anglican Consultative Council and the World Communion of Reformed Churches.[^s17]

## Connections

Grace runs through **the story of the Bible**, from God's favour to Noah to the gift of Christ.[^s1][^s2][^s3] It **complements faith**: Ephesians 2:8 joins them, saved "by grace … through faith".[^s7] It **complements justification**, which the *Catechism* and the Augsburg Confession both trace to God's grace.[^s14][^s15] It is **studied in biblical theology**, which follows it across both Testaments.[^s12] It is related to the **Kingdom of God** and the **parables of Jesus**, which picture God's undeserved generosity; to the **atonement**, the redemption through which grace justifies (Romans 3:24); and to the **Mosaic covenant**, given where God named himself gracious.[^s1][^s4][^s12] **Paul** and **Barnabas** illustrate grace in their lives and preaching, and **forgiveness** is one of its fruits.[^s11][^s12]
