# Galilee

Galilee, the northern hill country of the land of Israel, is where most of the Gospel story takes place. Jesus grew up in Nazareth, made the lakeside town of Capernaum his home, called fishermen from their boats and preached in the region's synagogues.[^s3][^s2][^s14] The name comes from the Hebrew *ha-Galil*, "the circle" or "the district", and Isaiah calls it *Gelil ha-Goyim*, "Galilee of the nations".[^s13][^s14][^s1]

## Why it matters

Jerusalem was the centre of Israel's worship, but the good news began in the north: "Jesus came into Galilee, preaching the Gospel of the Kingdom of God" (Mark 1:14, WEB).[^s3] Knowing its villages, lake and Jewish life under a Herodian ruler helps readers picture the parables and healings.[^s14][^s17]

## Geography

Galilee runs roughly 60 miles north to south and 30 miles east to west.[^s14] Josephus, who commanded Jewish forces there against Rome, writes that "the Galilees" are two, Upper and Lower, ringed by Phoenicia and Syria.[^s15] Upper Galilee is rugged, with peaks above 3,000 feet; Lower Galilee is gentler, its hills under 2,000 feet, opening south onto the Jezreel (Esdraelon) valley.[^s14][^s13] Josephus praises the soil as rich and wholly farmed, and says the region was crowded with villages; his claim that even the smallest held over fifteen thousand people is usually treated as an exaggeration, though it was certainly densely settled.[^s15][^s13]

On the eastern edge lies the lake that the Gospels call the Sea of Galilee, about 13 miles long and 7.5 miles wide, some 685 feet below sea level; it was also known as Lake Gennesaret.[^s14][^s13] Capernaum sat on its northern shore and Tiberias on its western shore.[^s14]

## In the biblical story

**Israel's north.** Joshua 20:7 names "Kedesh in Galilee in the hill country of Naphtali" as a city of refuge, and the region covered the tribal lands of Naphtali, Asher and probably Zebulun.[^s9][^s14] Solomon paid Hiram of Tyre with "twenty cities in the land of Galilee" (1 Kings 9:11, WEB).[^s10] In the eighth century BC the Assyrian king Tiglath-pileser III took "Galilee, all the land of Naphtali" and "carried them captive to Assyria" (2 Kings 15:29, WEB).[^s8][^s14]

**A promise of light.** Isaiah answers that loss with hope: the land of Zebulun and Naphtali, once brought low, would be made glorious, and "the people who walked in darkness have seen a great light" (Isaiah 9:1-2, WEB; in Hebrew Bibles the first verse is numbered 8:23).[^s1][^s13]

**Between the Testaments.** After the exile, Jews were again living in Galilee; the deuterocanonical book 1 Maccabees (Scripture for Catholic and Orthodox Christians) recalls Simon the Maccabee bringing Galilean Jews to Judea around 165 BC, and under the Hasmonean kings the region was brought under Jewish rule and largely resettled by Jews.[^s14][^s13] When Herod the Great died in 4 BC, Galilee passed to his son Herod Antipas, the "tetrarch of Galilee" of Luke 3:1, who rebuilt Sepphoris and founded Tiberias.[^s11][^s14][^s17]

**Jesus' ministry.** Jesus came "from Nazareth of Galilee" to be baptized (Mark 1:9, WEB), then returned to preach the kingdom.[^s3] Matthew says he left Nazareth for Capernaum "that it might be fulfilled which was spoken through Isaiah", quoting the promise of light to "Galilee of the Gentiles" (Matthew 4:13-16, WEB).[^s2] Beside the lake he called Simon and Andrew, James and John, and he taught "in their synagogues throughout all Galilee" (Mark 1:16-20, 39, WEB).[^s3] The Sermon on the Mount and most of his parables and miracles are set in Galilee.[^s14]

**Doubt from the south.** Galilee had a mixed reputation. Nathanael asks, "Can any good thing come out of Nazareth?" (John 1:46, WEB), some in Jerusalem ask, "does the Christ come out of Galilee?", and Jesus' opponents insist that "no prophet has arisen out of Galilee" (John 7:41, 52, WEB).[^s5][^s4] When Peter denied knowing Jesus, his speech gave him away: "you are a Galilean, and your speech shows it" (Mark 14:70, WEB).[^s6]

**After the resurrection.** The risen Jesus "goes before you into Galilee" (Matthew 28:7, WEB), and the eleven meet him "to the mountain where Jesus had sent them", where he commissions them to "make disciples of all nations" (Matthew 28:16, 19, WEB).[^s7] By Acts 9:31 there are churches "throughout all Judea, Galilee, and Samaria" (WEB).[^s12] After AD 70 Galilee also became the heart of Jewish life in the land, and Tiberias a centre of the rabbinic learning that produced the Mishnah.[^s13][^s14]

## Common misreadings

- **"Galilee was a Gentile region."** Isaiah's phrase "Galilee of the nations" (Matthew: "of the Gentiles") has often been read as if Jesus' Galilee were mostly pagan. The archaeological evidence points instead to a mainly Jewish population in his day, with ritual baths and stone vessels.[^s16][^s2]
- **Galileans as country bumpkins.** The Gospels record Judean scepticism, but Jewish sources describe Galileans as strict in religious observance.[^s4][^s13]

## Scholarly views

- **How Jewish was Galilee?** Mark Chancey notes that many older dictionaries and studies of the historical Jesus assumed a large, even majority, Gentile population. Surveying excavations at Sepphoris, Tiberias and some 27 village sites, he finds first-century Jewish markers and few pagan ones before the second century, and concludes Galilee was mostly Jewish in Jesus' time.[^s16] The Encyclopedia of the Bible reads Isaiah's phrase as a sign of a long-mixed population.[^s14]
- **Cities and villages.** Jonathan Reed, who has excavated Sepphoris, argues that Antipas's building of Sepphoris and Tiberias drew Galilee into a more urban, taxed economy, while villages like Nazareth and Capernaum stayed small; he stresses that archaeology reconstructs Jesus' world rather than proving the Gospels.[^s17] The New Testament never records Jesus visiting Sepphoris or Tiberias.[^s14]

## Connections

- Galilee **illustrates** the kingdom of God, the message Jesus first proclaimed there.[^s3]
- It is **explained by** Second Temple Judaism, whose purity practices and synagogue life Galilean villages shared.[^s16]
- It is **corroborated by** Josephus's *Jewish War*, which describes the region's two parts, its soil and its people.[^s15]
- It is **studied in** biblical archaeology, through digs at Sepphoris, Tiberias and its villages.[^s17][^s16]
- It is **related** to Jerusalem, Peter, the Gospels of Mark and Matthew, Isaiah's prophecy and the Great Commission.[^s1][^s2][^s3][^s7]
