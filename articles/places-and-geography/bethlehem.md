# Bethlehem

Bethlehem is a small hill town in Judah, a few miles south of Jerusalem, whose name in Hebrew, *Beth-lehem*, is usually understood as "house of bread".[^s10][^s12] Its older name was Ephrath or Ephrathah, which is why Scripture sometimes calls it Bethlehem Ephrathah or Bethlehem Judah.[^s10][^s11] For a village of modest size it carries a remarkable weight in the Bible: it is the home of Ruth and Boaz, the hometown of King David and, in the Gospels of Matthew and Luke, the place where Jesus was born.[^s2][^s4][^s7][^s8]

## Why it matters

Bethlehem ties the story of David to the story of Jesus. Micah promised that from this town, "small among the clans of Judah", would come one "to be ruler in Israel" (Micah 5:2, WEB), and Matthew and Luke both stress that Jesus, David's descendant, was born in David's town.[^s6][^s7][^s8] Its earlier history explains why the Gospel writers cared about it.[^s15]

## Geography

Judean Bethlehem lies about five or six miles (some 8 km) south and slightly west of Jerusalem, on a ridge above fertile valleys at roughly 2,300 to 2,500 feet (about 760 m), near the old road running south to Hebron.[^s10][^s12][^s15] Its height made it easy to defend, and the surrounding fields and terraces made it farming and shepherding country.[^s10][^s11] The Bible also knows a second, smaller Bethlehem in the tribal land of Zebulun, near Nazareth in Galilee, which should not be confused with Bethlehem of Judah.[^s10][^s12]

## In the biblical story

**Rachel's road.** Bethlehem first appears in connection with a death: "Rachel died, and was buried in the way to Ephrath (the same is Bethlehem)" (Genesis 35:19, WEB).[^s1] Matthew later recalls Jeremiah's image of "Rachel weeping for her children" when Herod kills the boys of Bethlehem (Matthew 2:16-18, WEB).[^s7]

**Ruth and Boaz.** The Book of Ruth opens with "a certain man of Bethlehem Judah" leaving for Moab because of famine (Ruth 1:1, WEB). Naomi returns empty with her Moabite daughter-in-law Ruth, and they reach Bethlehem "in the beginning of barley harvest" (Ruth 1:22, WEB).[^s2] The elders bless Boaz's marriage to Ruth with the wish that he "be famous in Bethlehem", and their son Obed becomes "the father of Jesse, the father of David" (Ruth 4:11, 17, WEB).[^s3]

**David's town.** God sends Samuel to "Jesse the Bethlehemite" to anoint a king, and Samuel anoints the youngest son, the shepherd David (1 Samuel 16:1, 11-13, WEB).[^s4] Later, while David is in his stronghold and the Philistines hold a garrison in Bethlehem, he longs for water "of the well of Bethlehem, which is by the gate"; when three of his warriors risk their lives to bring it, he pours it out to the Lord (2 Samuel 23:14-16, WEB).[^s5] Rehoboam later fortified the town (2 Chronicles 11:6), and after the exile 123 "children of Beth-lehem" returned (Ezra 2:21, WEB).[^s20][^s19]

**Micah's promise.** In a time of siege, Micah looks past Israel's humiliated ruler, struck "with a rod on the cheek", to little Bethlehem Ephrathah, from which a ruler "whose goings out are from of old" will come to "shepherd in the strength of Yahweh" (Micah 5:2, 4, WEB).[^s6] The hope is for a new David from David's own town.[^s11][^s15]

**The birth of Jesus.** Luke says Joseph went "out of the city of Nazareth, into Judea, to David's city, which is called Bethlehem, because he was of the house and family of David", and that Mary laid her son "in a feeding trough" (Luke 2:4, 7, WEB). The shepherds say, "Let's go to Bethlehem, now, and see" (Luke 2:15, WEB).[^s8] Matthew tells of wise men asking Herod where the Messiah would be born; the chief priests and scribes answer "In Bethlehem of Judea" and quote Micah (Matthew 2:1-6, WEB).[^s7] John records a crowd assuming the Messiah must come "from Bethlehem, the village where David was" (John 7:42, WEB), apparently unaware of where Jesus had been born.[^s9]

**After the New Testament.** Around AD 150 Justin Martyr wrote that Joseph, finding no lodging, stayed "in a certain cave near the village", where Mary gave birth.[^s13] Jerome, who lived in Bethlehem while translating the Bible, complained that the cave had once been overshadowed by a pagan grove of Adonis.[^s14][^s11] A church was built over the cave in the fourth century and rebuilt under Justinian in the sixth; that Church of the Nativity, shared by Catholic, Orthodox and Armenian communities, still stands.[^s10][^s12]

## Archaeology

Excavations show Bronze and Iron Age settlement at Bethlehem.[^s15] A fourteenth-century BC Amarna letter may mention a town called *Bit-Lahmi* near Jerusalem, though that reading is disputed.[^s10][^s17] In 2012 the Israel Antiquities Authority announced a small clay sealing found in Jerusalem's City of David, dated to the late eighth or seventh century BC, which mentions Bethlehem and appears to record a shipment sent "for the king".[^s16][^s17] It is the oldest undisputed mention of the town outside the Bible, but it confirms a Judean town that no one doubted existed rather than any particular story.[^s17]

## Common misreadings

- **"The inn" as a hotel.** The Gospels say nothing of an innkeeper; Luke only says there was no room "in the inn", and early tradition remembered a cave with a manger.[^s8][^s13]

## Scholarly views

- **Traditional view.** Christians have long held, on the testimony of Matthew and Luke, that Jesus was born in Bethlehem of Judea, and the cave tradition is attested by the mid-second century.[^s7][^s8][^s13][^s12] Defenders reply that Bethlehem was not so central to Jewish messianic hope that a birth there had to be invented, and that travel to an ancestral home for property or tax purposes had precedents even if an empire-wide census is hard to place.[^s15]
- **Critical view.** Many historians think Jesus, always called "of Nazareth", was more likely born there, and that the Bethlehem birth may have been shaped by Micah 5:2; they also question an empire-wide census like the one in Luke 2.[^s15][^s12] Mark and Paul never mention Bethlehem.[^s15]
- **Bethlehem of Galilee.** Aviram Oshri, an archaeologist with the Israel Antiquities Authority, has proposed that the Galilean Bethlehem near Nazareth was the true birthplace; others reply that Bethlehem's importance to the tradition lies in its link to David, which only the Judean town has.[^s18]

## Connections

- Bethlehem **illustrates** the Davidic covenant: Micah's promise and the Gospels' birth stories tie the coming ruler to David's town.[^s15][^s6]
- It is **corroborated by** the Amarna letters (a possible early mention) and by the Bethlehem bulla from Jerusalem.[^s10][^s16]
- It is **studied in** biblical archaeology, through excavation of the town and finds that name it.[^s15]
- It is **related** to David, the birth of Jesus, Ruth, Micah, Matthew and Luke.[^s3][^s4][^s6][^s7][^s8]
