# Reformed Tradition

The Reformed tradition is the family of Protestant churches that share a common origin in the Reformation of sixteenth-century Switzerland and are regarded as essentially Calvinistic in doctrine.[^s8] Huldrych Zwingli in Zurich and John Calvin in Geneva stand at its head, and the Zurich Consensus of 1549 helped the name Reformed come into wider use.[^s8] Presbyterian churches are governed by pastors and lay elders (presbyters) meeting in sessions, presbyteries, synods and assemblies.[^s8]

## Why it matters

Reformed churches wrote their faith into national confessions (Geneva, Helvetic, French, Belgic, Scots); the Heidelberg Catechism and the Westminster Confession became especially influential.[^s8] Covenant, election and the Spirit's witness to Scripture come up often in Bible study groups, and knowing what the Reformed mean by them helps every reader describe them fairly.[^s9][^s10]

## Key teachings

**To God alone the glory.** To the Lutheran watchword of faith alone, Reformed Christians added "to God alone the glory", a conviction that shaped their worship, church government and buildings.[^s8] Paul's doxology voices the same instinct: "For of him and through him and to him are all things. To him be the glory for ever!" (Romans 11:36, WEB).[^s6]

**Scripture, the rule of faith and life.** The Westminster Confession lists the thirty-nine books of the Old Testament and twenty-seven of the New as given by inspiration of God to be "the rule of faith and life", and it places the Apocrypha outside the canon.[^s9][^s10] Scripture's authority rests not on any person or church but wholly on God its author, and full assurance of it comes from the inward work of the Holy Spirit.[^s9] Behind this stands Paul's reminder that every Scripture is God-breathed and useful for teaching and training in righteousness (2 Timothy 3:15-17).[^s1]

**Grace and election.** The Confession teaches that God chose people to life in Christ before the world began, out of free grace rather than foreseen faith or works, citing Ephesians 1.[^s10] Paul writes that God "chose us in him before the foundation of the world" (Ephesians 1:4, WEB), "to the praise of the glory of his grace" (1:6).[^s2] The Confession asks that this doctrine be taught with "special prudence and care", to yield humility, praise and comfort.[^s10]

**One covenant of grace.** Reformed theology reads the Bible as the unfolding of one covenant of grace, in which God freely offers life through Christ and promises his Spirit.[^s10] Under the law it was administered by promises, sacrifices, circumcision and types pointing to Christ; under the gospel, by preaching, baptism and the Lord's Supper: one covenant, not two differing in substance.[^s10] So the Heidelberg Catechism teaches that the infants of believers belong to God's covenant and should receive baptism as its sign, in place of circumcision, citing Genesis 17:7 and Acts 2:39.[^s11] God promised to keep his covenant with Abraham "and your seed after you throughout their generations" (Genesis 17:7, WEB), and at Pentecost Peter said the promise was "to you, and to your children" (Acts 2:39, WEB).[^s3][^s4]

**The Lord's Supper.** The Heidelberg Catechism teaches that the bread is not changed into Christ's body but is called his body by the nature and usage of sacraments; through the Holy Spirit, believers truly share in his body and blood as surely as they receive the signs.[^s12] Westminster speaks of a real but spiritual feeding on Christ by faith.[^s10] Both appeal to Paul's question, "The bread which we break, is it not a communion of the body of Christ?" (1 Corinthians 10:16, ASV).[^s5][^s12]

## How it reads Scripture

**Scripture interprets Scripture.** The Westminster Confession calls Scripture itself the infallible rule for interpreting Scripture, and the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture the supreme judge in controversies.[^s9] Calvin, trained first as a classical philologist, joined *sola Scriptura* to *tota Scriptura*: each passage is explained in the light of the whole Bible.[^s15]

**Scripture carries its own evidence.** In the *Institutes* (book 1, chapter 7) Calvin denied that the church gives the Bible its authority, since the church is itself built on the foundation of the apostles and prophets (Ephesians 2:20).[^s13][^s7] Arguments can support its credibility, but certainty comes from the Spirit's inward testimony, for Scripture comes "carrying its own evidence along with it".[^s13]

**Plain sense, briefly told.** Writing to Simon Grynaeus in 1539, Calvin named "lucid brevity" as an interpreter's chief virtue and said the main task is to lay open the mind of the writer.[^s14] His commentaries aimed to show the authors' meaning rather than his own learning.[^s16] He favoured the historical and grammatical sense, treated Hebrew and Greek as essential, and set aside allegorical readings.[^s15]

**Preaching through whole books.** Beginning his Zurich ministry in January 1519, Zwingli set aside the prescribed lessons to preach straight through Matthew, then Acts and the epistles.[^s17]

## Views across traditions

All confess Scripture as God's Word; they differ over its relation to Tradition and the Church's teaching office, and over the sacraments.[^s9][^s18][^s19]

- **Reformed.** Scripture rests on God's authority, sealed by the Spirit's witness, and interprets itself.[^s9][^s13]
- **Catholic.** The Second Vatican Council's *Dei Verbum* teaches that Tradition and Scripture flow from the same divine source and form one sacred deposit of the Word of God entrusted to the Church, whose living teaching office interprets it authentically in the name of Jesus Christ.[^s18]
- **Eastern Orthodox.** Scripture is essential but "cannot be isolated from the ongoing life of God's people"; the Bible alone, apart from the Church, is rejected.[^s19]
- **Lutheran.** Lutherans confess that Christ's body and blood are truly present and received with the bread and wine, and they reject a merely spiritual eating; this is where Lutheran and Reformed readings of the Supper part.[^s20][^s12]

## Connections

The Reformed tradition was **introduced by** Huldrych Zwingli and John Calvin and is **set out in** the Heidelberg Catechism and the Westminster Confession.[^s8] It is a branch of **Protestantism** and of **biblical interpretation**.[^s8][^s15] It holds that **Scripture interprets Scripture**,[^s9] sets the **deuterocanonical books** outside the **biblical canon**,[^s9] and reads the **Abrahamic covenant** and the **Mosaic covenant** as administrations of one **covenant** of **grace**, and finds election taught in **Paul**'s letters.[^s10][^s2] It grounds infant **baptism** in Genesis 17 and Acts 2,[^s11] differs from **Lutheranism** on the Supper,[^s20] and from the **Catholic Church** and **Eastern Orthodoxy** on Scripture and Tradition.[^s18][^s19]
