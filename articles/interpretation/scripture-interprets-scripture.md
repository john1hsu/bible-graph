# Scripture Interprets Scripture

"Scripture interprets Scripture" is the conviction that the Bible is its own best commentary. Because its many books form one witness to one God, a passage is best understood when it is read alongside the rest of the Bible, with passages that speak plainly and at length helping us with those that are brief, obscure or easily misread.[^s8][^s9]

The habit appears inside the Bible itself. When the devil set Jesus on the pinnacle of the temple, he quoted Psalm 91 at him: "He will command his angels concerning you" (Matthew 4:6, WEB). Jesus answered, "Again, it is written, 'You shall not test the Lord, your God'" (Matthew 4:7, WEB), words from Deuteronomy 6:16, where Moses recalls how Israel tested God in the wilderness at Massah.[^s1][^s3] His first reply, too, came from Deuteronomy, where God taught Israel through the manna that "man does not live by bread only" (Deuteronomy 8:3, WEB).[^s1][^s4] After the resurrection, "beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, he explained to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27, WEB), and he opened their minds to understand the law, the prophets and the psalms together (Luke 24:44-45).[^s5] The Beroeans, for their part, received Paul's message eagerly, "examining the Scriptures daily to see whether these things were so" (Acts 17:11, WEB).[^s6]

The principle was put into words early in the Church. Augustine advised that where Scripture is obscure we should gather its sense from places where the meaning is more plain, and that an interpretation is safest when other passages of Scripture confirm it; for genuinely ambiguous passages he pointed readers to the "rule of faith", which he said is gathered from the plainer passages of Scripture and the authority of the Church.[^s7]

## Why it matters

A single verse lifted out of the Bible can be made to say almost anything, as the temptation story shows: the devil's quotation was accurate, but it was used against the grain of the rest of Scripture.[^s1][^s2] Psalm 91 is a song of trust for the one who "dwells in the secret place of the Most High" (Psalm 91:1, WEB), not a licence to force God's hand.[^s2] Reading Scripture with Scripture keeps us from building a doctrine or a life decision on one obscure verse; a classic Lutheran essay passes on the warning that such a doctrine can be accepted only with great reserve.[^s12]

Positively, Christians of every tradition read the Bible as one story centred on Christ, so reading a part in the light of the whole lets Scripture show us its centre, as on the road to Emmaus.[^s5][^s9][^s10]

## How to apply it

Follow the steps above. When the New Testament quotes the Old, open the Old Testament passage: Jesus's answers in Matthew 4 mean far more once we see that they come from Moses' reminders to Israel about its testing in the wilderness.[^s1][^s3][^s4] Look for the passages that treat a subject directly and at length, which Lutheran theologians called the *sedes doctrinae*, the "seat" of a teaching, and let them shed light on brief or puzzling references; when two passages seem to pull apart, hold both before discarding either.[^s12]

## Common mistakes

- **Word-matching instead of meaning-matching.** Verses sharing an English word are not necessarily about the same thing. Augustine warned that the same image does not always carry the same sense in every passage.[^s7]
- **Flattening the Bible's voices.** Comparing passages is not a way to make every book sound the same; each must first be heard on its own terms before it is placed beside others.[^s12]
- **Letting a system overrule the text.** Scholars have noted the risk that a theological framework, presented as "the whole of Scripture", can dominate and override what a particular passage plainly says.[^s13][^s12]

## Views across traditions

Christians broadly agree that Scripture is a unity and should be read as a whole, but they describe the principle, and the interpreter's place within the Church, in different ways.[^s8][^s9][^s10]

**Reformed and other Protestant traditions.** The Westminster Confession states that the infallible rule of interpreting Scripture "is the Scripture itself", so that a disputed passage is searched out by other places that speak more clearly, and that the Holy Spirit speaking in Scripture is the final judge of controversies.[^s8] Lutheran teaching uses the same principle, often in the Latin form *Scriptura Scripturae interpres*, and calls the sum of clear passages the "analogy of faith".[^s12]

**Catholic Church.** The Catechism teaches that because Scripture was inspired, it must be read in the light of the same Spirit who inspired it, and names three criteria: attention to the content and unity of the whole of Scripture, with Christ at its centre; reading within the living Tradition of the whole Church; and the "analogy of faith", the coherence of the truths of faith among themselves.[^s9] It adds that interpretation is ultimately subject to the judgement of the Church, whose ministry is to watch over and interpret the Word of God.[^s9]

**Eastern Orthodox Church.** Orthodox interpretation affirms the unity and centrality of the Bible and reads it with Christ as the decisive criterion, but stresses the interdependence of Church, Bible and Tradition, following the Church Fathers closely and treating Tradition as the final authority when interpretations conflict.[^s10] An Orthodox archdiocese's teaching page puts it simply: Scripture is rightly understood within the living Tradition of the Church rather than by private interpretation.[^s11]

## Connections

A principle of **biblical interpretation**, it presupposes **reading in context**, since each passage must be heard in its own setting first, and it helps correct **proof-texting** by asking what the rest of Scripture says.[^s12] Augustine set out an early form of it in **On Christian Doctrine**.[^s7] It is held explicitly in the **Reformed tradition**[^s8] and **Lutheranism**,[^s12] while the **Catholic Church** expresses a related principle through Scripture's unity, Tradition and the analogy of faith.[^s9] It sits alongside **inductive Bible study**, **genre** and **biblical theology**, which also ask how the parts fit the whole.[^s10]
