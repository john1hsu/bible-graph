# Hebrew Poetry

Open almost any page of the Old Testament and there is a good chance you are reading poetry. BibleProject estimates that about a third of the Bible is ancient Israelite poetry, with the Psalms as its largest collection and the prophets full of dense poetic imagery.[^s14] The Masoretic scribes marked Job, Proverbs and Psalms as the three poetical books, and readers usually add the Song of Songs, Lamentations and Ecclesiastes; but poems also break into the story books, such as Jacob's blessing, the Song of the Sea and Deborah's song.[^s10] Hebrew poetry does not rhyme much and has no agreed system of metre. Its heartbeat is the **parallel line**: a short statement followed by a second that echoes, sharpens or completes it.[^s9][^s10]

Psalm 19 shows the pattern at once: "The heavens declare the glory of God. The expanse shows his handiwork" (Psalm 19:1, WEB), followed by "Day after day they pour out speech, and night after night they display knowledge" (Psalm 19:2).[^s2] The medieval Jewish commentators Ibn Ezra and David Kimhi already called this feature a "doubling", and the Anglican bishop Robert Lowth described it systematically in his 1753 lectures on the sacred poetry of the Hebrews.[^s9]

## Why it matters

Much of the Bible's prayer and praise comes to us as poetry. Christine Hayes of Yale calls the Psalms the Bible's principal collection of religious lyric poetry, and shows how they speak of God through images such as king, shield, rock and refuge.[^s13] When Christians pray the Psalms, sing the canticles or read the prophets, they are using a poetic language that slows the reader down and invites the heart as well as the mind.[^s14][^s13] The habit carries into the New Testament: Mary's song is built of paired lines in the manner of Israel's psalms, "My soul magnifies the Lord. My spirit has rejoiced in God my Savior" (Luke 1:46-47, WEB), and it praises a God who "has put down princes from their thrones, and has exalted the lowly" (Luke 1:52).[^s8]

## How to read it

**Read in pairs, and look for the difference.** Lowth sorted parallel lines into three main kinds: *synonymous* (the second line restates the first), *antithetic* (it states the opposite side) and *synthetic* (it builds on it).[^s9] Proverbs 11 is full of antithetic pairs joined by "but": "The integrity of the upright shall guide them, but the perverseness of the treacherous shall destroy them" (Proverbs 11:3, WEB).[^s3] Harry Hagan's guide for readers adds that similarity between lines is there partly to make the difference stand out, so the useful question is always what the second line adds.[^s11] In Psalm 1:1 the person who is blessed does not "walk", "stand" or "sit" with the wicked, the sinners and the scoffers, a sequence that pictures someone settling ever deeper into bad company.[^s11][^s1]

**Let the images work.** Poetry thinks in pictures. "Yahweh is my shepherd; I shall lack nothing" (Psalm 23:1, WEB) is followed by pastures, still waters, a dark valley, a rod and staff.[^s4] BibleProject treats understanding metaphor as an essential tool for reading biblical poetry,[^s14] and Hayes shows how Psalm 23 carries its trust through the shepherd who guides through the frightening valley.[^s13] Ask what each image says about God and about us, rather than reducing it at once to a flat statement.

**Notice the larger shape.** Some poems are built on the Hebrew alphabet. Psalm 119 has twenty-two sections of eight verses, one for each letter from aleph to tav,[^s5] and Lamentations 1 runs through the alphabet in its twenty-two verses; the International Standard Bible Encyclopedia lists several other psalms and part of Proverbs among the acrostics.[^s6][^s10] The form itself speaks: devotion to God's word, or grief over Jerusalem, expressed "from A to Z".

**Hear the prophets as poets.** Amos piles up parallel lines as God rejects worship without justice, ending, "But let justice roll on like rivers, and righteousness like a mighty stream" (Amos 5:24, WEB).[^s7] The rhythm drives the message home in a way prose could not.

## Common mistakes

- **Treating the second line as mere repetition.** Parallel lines usually move forward, developing the thought, feeling or action of the first; skimming the echo misses the point.[^s11]
- **Reading images as technical description.** Heavens that "pour out speech" (Psalm 19:2) are praising God, not giving a lecture on astronomy.[^s2][^s14]
- **Turning poetic contrasts into guarantees.** The "but" sayings of Proverbs describe two ways of life; they are wisdom to live by, not promises that every case will turn out that way.[^s3][^s11]
- **Missing the architecture.** Reading Psalm 119 or Lamentations as a random string of verses hides the alphabetic order that holds them together.[^s5][^s6]

## Scholarly views

Scholars agree that parallelism, compression and imagery mark Hebrew poetry, but they debate how to describe it. Lowth's three categories shaped study for two centuries.[^s9] James Kugel challenged them in *The Idea of Biblical Poetry* (Johns Hopkins University Press), arguing that the Bible itself has no native notion of "poetry" and that its style is better seen as a continuum of more or less heightened speech.[^s12] Kugel summed up the paired line as "There is A, and what's more there is B," stressing that the second line follows and completes the first; Hagan combines both approaches, treating similarity and sequence as two dimensions of one movement.[^s11] Metre is the most disputed point: the older International Standard Bible Encyclopedia judges Hebrew verse rhythmic rather than strictly metrical, notes that proposed metrical systems have been widely rejected, and even holds that the New Testament has very little poetry, though passages such as Luke 1:46-55 continue the psalmists' paired style.[^s10][^s8]

## Connections

Hebrew poetry fills much of the [Old Testament](bg:old-testament),[^s14] and it is easiest to read after learning the basic idea of [genre in the Bible](bg:genre-in-the-bible).[^s14] It **contrasts with** [biblical narrative](bg:biblical-narrative), the prose storytelling of books like Genesis and Kings, although poems are set within that prose and Kugel argues the line between them is not sharp.[^s10][^s12] The study of its parallelism was **introduced by** [Robert Lowth](bg:robert-lowth) in his [Lectures on the Sacred Poetry of the Hebrews](bg:lectures-on-the-sacred-poetry-of-the-hebrews).[^s9] Its home is above all the [Book of Psalms](bg:book-of-psalms), including [Psalm 22](bg:psalm-22), and it also shapes [wisdom literature](bg:wisdom-literature), the [Book of Job](bg:book-of-job), the [Book of Lamentations](bg:book-of-lamentations) and the [Song of the Sea](bg:song-of-the-sea).[^s10][^s13] Knowing how it works guards against [reading proverbs as promises](bg:reading-proverbs-as-promises), gives voice to [suffering and lament](bg:suffering-and-lament), and helps us hear Mary's song in the [Gospel of Luke](bg:gospel-of-luke).[^s3][^s6][^s8]
