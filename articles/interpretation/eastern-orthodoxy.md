# Eastern Orthodoxy

Eastern Orthodoxy is one of the three great families of Christianity, marked by its continuity with the apostolic church, its liturgy and its territorial churches.[^s7] It is a fellowship of self-governing ("autocephalous") churches, among which the patriarch of Constantinople holds a primacy of honour; its separation from the Western church, usually dated to 1054, was the end of a long estrangement.[^s7] This article describes how the Orthodox Church receives and reads the Bible, in its own terms, and notes where other Christians see things differently.

## Why it matters

Many readers know Orthodox Christians, visit an Orthodox liturgy, or meet the Church Fathers, icons and hymns that Orthodoxy treasures. A common assumption is that the Bible matters less to the Orthodox than to Protestants. Orthodox teaching says otherwise: Scripture is the supreme record of God's revelation and the standard for worship, theology and ethics.[^s6] What is distinctive is *where* it is read: inside the Church's worship and Tradition, never as a book standing on its own.[^s11] Understanding that helps Christians of every tradition describe Orthodoxy fairly, and it raises useful questions for anyone's Bible reading.

## Key teachings

**Scripture within Tradition.** The Orthodox Church in America calls Scripture an essential source of faith that must not be cut off from the Church's living Tradition: the councils, the writings of the Fathers, the liturgy and the lives of the saints.[^s11] An Orthodox scholar describes an "organic bond" between the Church and its Bible, in which Tradition is the final authority over disputed interpretations, expressed through the ecumenical councils and their reception by the Church.[^s6] Orthodoxy recognises seven ecumenical councils, from Nicaea (325) to Nicaea II (787), and holds that a council is truly ecumenical only when the body of the Church accepts it.[^s8] Paul himself charged the Thessalonians to hold the traditions they were taught "by word or by letter" (2 Thessalonians 2:15, WEB), and Orthodoxy sees written Scripture and the living Tradition as one inheritance from the apostles.[^s5][^s11]

**The Bible of the Church.** The Orthodox Church uses the Greek Septuagint as its Old Testament, in its wider canon.[^s6] The Orthodox Faith series counts the so-called apocryphal books among the genuine books of the Old Testament, while noting that other Christians rank them lower or reject them.[^s12] Orthodox writers themselves describe these books in different ways. The Synod of Jerusalem (1672) named Wisdom, Judith, Tobit, Bel and the Dragon, Susanna, the Maccabees and Sirach as canonical,[^s10] while another strand follows Athanasius's fourth-century category of books "to be read" (*anagignoskomena*), which sit in Orthodox Bibles and are used in worship without being called canonical in the strict sense.[^s15]

**The goal: communion with God.** Britannica summarises the aim of the Christian life in Orthodoxy as communion with God and deification (*theosis*), which raises people to the dignity intended at creation and "does not mean dehumanization."[^s8] The language echoes 2 Peter 1:4, where God's promises enable believers to "become partakers of the divine nature" (WEB).[^s3]

## How it reads Scripture

**Christ is the key.** On the road to Emmaus the risen Jesus, "beginning from Moses and from all the prophets", explained "the things concerning himself", and later "opened their minds, that they might understand the Scriptures" (Luke 24:27, 45, WEB).[^s1] The Orthodox Faith series takes these verses as the pattern: Christ, the living Word, gives the right understanding of the written word, through the Holy Spirit in the Church, which Paul calls "the pillar and ground of the truth" (1 Timothy 3:15, WEB).[^s13][^s4] It adds that no prophecy of Scripture is "of private interpretation" (2 Peter 1:20, WEB).[^s3][^s13] For Stylianopoulos, Christ is the decisive criterion for interpreting the Old Testament, and the Fathers adapted grammatical, allegorical and typological methods to read it.[^s6]

**With the Fathers and the liturgy.** Metropolitan Kallistos Ware urges readers to receive Scripture "through the Church and in the Church", to consult the Fathers, especially St John Chrysostom's homilies, and to notice how the Church's choice of readings for feasts reveals its interpretation.[^s14] Only the book of the Gospels, not the whole Bible, is enthroned on the altar of Orthodox churches, a sign that all of Scripture leads to Christ.[^s12] Stylianopoulos notes that the Fathers' biblical legacy is woven into the Church's prayers, hymns and rites.[^s6]

**Personally and obediently.** Ware calls for an approach of obedience and listening, and also a personal one: the reader should see himself in Adam, Peter or the sinful woman and hear Christ speaking to him.[^s14] He cites the Ethiopian official who asks, "How can I, unless someone explains it to me?" (Acts 8:31, WEB), and encourages group Bible study in parishes.[^s14][^s2] That warmth sits alongside older caution: the 1672 Synod held that every Orthodox may hear the Scriptures read, but restricted private reading, especially of the Old Testament, to those trained to interpret it.[^s10] Today Orthodox scholars engage modern biblical studies while seeking to avoid both fundamentalism and a purely historical reading.[^s6]

## Views across traditions

All three traditions confess that Scripture is inspired, receive the early creeds, and hear the Bible in worship; they differ on how Scripture, Tradition and church authority relate, and on the Old Testament canon.[^s6][^s16][^s17]

- **Eastern Orthodox.** Scripture is the supreme record of revelation, read within Tradition; the Bible "cannot prove or interpret itself" apart from the Church's experience.[^s11][^s6] The 1672 Synod went further, saying the Church's witness is not of lesser authority than Scripture.[^s10]
- **Catholic.** The Second Vatican Council teaches that Tradition and Scripture "form one sacred deposit of the word of God," interpreted by the Church's living teaching office.[^s17]
- **Lutheran and other Protestants.** The Formula of Concord makes the prophetic and apostolic Scriptures the "sole rule and standard" of doctrine; creeds and confessions are witnesses to how Scripture was understood, not judges alongside it.[^s16]

## Connections

Orthodox teaching on Scripture was **set out in** the Confession of Dositheus, adopted by the Synod of Jerusalem in 1672 against the Calvinist-leaning confession of Cyril Lucaris.[^s9][^s10] It **contrasts with** Lutheranism, which makes Scripture the sole rule and norm.[^s16][^s11] Its treatment of the wider Old Testament is **explained by** Athanasius's Festal Letter 39, which first distinguished canonical books from books "to be read".[^s15] It is closely related to the Catholic Church's teaching on Scripture and Tradition,[^s17] to the Septuagint and the question of the biblical canon,[^s6][^s12] and to typology, which the Fathers used to read the Old Testament in the light of Christ.[^s6][^s14]
