# Catholic Church

The Catholic Church is the communion of Christians united with the bishop of Rome, and it has a long, carefully articulated tradition of reading the Bible. In its own words, Scripture and apostolic Tradition "form one sacred deposit of the word of God," and the task of interpreting that word authentically belongs to the Church's living teaching office, the Magisterium.[^s5] Catholics hear the Bible above all in the liturgy, are urged to read it frequently and prayerfully, and are taught to look for both its literal and its spiritual senses.[^s5][^s7] This article describes that tradition of reading in its own terms, and notes where other Christians see things differently.

## Why it matters

Many Christians meet Catholic neighbours, attend a Catholic wedding or funeral, or read Catholic commentaries, Bibles and study notes. Knowing how Catholics understand Scripture helps readers avoid caricatures: Catholic teaching neither sets the Bible aside nor treats it as one source among many equals competing for attention.[^s5][^s10] The Second Vatican Council says the Church venerates the Scriptures as she venerates the Lord's body, and approvingly repeats St Jerome's warning that ignorance of Scripture is ignorance of Christ.[^s5] It also commends Bible translations made together with "separated brethren", so that all Christians can use them.[^s5]

## Key teachings

**Scripture and Tradition.** The Catechism teaches that Scripture and Tradition flow from the same divine source and move toward the same goal: Scripture is God's word put into writing under the Spirit's inspiration, while Tradition hands on the word Christ entrusted to the apostles.[^s6] For that reason the Church does not draw its certainty about revealed truth from Scripture alone, and honours both with the same devotion and reverence.[^s6][^s5] Trent had received the apostles' unwritten traditions with the same reverence as the books; Britannica notes that the council deliberately avoided calling them two separate sources, and that some modern Catholic theologians speak of a single source.[^s11][^s10] Catholics point to Paul's instruction to hold the traditions taught "by word or by letter" (2 Thessalonians 2:15, WEB), and to the first believers who "continued steadfastly in the apostles' teaching" (Acts 2:42, WEB); *Dei Verbum* cites both verses.[^s1][^s3][^s5] This capital-T Tradition is distinguished from the many local customs and devotions, which may change.[^s6]

**The teaching office.** The Magisterium, the pope and bishops teaching together, alone gives the authentic interpretation of the word of God, but the council insists that it "is not above the word of God, but serves it," teaching only what has been handed on.[^s5][^s10]

**Inspiration and the canon.** God is the author of Scripture, working through human writers who used their own powers, and the books teach without error the truth God wished written "for the sake of salvation."[^s5] The 2 Timothy 3 description of every Scripture as "God-breathed" and able to make one "wise for salvation" sits behind this teaching.[^s2][^s5] The Council of Trent in 1546 listed the canonical books, including Tobit, Judith, Wisdom, Sirach (Ecclesiasticus), Baruch and 1–2 Maccabees, and declared the Latin Vulgate authentic for public use.[^s11]

## How it reads Scripture

*Dei Verbum* asks readers to find what the human authors meant, paying attention to literary forms and the customs of their time, and also to read each text within the unity of the whole of Scripture, the living Tradition of the Church and the harmony of the faith.[^s5] The Catechism adds the ancient teaching of four senses: the **literal** sense, on which all others rest, and three **spiritual** senses — allegorical (pointing to Christ), moral (guiding how we act) and anagogical (pointing to our eternal destiny).[^s7] Its example of the allegorical sense is the crossing of the Red Sea as a sign of Christian baptism, which echoes Paul's own reading: the Israelites were "baptized into Moses in the cloud and in the sea," and these things were "written for our admonition" (1 Corinthians 10:2, 11, WEB).[^s4][^s7]

Catholic scholarship changed markedly in the twentieth century. Pius XII's 1943 encyclical *Divino Afflante Spiritu* urged study of the original languages, textual criticism and ancient literary forms, and allowed official translations from the Hebrew and Greek rather than only from the Vulgate.[^s9] In 1993 the Pontifical Biblical Commission called the historical-critical method "indispensable," while criticising fundamentalist reading for treating the Bible as if it were dictated word for word.[^s8]

## Views across traditions

**Catholic.** Scripture and Tradition together form one deposit of faith, authentically interpreted by the Magisterium in service of the word; Scripture with Tradition is the "supreme rule of faith."[^s5][^s6]

**Eastern Orthodox.** The Orthodox Church in America describes Scripture as an essential source of faith that cannot be separated from the Church's living Tradition — the councils, the Fathers, the lives of the saints and worship — and holds that the Bible cannot interpret itself apart from the Church's experience.[^s13]

**Lutheran and other Protestants.** The Lutheran Formula of Concord holds that the prophetic and apostolic Scriptures are the sole rule and standard by which all teachings and teachers are judged; creeds and confessions are valued as faithful witnesses but are not equal to Scripture.[^s12] Britannica's account of Catholic teaching observes, for its part, that Protestant churches have also developed dogmatic traditions of their own.[^s10]

All three traditions confess that Scripture is inspired, read it in the worshipping community, and receive the ancient creeds; the disagreement concerns how Scripture, Tradition and church authority relate.[^s5][^s12][^s13]

## Connections

Catholic teaching on Scripture is **set out in** *Dei Verbum* (1965), the council's constitution on revelation,[^s5] and summarised **in** the *Catechism of the Catholic Church*.[^s6] Its modern approach to biblical scholarship was **set out in** *Divino Afflante Spiritu* (1943).[^s9] It **contrasts with** Lutheranism, whose confessions make Scripture the sole rule and norm of doctrine.[^s12] It is closely related to Eastern Orthodoxy's understanding of Scripture within Tradition,[^s13] to the questions of the biblical canon and the deuterocanonical books,[^s11] to the Vulgate, which Trent declared authentic,[^s11] and to practices such as lectio divina and readings such as typology, which the spiritual senses describe.[^s7]
