  - Before interpreting a passage, ask what kind of writing it is (narrative, law, poetry, proverb, prophecy, gospel, letter or apocalypse) and look for clues the book gives about itself.
  - Note the features of that kind of writing, such as parallel lines and imagery in poetry, short general sayings in proverbs, or greetings and arguments in letters.
  - Ask what that kind of writing is designed to do (tell what happened, command, praise, give practical wisdom, warn, comfort or encourage endurance) and let that purpose guide your conclusions.
  - Compare passages where the Bible tells the same thing in two forms, such as the Red Sea in Exodus 14 and 15, and notice what each form adds.
  - Check your genre judgment with a study Bible or Bible dictionary, and hold it humbly where careful readers disagree.
  - Apply the passage in a way that fits its genre, taking a promise as a promise, a proverb as wise counsel and a song as praise to join.

# Genre in the Bible

The Bible is one book, but it is also a library, and its books are written in many different kinds of literature: narrative and law, songs and laments, proverbs, prophetic oracles, gospels, letters and apocalypse. "Genre" is simply a French word for "kind" or "type", and to recognise a text's genre is to know how that kind of writing works; biblical scholar Kenton Sparks calls this "generic competence".[^s9] Reading by genre means asking, before anything else, what kind of writing is in front of us, and then reading it the way that kind of writing is meant to be read.[^s9][^s10]

We already do this every day without thinking. Nobody reads a birthday card like a tax form, or a hymn like a news report. The Bible asks the same skill of us, and it often tells us what it is: Luke says he is writing an orderly "narrative" based on eyewitnesses so that Theophilus may know the certainty of what he was taught (Luke 1:1-4),[^s5] while John's book calls itself a revelation, pronounces a blessing on those who hear "the prophecy", and is addressed like a letter "to the seven assemblies that are in Asia" (Revelation 1:1-4, WEB).[^s6]

## Why it matters

Christians across traditions agree that God speaks through human authors in human words, and that is why genre matters. Vatican II's *Dei Verbum* teaches that to find what God wanted to communicate, readers must look for what the sacred writers intended, paying attention among other things to literary forms, since truth is expressed differently in historical, prophetic and poetic texts and in the customary ways people spoke and told stories at the time.[^s7] Evangelical scholars who drafted the Chicago Statement on Biblical Hermeneutics likewise affirmed that "Scripture communicates God's truth to us verbally through a wide variety of literary forms," and called awareness of those forms essential for sound interpretation.[^s8] The goal is not to make the Bible less true, but to hear its truth in the voice God chose.[^s7][^s8]

A vivid example sits in the middle of Exodus. Chapter 14 tells how the Lord drove back the sea with a strong east wind, how Israel crossed on dry ground and how the waters returned on Pharaoh's army (Exodus 14:21-31).[^s2] Chapter 15 then sings the same victory: Moses and the people praise the Lord's right hand, and the waters pile up at the blast of his breath (Exodus 15:1-10), before Miriam leads the women in the refrain (Exodus 15:20-21).[^s3] BibleProject points to this pair as the same event told twice, once as narrative and once as Hebrew poetry.[^s12] The story tells us what happened; the song teaches us how to respond, with awe and praise.

## How to apply it

Start by naming the kind of writing and its features. Poetry speaks in images: when Psalm 98 calls on the sea to roar and the rivers to clap their hands because the Lord comes to judge the earth with righteousness (Psalm 98:7-9), it invites the whole creation, and us, into joyful worship, not into a lesson about rivers.[^s4] Proverbs are compressed wisdom for life. Proverbs 26 places two sayings side by side: "Don't answer a fool according to his folly, lest you also be like him," and "Answer a fool according to his folly, lest he be wise in his own eyes" (Proverbs 26:4-5, WEB).[^s1] Luther Seminary's *Enter the Bible* notes that this pairing shows proverbs are not laws: they offer wisdom that depends on the situation, and the reader must discern which applies.[^s11] Prophecy and apocalyptic speak first to their own day with vivid symbols, and wisdom books wrestle with life's real questions; readers who know this ask what a vision meant to its first hearers before asking what it means now.[^s10]

Letters argue, encourage and correct; gospels tell the story of Jesus to bring readers to faith; law shapes the life of a covenant people. Each asks a slightly different question of us, and good study keeps those questions distinct.[^s12][^s9]

## Common mistakes

- **Reading poetry as a technical description.** Images such as clapping rivers or waters heaped up by the breath of God are the language of praise.[^s4][^s3]
- **Treating proverbs as promises or rigid rules.** A proverb states what is generally wise, not a guarantee for every case; Proverbs 26:4-5 makes the point by design.[^s11][^s1]
- **Using Revelation as a coded news feed.** Apocalyptic writing works through symbol and was first addressed to real churches.[^s6][^s10]
- **Using genre to explain the text away.** The Chicago Statement warns against imposing categories that deny the historicity of narratives that present themselves as factual,[^s8] while *Dei Verbum* equally asks readers to respect each form on its own terms.[^s7]

### Scholarly views

Genre judgments are not always settled. Steven McKenzie, writing for Oxford University Press, argues for example that Jonah is best read as satire and that much apocalyptic describes events in its authors' own time.[^s10] Many Christian readers and scholars hold that books such as Jonah are historical as well as artful, and the Chicago Statement treats that question as one that genre analysis must not prejudge.[^s8] Where careful readers differ, the wise course is to state the options fairly and let the text's own signals weigh most.[^s9]

## Connections

Reading by genre belongs to **biblical interpretation** as one of its basic tools,[^s9] and it works hand in hand with **reading in context**, since a passage's literary form is part of its context.[^s7] It also sharpens the observation stage of **inductive Bible study**, and it fits with **Scripture interprets Scripture**, since passages like Exodus 14 and 15 explain each other.[^s12] It is especially useful for **Hebrew poetry**, where it shows how the song of the sea complements the story of **the Exodus**,[^s12][^s2] and for **wisdom literature** and **apocalyptic literature**, which modern readers often misread when they forget their kind.[^s10] It guards directly against **reading proverbs as promises**.[^s11]
