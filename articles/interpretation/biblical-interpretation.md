# Biblical Interpretation

Biblical interpretation is the work of understanding what the Bible means: what its human authors said to their first hearers, and what God says through their words to his people. The study of the principles that guide this work is usually called *hermeneutics*, and the close reading of a particular passage *exegesis*; Jews and Christians alike have long used both to discover the truths the Scriptures hold.[^s6] Everyone who opens a Bible is already interpreting it, so the real question is whether we do it carefully and faithfully.

## In the Bible itself

Scripture shows interpretation happening within its own pages. When Ezra read the law of Moses to the people gathered before the Water Gate, Levites moved among the crowd: "They read in the book, in the law of God, distinctly; and they gave the sense, so that they understood the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8, WEB). The people first wept, and were then sent away to feast and share with those who had nothing, because they understood what they had heard (Nehemiah 8:9-12).[^s1]

Centuries later Philip heard an Ethiopian official reading Isaiah aloud and asked, "Do you understand what you are reading?" The answer was disarmingly honest: "How can I, unless someone explains it to me?" (Acts 8:30-31, WEB). The passage was Isaiah's sheep led to the slaughter, and "beginning from this Scripture," Philip "preached to him Jesus" (Acts 8:35, WEB).[^s2] The risen Jesus had done the same on the road to Emmaus: "Beginning from Moses and from all the prophets, he explained to them in all the Scriptures the things concerning himself" (Luke 24:27, WEB).[^s3]

The New Testament also admits that interpretation can go wrong. Paul's letters, says 2 Peter, contain "some things hard to be understood", which the ignorant and unsettled twist, as they do the other Scriptures (2 Peter 3:16, WEB).[^s4] Paul urges Timothy to be a workman who need not be ashamed, "properly handling the Word of Truth" (2 Timothy 2:15, WEB; the KJV has "rightly dividing").[^s5]

## A short history

Jewish interpreters came to distinguish the plain sense of the text (*peshat*) from interpretive exposition (*derash*). The earliest rabbis treated their midrashic readings as the text's true meaning; later teachers separated the two, and the Babylonian Talmud held that interpretation should not wholly overturn the plain sense. Kabbalistic writers later spoke of four levels, remembered by the acronym PaRDeS.[^s10] In Alexandria, the Jewish philosopher Philo read the Scriptures with Platonic and Stoic categories.[^s6]

Early Christian teachers in Alexandria, Clement and Origen, took up allegorical reading from Philo and from Greek readers of Homer, without discarding the literal sense.[^s7] Origen, drawing on Proverbs 22:20, compared Scripture to a person with body, soul and spirit: the plain historical sense nourishes simpler readers, while deeper senses serve those who have made progress.[^s8] Teachers linked with Antioch, such as Theodore of Mopsuestia and John Chrysostom, gave more weight to the literal meaning and historical setting, and Jerome attended closely to grammar and to the Hebrew text.[^s7] Augustine made love the unifying principle: whoever thinks he understands Scripture but does not build up the twofold love of God and neighbour "does not yet understand them as he ought".[^s9]

In the Middle Ages Origen's three senses became four (literal, allegorical, moral and anagogical), while Thomas Aquinas, Nicholas of Lyra, and later Luther and Calvin insisted on the primacy of the literal sense.[^s6][^s12] Modern scholarship added the historical-critical method, which studies how texts came to be through textual, literary, form and redaction criticism; a 1993 Catholic document called it indispensable while noting its limits and welcoming approaches such as reading each book within the whole canon.[^s13]

## Why it matters

Careful interpretation lets Scripture speak on its own terms instead of echoing our assumptions back to us. It keeps Christ at the centre, as on the Emmaus road; it guards against twisting hard passages; and it leads, as in Nehemiah's day, from understanding to repentance and joy.[^s1][^s3][^s4] Augustine's test still serves every reader: a reading that does not build up love of God and neighbour has not yet reached Scripture's goal.[^s9]

Two misreadings are common. One is that interpretation belongs only to experts. Yet the Westminster Confession, while granting that not all of Scripture is equally plain, holds that what is necessary for salvation is clear enough for ordinary believers using ordinary means, and Acts 8 shows that a guide is a gift rather than a gatekeeper.[^s14][^s2] The other is that any meaning a reader finds is valid. The Pontifical Biblical Commission warns that some readings in history have been false and harmful, including anti-Jewish ones, so discernment is required.[^s13]

## Views across traditions

Christians broadly agree that God speaks in Scripture through human authors, that the whole Bible should be read as a unity centred on Christ, and that attention to genre and setting matters.[^s11][^s14][^s16] They differ on how the interpreter relates to the Church and its Tradition.

**Catholic Church.** *Dei Verbum* teaches that interpreters should seek what the sacred writers intended and what God wished to show by their words, attending to literary forms, to the unity of the whole of Scripture and to the living Tradition of the whole Church, with interpretation subject finally to the Church's judgement.[^s11] The Catechism distinguishes the literal sense from a spiritual sense subdivided into allegorical, moral and anagogical senses.[^s12]

**Eastern Orthodox Church.** Orthodox interpretation treats Church, Bible and Tradition as interdependent, receives the Church Fathers' biblical legacy, and holds Christ to be the key to the Old Testament. Tradition, expressed through the ecumenical councils and the Church's reception of teaching, is the final authority when interpretations conflict, while Orthodox scholars also engage modern biblical study.[^s16]

**Protestant traditions.** The Westminster Confession, central to the Reformed tradition, states that "the infallible rule of interpretation of Scripture is the Scripture itself", so unclear passages are read in the light of clearer ones.[^s14] The Lutheran Formula of Concord names the prophetic and apostolic Scriptures alone as the rule and norm of all teaching, treating the creeds and confessions as witnesses to how Scripture has been understood.[^s15]

## Connections

Biblical interpretation is the wider discipline of how to read **the Bible** well. Its history was shaped by **Origen**, whose threefold sense became the medieval fourfold scheme,[^s6] by **Philo of Alexandria**, from whom Alexandrian Christians learned allegorical reading,[^s7] and by Augustine's **On Christian Doctrine**, with its rule of love.[^s9] It works hand in hand with **textual criticism**, which establishes the text that interpretation then reads.[^s13] It is related to **allegory** and **typology**, to **rabbinic Judaism**'s distinction between plain sense and exposition,[^s10] to the **Reformed tradition**,[^s14] and to the pitfalls of **eisegesis** and **proof-texting** that careful readers learn to avoid, as 2 Peter 3:16 warns.[^s4]
