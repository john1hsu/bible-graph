# Lutheranism

Lutheranism is the branch of Protestant Christianity that traces its reading of the faith to the sixteenth-century reformer Martin Luther.[^s8] Opponents coined the name "Lutheran"; his followers preferred "evangelical", and the churches are also called the Churches of the Augsburg Confession, after the statement of faith presented to the emperor in 1530.[^s8] More than 77 million Lutherans belong to autonomous regional and national churches, most joined in the Lutheran World Federation.[^s8] This article describes how Lutherans read the Bible.

## Why it matters

Lutheranism grew from a struggle over how to read one verse of the Bible, and it has shaped Bible reading ever since: Luther's German translation put Scripture in ordinary hands, his catechisms taught believers from the text, and phrases such as "justification by faith" are now shared far beyond Lutheran churches.[^s14][^s13][^s22] Knowing what Lutherans mean by them helps every reader describe them fairly.

## Key teachings

**Scripture as the only rule and norm.** The Formula of Concord (1577) confesses that the prophetic and apostolic Scriptures of the Old and New Testaments are the "only rule and norm" by which all teachings and teachers are to be judged.[^s10] The creeds and confessions gathered in the Book of Concord are witnesses to how Scripture was understood, not judges beside it.[^s10] This is the Lutheran *sola Scriptura*.[^s8]

**Justification by grace through faith.** The Augsburg Confession teaches that people are justified not by their own merits or works but freely, for Christ's sake, through faith, citing Romans 3 and 4.[^s9] Paul writes that "a man is justified by faith apart from the works of the law" (Romans 3:28, WEB), "being justified freely by his grace" (3:24), so that boasting "is excluded" (3:27).[^s2] Luther recalled dreading "God's righteousness" in Romans 1:17 as punishing justice, until he saw it as the gift by which the merciful God justifies sinners through faith; he felt he had entered paradise "through open gates".[^s17][^s1]

**The Word joined to the sacraments.** Lutherans keep two sacraments, rejecting five of the traditional seven.[^s8] Luther's Small Catechism teaches that Baptism, water joined to God's Word, gives forgiveness to those who believe and means a daily dying and rising, citing Romans 6, where believers are "buried therefore with him through baptism" to "walk in newness of life" (Romans 6:4, WEB).[^s13][^s7] In the Lord's Supper the Formula of Concord confesses that Christ's true body and blood are truly present and received with the bread and wine.[^s12]

## How it reads Scripture

**Christ is the centre.** Jesus said the Scriptures "testify about me" (John 5:39, WEB).[^s5] Luther made this his touchstone: the truly sacred books preach and inculcate Christ.[^s16] The same test led him to place Hebrews, James, Jude and Revelation at the end of his 1522 New Testament, noting that they had long had a different reputation.[^s16][^s14]

**Law and Gospel.** The Formula of Concord calls the distinction between Law and Gospel "a particularly brilliant light", grounding it in Paul's charge to handle "the Word of Truth" rightly (2 Timothy 2:15, WEB).[^s11][^s4] The Law teaches what is right and exposes sin; the Gospel announces that Christ has paid for sin and freely gives forgiveness and eternal life.[^s11] Mixing them turns the Gospel into Law and robs troubled consciences of comfort.[^s11]

**The plain sense and the clarity of Scripture.** The confessions hold that Scripture interprets itself and is clear even where single words are obscure, read by ordinary grammar rather than imported philosophy.[^s18] So Lutherans take Christ's "this is my body" (Matthew 26:26, WEB) as it reads.[^s6][^s12]

**The Bible in the people's language.** Luther drafted his New Testament from Erasmus's Greek in about eleven weeks at the Wartburg; the "September Testament" of 1522 needed a second edition by December.[^s14] His complete Bible of 1534 set the Apocrypha apart as books not equal to Holy Scripture but useful and good to read.[^s15] Paul's reminder that Timothy had known the Scriptures from infancy and that "Every Scripture is God-breathed" (2 Timothy 3:15-16, WEB) sums up the Lutheran confidence that ordinary believers should read it.[^s3]

## Scholarly views

Lutheran scholars debate how far Law and Gospel works as a method of reading. Horace Hummel argued that it is a true hermeneutical principle for every text, though not the only one, and must stay tied to the written text; he noted that some Lutherans had narrowed Scripture to the Gospel alone.[^s19] Erling Teigen, in the same 1982 issue, centred Lutheran interpretation instead on Scripture's clarity and plain grammatical sense.[^s18]

## Views across traditions

These traditions confess Scripture as God's Word and salvation as God's gift in Christ; they differ on Scripture and church authority and on the sacraments.[^s10][^s22][^s23]

- **Lutheran.** Scripture alone is the rule and norm; the confessions witness to it.[^s10]
- **Catholic.** In the 1999 *Joint Declaration on the Doctrine of Justification*, Catholics and Lutherans together confess acceptance by God by grace alone, in faith in Christ's saving work, and say the old condemnations do not apply to the teaching each now presents; questions remain on the Word of God and church doctrine, authority and the sacraments.[^s22]
- **Eastern Orthodox.** Scripture is essential but cannot be isolated from the Church's living Tradition; accepting the Bible alone, apart from the Church, is rejected.[^s23]
- **Reformed.** Lutherans and the Reformed share much of the Reformation's teaching but differ over the Lord's Supper. At the Marburg Colloquy of 1529 Luther and Zwingli agreed on fourteen articles of common doctrine but not on Christ's presence in the Supper; Luther held to the literal "This is my body".[^s20] The Heidelberg Catechism teaches that the bread is not changed into Christ's body but is called his body by the nature of sacraments, a visible sign and assurance.[^s21] The Formula of Concord rejects a merely spiritual reception.[^s12]

## Connections

Lutheranism was **introduced by** Martin Luther and is **set out in** the Book of Concord.[^s8][^s10] It **contrasts with** the Reformed tradition over the Lord's Supper.[^s20] It holds that **Scripture interprets Scripture**,[^s18] grounds **justification** and **grace** in Paul's letters,[^s9][^s2] reads the **Mosaic covenant** through Law and Gospel,[^s11] set the **deuterocanonical books** apart in its 1534 Bible,[^s15] and draws its teaching on **baptism** from Romans 6.[^s13][^s7]
