# Reading in Context

Reading in context means letting a verse be part of the passage, the book and the Bible it belongs to, and hearing it first as its original readers would have heard it. Bible teachers often describe several rings of context: the immediate passage, the wider book, the whole canon of Scripture, and the historical and cultural setting in which the words were first written and received; the closer the ring, the more weight it usually carries.[^s8] Scholars speak in a similar way of the "world of the text" (its form, parts, characters and place in the book) and the "world behind the text" (the history it describes and the time in which it was written).[^s10]

The habit is as old as Scripture itself. When "the law of God" was read aloud to the gathered people in Nehemiah 8, the readers "gave the sense, so that they understood the reading" (Nehemiah 8:8, WEB): they did not only read the words, they explained them.[^s4]

## Why it matters

Christians read the Bible because they believe God speaks through it, and that is exactly why context matters: we want to hear what God is saying, not what we wish he were saying. The Catechism of the Catholic Church asks readers to attend to what the human authors truly wanted to affirm, and to the conditions of their time and culture and the literary genres they used.[^s5] The Westminster Confession, a Reformed Protestant standard, likewise teaches that Scripture is its own best interpreter, so that a less clear passage is understood by others that speak more plainly.[^s6] Both traditions, in their own terms, ask readers to look beyond the single verse.

Three much-loved verses show what is at stake. Jeremiah 29:11 was written in a letter "to all the captivity", the people deported to Babylon (Jeremiah 29:1, 4).[^s1] Instead of promising a quick rescue, God told them to build houses, plant gardens and "seek the peace of the city" where they had been carried, warned them against prophets who said otherwise, and promised to bring them home "after seventy years" (Jeremiah 29:7-10, WEB).[^s1] The promise of "thoughts of peace, and not of evil" in verse 11 is a word of long-range hope to a community in exile, and Luther Seminary's study notes that the letter calls them to live faithfully through a long wait.[^s7] BibleProject warns that turning the verse into a slogan of personal success echoes the very false prophets Jeremiah opposed, while still finding in it real comfort: God's purposes are good even when restoration is slow.[^s9]

Philippians 4:13, "I can do all things through Christ who strengthens me" (WEB), comes in a passage where Paul thanks the Philippians for their gift and says he has learned to be content whether "filled" or "hungry", whether he has plenty or is in need (Philippians 4:10-14).[^s2] Its "all things" is first of all contentment in every circumstance, not a guarantee of winning. And Revelation 3:20, "Behold, I stand at the door and knock" (WEB), is part of the risen Christ's message to the church in Laodicea, ending "let him hear what the Spirit says to the assemblies" (Revelation 3:14-22).[^s3] It is first a call to a complacent congregation to open its door to him; it may still be used to invite people to faith, but its context makes it a word to believers as well.[^s8]

## How to apply it

Start small: before you settle on a verse's meaning, read the paragraph, then the chapter. Ask who is speaking, to whom and why, and follow the argument or story through the verse.[^s8] Notice the kind of writing you are reading, because the Catechism observes that truth is expressed differently in history, prophecy, poetry and other forms.[^s5] Use a study Bible or Bible dictionary for background, but treat the Bible itself as the first and best source of its own context.[^s8] Then read the passage alongside the rest of Scripture: Catholic teaching speaks of the content and unity of the whole Scripture with Christ at its centre, read within the Church's living Tradition and the coherence of the faith,[^s5] and Reformed teaching of comparing Scripture with Scripture.[^s6] Only after that, ask how it applies today.

## Common mistakes

- **Verse-hopping.** Lifting a line out of its paragraph to prove a point is the habit known as proof-texting, and it can make a verse say the opposite of what its author meant.[^s8]
- **Forgetting the first audience.** Promises addressed to Israel in exile, or to a particular church, are still God's word to us, but they reach us through their first setting, not around it.[^s9]
- **Swinging to the other extreme.** Context does not make a passage irrelevant; the aim is to hear what it meant so that we can rightly hear what it means now.[^s8]
- **Burying the text in background.** Historical detail should illuminate the passage, not replace it, and readers should be clear which kind of context they are examining.[^s8][^s10]
- **Reading our own world in.** Every reader brings assumptions; noticing the gap between the world behind the text and our own helps guard against reading our ideas into it.[^s10]

## Connections

Reading in context is a foundation of **biblical interpretation** more broadly.[^s8] It is the main remedy for **proof-texting**, quoting verses without their setting,[^s8] and it helps guard against **eisegesis**, reading our own assumptions into a text, by attending to the world behind the text.[^s10] It works hand in hand with **Scripture interprets Scripture**, since the widest ring of context is the whole Bible,[^s6] and with **reading by genre**, since knowing what kind of writing a passage is forms part of its context.[^s5]
