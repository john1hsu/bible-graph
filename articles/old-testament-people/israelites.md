# Israelites

The Israelites are the people whose story runs through almost the whole Old Testament. Their name comes from their ancestor Jacob, who is told, "Your name will no longer be called Jacob, but Israel," because he has fought with God and with men and prevailed (Genesis 32:28, WEB).[^s1] His descendants are therefore the "children of Israel" (literally "sons of Israel"), a family that becomes a nation, is bound to God by covenant, and carries God's promise through centuries of faithfulness and failure.[^s2][^s3] Christians read this story as their own family history in faith, since Christ himself came from this people.[^s8]

## Why it matters

Nearly every Old Testament book is addressed to, or written about, this people, so knowing who they were is the key to reading it well. Their identity rests on an act of grace: Deuteronomy says God set his love on them not because they were numerous, since they were the fewest of peoples, but because he loved them and kept the oath sworn to their fathers (Deuteronomy 7:6-9).[^s5] Paul, "an Israelite" of the tribe of Benjamin (Romans 11:1, WEB), lists their gifts — adoption, glory, covenants, law, worship, promises and Christ himself (Romans 9:4-5).[^s8][^s9]

## Who they were

**One family.** Genesis traces the people to Jacob's twelve sons by Leah, Rachel and their servants Bilhah and Zilpah: Reuben, Simeon, Levi, Judah, Issachar, Zebulun, Joseph, Benjamin, Dan, Naphtali, Gad and Asher (Genesis 35:23-26).[^s2] When God confirms the name Israel, he promises that "a nation and a company of nations" will come from Jacob and gives him the land promised to Abraham and Isaac (Genesis 35:10-12, WEB).[^s2]

**Tribes, clans and households.** Israelite society was built in layers — tribe, clan and household — with elders settling disputes; before the monarchy the tribes were loosely bound by kinship, a shared sanctuary and the pilgrim feasts.[^s10] Tribal identity lasted into the New Testament, where Anna and Paul still know their tribe and "the twelve tribes" can stand for the whole people of God.[^s10]

**A covenant people.** What made these tribes one nation was above all their relationship with God. At Sinai, God reminds "the house of Jacob" how he carried them out of Egypt and promises that, if they keep his covenant, they will be his own possession, "a kingdom of priests and a holy nation"; the people answer together, "All that Yahweh has spoken we will do" (Exodus 19:3-8, WEB).[^s4]

## In the biblical story

**From family to nation.** Seventy members of Jacob's household go down to Egypt; after Joseph's generation dies, the Israelites multiply until a new Pharaoh fears them and enslaves them (Exodus 1:1-9).[^s3] God brings them out under Moses, gives the covenant at Sinai and leads them toward Canaan, where the tribes settle under Joshua and the judges.[^s13]

**Kingdom and division.** Under Saul, David and Solomon the tribes become a monarchy centred on Jerusalem.[^s13] After Solomon, the northern tribes cry, "What portion have we in David?" and make Jeroboam king, leaving only Judah with David's house (1 Kings 12:16-20, WEB).[^s6] From then on "Israel" can mean either the whole people or the northern kingdom, so readers need to watch the context.[^s13]

**Exile and survival.** Assyria takes Samaria and carries the northern Israelites away, which 2 Kings explains as the result of their turning to other gods; "there was none left but the tribe of Judah only" (2 Kings 17:5-18, WEB).[^s7] After Babylon's exile of Judah and the return under Persia, the restored community in Judah carries Israel's heritage forward.[^s13]

**In the New Testament.** Paul grieves that many of his fellow Israelites have not believed, yet insists God's word has not failed and that God "didn't reject his people" (Romans 9:6; 11:2, WEB).[^s8][^s9] Gentile believers, he says, are wild olive shoots grafted into Israel's tree; they must not boast, for "the gifts and the calling of God are irrevocable" (Romans 11:17-29, WEB).[^s9] The Catholic Church, drawing on these verses at the Second Vatican Council, taught that the Church is nourished by that root and that Jews must not be presented as rejected or accursed by God.[^s14]

## Scholarly views

The **traditional view**, held by many Christian and Jewish readers, takes the biblical account from the patriarchs through Egypt and the exodus to the settlement in Canaan as the essential history of Israel's origins. Evangelical archaeologists such as Gary Byers point to the stela of Pharaoh Merneptah (about 1210 BC), the earliest mention of Israel outside the Bible, as placing Israel in Canaan in the period the Bible assigns to the judges.[^s12]

**Many critical historians** agree on the stela's importance but read the evidence differently. Richard Nelson notes that the stela names Israel as a people, not a place, and links Israel's emergence to small, unwalled highland villages of about 1200 BC; because their material culture continues Canaanite traditions, he concludes that most early Israelites — farmers and settling herders — came from within the land.[^s11] Other archaeologists propose origins among pastoralists on the edges of settled areas or in the steppe lands east of the Jordan, so the question remains open.[^s15] The Posen Library observes that, as with many ancient peoples, Israel's origins are obscure.[^s13] Whichever reconstruction one finds most persuasive, the Bible's own emphasis is that Israel's identity came from God's call and covenant, not from its size or strength.[^s5]

## Common misreadings

"Israel" can mean Jacob himself, the twelve tribes or the northern kingdom alone, so context matters.[^s1][^s6] Nor was Israel chosen for its virtue: Deuteronomy grounds the choice in love and promise,[^s5] and Paul warns Gentile Christians against arrogance toward the natural branches.[^s9]

## Connections

The Israelites **illustrate** the Mosaic covenant, since Exodus 19 shows them becoming God's covenant people at Sinai.[^s4] They also **illustrate** the Abrahamic covenant, because the promise of land and nations made to Abraham passes through Isaac to Jacob and his descendants (Genesis 35:11-12).[^s2] Their existence is **attested outside the Bible** by the Merneptah Stele.[^s11] They are closely tied to Jacob, Abraham and Moses, the Exodus, Sinai, the Shema, the twelve tribes, the Babylonian exile and Second Temple Judaism.[^s2][^s4][^s10][^s13]
