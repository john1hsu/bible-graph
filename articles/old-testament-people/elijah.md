# Elijah

Elijah is the fiery prophet of the northern kingdom of Israel, who stood almost alone against the worship of Baal in the days of King Ahab and Queen Jezebel.[^s2][^s14] His name means "the LORD (Yahweh) is my God," and his story reads like a sermon on that name.[^s16][^s15]

## Why it matters

Elijah presses the question every generation faces: will God's people serve the LORD alone?[^s2][^s15] He was bold one day and despairing the next, and God met him in both.[^s3][^s17] He was taken to heaven without dying, and the New Testament returns to him for John the Baptist, the Transfiguration and prayer.[^s6][^s9][^s13]

## Life and story

**Drought and the widow.** "Elijah the Tishbite," of Gilead, tells Ahab there will be neither dew nor rain except at his word (1 Kings 17:1, WEB).[^s1] Ravens fed him by the brook Cherith; then a widow in Zarephath of Sidon fed him from a jar of meal and oil that did not run out. When her son died, Elijah stretched himself on the child three times and prayed, and the boy lived (17:3-24).[^s1] Jesus later recalled that Elijah was sent to this foreign widow (Luke 4:25-26).[^s11]

**Mount Carmel.** Ahab greeted him as "you troubler of Israel" (1 Kings 18:17, WEB).[^s2] Before 450 prophets of Baal Elijah asked the people, "How long will you waver between the two sides?" (18:19-21, WEB). Elijah rebuilt the LORD's altar with twelve stones, one for each tribe, and prayed; fire fell, and the people cried, "Yahweh, he is God!" (18:30-39, WEB).[^s2] Baal's prophets were killed at the Kishon, and after Elijah prayed with his face between his knees, a small cloud brought rain (18:40-45).[^s2] Baal was honoured as the rain-giver, so the drought struck at his very claim.[^s15]

**Horeb.** Threatened by Jezebel, Elijah fled into the wilderness and prayed, "It is enough. Now, O Yahweh, take away my life" (1 Kings 19:4, WEB).[^s3] An angel twice woke him to eat, and he walked forty days to Horeb. The LORD was not in the wind, the earthquake or the fire, but came in "a still small voice" (19:8-12, WEB).[^s3] To God's question, "What are you doing here, Elijah?", he answered that he alone was left. God sent him back to anoint Hazael, Jehu and Elisha, and told him seven thousand had not bowed to Baal (19:13-18).[^s3]

**Naboth and Ahaziah.** After Jezebel had Naboth killed for his vineyard, Elijah confronted Ahab: "Have you killed and also taken possession?" (1 Kings 21:19, WEB). Ahab humbled himself, and judgement was deferred to his son's days (21:27-29).[^s4] Elijah later rebuked Ahab's son Ahaziah for consulting Baal Zebub, as if "there is no God in Israel" (2 Kings 1:3, WEB).[^s5][^s14]

**The whirlwind.** At the Jordan Elijah parted the water with his mantle; Elisha asked for "a double portion of your spirit," and a chariot and horses of fire parted them as Elijah went up by a whirlwind into heaven (2 Kings 2:1-11, WEB).[^s6] Elisha took up the mantle, and the prophets said, "The spirit of Elijah rests on Elisha" (2:13-15, WEB).[^s6]

## Legacy in Scripture

Malachi promises, "I will send you Elijah the prophet" before the day of the LORD, to turn the hearts of fathers and children (Malachi 4:5-6, WEB).[^s7] John the Baptist would go "in the spirit and power of Elijah" (Luke 1:17, WEB), and Jesus says of John, "this is Elijah, who is to come" (Matthew 11:14, WEB).[^s10][^s8] After the Transfiguration, where "Moses and Elijah appeared," Jesus said Elijah had already come, meaning John (Matthew 17:3, 10-13, WEB).[^s9] Paul uses God's seven thousand as a picture of the remnant chosen by grace (Romans 11:2-5),[^s19] and James calls Elijah "a man with a nature like ours" whose prayers shut and opened the sky (James 5:17-18, WEB).[^s13]

## Views across traditions

All Christian traditions honour Elijah as a great prophet whose story points to Christ.[^s17][^s18][^s14] The *Catechism of the Catholic Church* calls him the "father" of the prophets and a teacher of prayer;[^s17] Greek and Latin calendars keep his feast on 20 July, and Carmelites long regarded him as their order's founder.[^s14] The Orthodox Church prays to him in drought and holds that he will return as forerunner of Christ's Second Coming.[^s18] In Judaism Elijah is the expected herald of the Messiah, welcomed as a guest at the Passover Seder and believed present at every circumcision.[^s16]

## Scholarly views

The stories in 1 Kings 17 to 2 Kings 2 are our only sources for Elijah; no inscription names him.[^s15][^s16] The *Catholic Encyclopedia* judged that they bear the stamp of an almost contemporary age and took shape in northern Israel.[^s14] Collins agrees they arose in the north before Josiah's reform, but classes them as legendary prophet stories with symbolic meaning and voices "some doubt" about Elijah's historicity; he sees the editors of Kings at work in the Horeb chapter.[^s15] The *Jewish Encyclopedia* notes signs of more than one author.[^s16] Traditional Jewish and Christian readers have received the accounts as history.[^s14][^s18]

## Common misreadings

Elijah is no superhero: after Carmel he wanted to die, and James calls him a man like us.[^s3][^s13] God answered with food, rest, his presence and a renewed task; persistent despair is a reason to talk with a pastor, counsellor or doctor.[^s3] Nor was Elijah as alone as he felt: God had kept seven thousand.[^s3][^s19] The "still small voice" (KJV, WEB), rendered by others as a gentle whisper or a sound of sheer silence, teaches that God was not in the spectacle; it is not a technique for hearing inner voices.[^s3][^s15] And Kishon is no model for Christians: when James and John asked whether to call down fire on a village, Jesus rebuked them (Luke 9:54-55).[^s12]

## Connections

Elijah **was a member of** the Israelites, a man of Gilead serving the northern kingdom.[^s14] As the prophet promised in Malachi, he **prefigures** John the Baptist, whom Jesus called the Elijah who was to come.[^s9][^s8] James makes his prayers an example that **illustrates** prayer.[^s13] His contest on Carmel **illustrates** monotheism, the confession his name makes.[^s16][^s2] His ministry **is explained by** the worship of Baal, the rain-giver whose power the drought challenged.[^s15] He is linked to Moses (both met God at Horeb and appeared at the Transfiguration), Elisha, Ahab, Jezebel and Malachi.[^s3][^s9][^s6][^s7]
