## background-and-culture

### [Pharisees](../articles/background-and-culture/pharisees.md) — Group

Read: Matthew 23:1-28; Mark 7:1-13; Acts 23:6-9; Philippians 3:5-6; Acts 5:34-39; John 3:1-2; Luke 13:31; Luke 7:36

Study questions:
- According to Acts 23:6-8, which beliefs did the Pharisees hold that the Sadducees rejected?
- In Matthew 23:2-3, what does Jesus tell the crowds to do with the Pharisees' teaching, and what does he warn them not to copy?
- Nicodemus (John 3:1-2) and Gamaliel (Acts 5:34-39) were both Pharisees. How do their stories complicate the picture of the Pharisees as simply Jesus's enemies?
- Jesus's sharpest warnings were aimed at devout, Bible-loving people. Where might Matthew 23:23-28 speak to our own church life today?

### [Rabbinic Judaism](../articles/background-and-culture/rabbinic-judaism.md) — Tradition

Read: Nehemiah 8:1-8; Exodus 34:27; Mark 7:1-13; Matthew 23:2; Hosea 6:6; Matthew 9:10-13; Acts 15:21; Romans 11:1-2; Romans 11:28-29

Study questions:
- In Nehemiah 8:1-8, what do Ezra and the Levites do besides reading the law aloud, and why might that matter for the later Jewish practice of interpreting Torah?
- In Mark 7:1-13, what exactly does Jesus criticise about the "tradition of the elders" — the existence of tradition, or tradition that sets aside God's command?
- Jesus quotes Hosea 6:6 in Matthew 9:13, and the rabbinic sage Yohanan ben Zakkai is remembered as using the same verse after the Temple fell. What do the two uses have in common?
- Paul says in Romans 11:28-29 that God's gifts and calling are irrevocable. How should that shape the way Christians speak about Jewish neighbours and their reading of Scripture?

### [Sadducees](../articles/background-and-culture/sadducees.md) — Group

- Why were the priests and the Sadducees "upset" with Peter and John in Acts 4:1-2?
- Jesus tells the Sadducees they know neither "the Scriptures nor the power of God" (Mark 12:24). How might sincere Bible readers today make a similar mistake, and what helps guard against it?

### [Second Temple Judaism](../articles/background-and-culture/second-temple-judaism.md) — Group

### [Synagogue](../articles/background-and-culture/synagogue.md) — Place

### [Genre in the Bible](../articles/interpretation/genre-in-the-bible.md) — Method

Read: Exodus 14:21-31; Exodus 15:1-21; Psalm 98:7-9; Proverbs 26:4-5; Luke 1:1-4; Revelation 1:1-4

1. Before interpreting a passage, ask what kind of writing it is (narrative, law, poetry, proverb, prophecy, gospel, letter or apocalypse) and look for clues the book gives about itself.
2. Note the features of that kind of writing, such as parallel lines and imagery in poetry, short general sayings in proverbs, or greetings and arguments in letters.
3. Ask what that kind of writing is designed to do (tell what happened, command, praise, give practical wisdom, warn, comfort or encourage endurance) and let that purpose guide your conclusions.
4. Compare passages where the Bible tells the same thing in two forms, such as the Red Sea in Exodus 14 and 15, and notice what each form adds.
5. Check your genre judgment with a study Bible or Bible dictionary, and hold it humbly where careful readers disagree.
6. Apply the passage in a way that fits its genre, taking a promise as a promise, a proverb as wise counsel and a song as praise to join.

*A small-group member reads Proverbs 26:4 and 26:5 side by side and concludes that the Bible contradicts itself.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Verse 4 says don't answer a fool and verse 5 says answer him, so one of these must be wrong, and Proverbs can't be trusted. | These are proverbs, not laws. Placed side by side, they teach that sometimes silence is wise and sometimes a reply is needed, and wisdom lies in discerning which situation I am in. |

**Why:** Proverbs are short sayings that capture general truths for particular situations; reading them as rigid rules or guarantees misses how the genre works.

Study questions:
- Read Exodus 14:21-31 and then Exodus 15:1-10. What does the song add to the narrative account of the same event?
- Psalm 98:8 calls on rivers to clap their hands. What is the psalmist inviting us to see and do?
- How does Luke describe his purpose in Luke 1:1-4, and what does that tell you about the kind of book he is writing?
- Revelation 1:1-4 calls the book a revelation, a prophecy and (by its greeting) a letter. How might each of these shape the way you read it?
- Which part of the Bible do you find hardest to read, and could knowing more about its kind of writing help?

### [Lutheranism](../articles/interpretation/lutheranism.md) — Tradition

### [Reading in Context](../articles/interpretation/reading-in-context.md) — Method

Read: Jeremiah 29:1-14; Philippians 4:10-14; Revelation 3:14-22; Nehemiah 8:8

1. Read the whole paragraph, and then the whole chapter, around the verse before deciding what it means.
2. Ask who is speaking, to whom, and in what situation; note what the book itself tells you about its author and first readers.
3. Trace the flow of thought by asking what comes just before, what comes just after, and how the verse fits the argument or story.
4. Identify the kind of writing (letter, poem, prophecy, narrative) and read it the way that kind of writing is meant to be read.
5. Use a study Bible or Bible dictionary to fill in historical and cultural background, without letting the background drown out the text.
6. Compare the passage with other Scripture on the same subject, especially clearer passages, and see how it fits the whole story that centres on Christ.
7. Only then ask what the passage means for you or your church, applying it in a way its first readers would recognise as faithful to it.

*A friend facing a job interview posts Jeremiah 29:11 as a guarantee that everything will go well.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| God has promised me success and no setbacks, so this interview is bound to work out. | God told exiles in Babylon to settle in and seek the city's peace, promising a future after seventy years; I can trust his good purposes and be faithful where I am, even if this interview fails. |

**Why:** Reading the whole letter (Jeremiah 29:1-14) shows a promise of long-range hope to a community in exile, which still comforts believers today but not as a pledge of quick personal success.

Study questions:
- According to Jeremiah 29:1-10, who first received the letter containing verse 11, where were they, and how long did God say they would wait?
- Read Philippians 4:10-14. What "all things" is Paul talking about in verse 13, and how does that change the way the verse is often used?
- Revelation 3:20 is often used in evangelism. To whom is it first addressed (Revelation 3:14-22), and what does that add to its meaning?
- Which verse do you hold dear but have never read in its surrounding chapter, and what do you notice when you read the whole chapter this week?

### [Reformed Tradition](../articles/interpretation/reformed-tradition.md) — Tradition

Read: 2 Timothy 3:14-17; Ephesians 1:4-6; Ephesians 2:20; Romans 11:36; Genesis 17:7; Acts 2:39; 1 Corinthians 10:16

Study questions:
- Read 2 Timothy 3:14-17. What does Paul say the sacred writings are able to do for Timothy, and what is every Scripture useful for?
- In Ephesians 1:4-6, what did God do "before the foundation of the world", and to what end? Why might the Westminster Confession ask that this teaching be handled with special care?

### [Scripture Interprets Scripture](../articles/interpretation/scripture-interprets-scripture.md) — Method

Read: Matthew 4:1-11; Psalm 91:11-12; Deuteronomy 6:16; Deuteronomy 8:3; Luke 24:27; Luke 24:44-45; Acts 17:11

1. Read the passage in its own setting first (paragraph, chapter, book) so you know what it says before you compare it with anything else.
2. Look up the cross-references in a study Bible, starting with any passage the text quotes or echoes, and read each one in its own context too.
3. When the New Testament quotes the Old, open the Old Testament passage and ask what it meant there and why the New Testament writer reached for it.
4. Gather the passages that treat the same subject directly and at length, and let these clearer, fuller passages shed light on brief or puzzling ones.
5. Check that each link is a real link of meaning, not just a shared English word that may mean different things in different books.
6. When passages seem to pull in different directions, hold both and ask how they fit together before choosing one and setting the other aside.
7. Test your conclusion against the main lines of the whole biblical story and, as your church teaches, against its wider reading of the faith.

*Someone reads Psalm 91:11-12, "they will bear you up in their hands", and concludes that God guarantees protection from the consequences of any deliberate risk they choose to take.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| God promised his angels will catch me, so I can push my luck and he has to rescue me. | Psalm 91 is a song of trust for the one who makes God their refuge; Jesus, offered this very verse as a dare, answered from Deuteronomy 6:16 that we must not put God to the test. |

**Why:** In Matthew 4:5-7 the devil quotes the psalm on its own, and Jesus answers "Again, it is written", letting another Scripture show how the promise is meant to be held.

Study questions:
- In Matthew 4:1-11, where do Jesus's replies in verses 4 and 7 come from, and what situation of Israel's were those words first spoken into?
- What does the devil leave out when he uses Psalm 91 in Matthew 4:6, and what difference does Deuteronomy 6:16 make?
- According to Luke 24:27 and 24:44-45, how did the risen Jesus help his disciples understand the Scriptures as a whole?
- What did the people of Beroea do with Paul's preaching in Acts 17:11, and what would it look like for your study group to do the same?
- Is there a verse you have built a lot on by itself? Which other passages on the same subject could you read alongside it this week?

## study-practices

### [Choosing a Bible Translation](../articles/study-practices/choosing-a-bible-translation.md) — Practice

Read: Nehemiah 8:8; Acts 2:6-8; 1 Corinthians 14:9-11; 1 Corinthians 14:19

1. Decide what the Bible is mainly for right now (daily reading, close study, reading aloud in church, reading with children or new readers), because different translations serve different purposes.
2. Read the translation's preface to learn its approach (more word-for-word or more meaning-based), which Hebrew and Greek texts it translates, and how it uses footnotes.
3. Check which books it contains and, if you belong to a church that approves translations, whether this one (or this edition of it) is approved.
4. Choose one main translation you can stay with for years, so you come to know its wording well.
5. When a passage matters for study, teaching or a disagreement, compare at least one translation from the other end of the spectrum and read the footnotes before drawing conclusions.
6. Where versions differ sharply, look the verse up in a good commentary to see why scholars render it differently, rather than picking the wording you like best.

*In a small group, two members read the same verse aloud from different translations and the wording does not match. One of them says one Bible must be wrong.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Your version changed the Bible. Mine says it the way I like, so I'll go with mine. | Let's check the footnotes and the prefaces: one version is more word-for-word and the other more meaning-based, and we can see from a commentary what the original says and why they render it differently. |

**Why:** Every translation is also an interpretation, so differences usually show the range of the original's meaning rather than a corruption, and comparing versions with their notes keeps the group close to what the text actually says.

Study questions:
- In Nehemiah 8:8, what did the Levites do besides reading the law aloud, and what was the result for the people listening?
- According to Acts 2:6-8, what amazed the crowd at Pentecost about the way they heard the apostles?
- In 1 Corinthians 14:9-11 and 14:19, why does Paul insist on words the hearers can understand in the assembly?
- What is the difference between a word-for-word translation, a meaning-based translation and a paraphrase, and when might each be useful?
- Which translation do you read most, and what do you know about how and by whom it was made? What would you gain by reading a passage this week in a second version?

### [Inductive Bible Study](../articles/study-practices/inductive-bible-study.md) — Practice

Read: Acts 17:10-12; Ezra 7:10; Psalm 119:18; James 1:22-25; Mark 4:35-41; Psalm 107:23-30

1. Pray for understanding, then choose a unit to study (a paragraph, a chapter or a whole short book) and read it through several times without notes or commentary.
2. Observe before you explain - write down who is involved, what happens or is argued, where and when, and note repeated words, contrasts, questions, turning points and the kind of writing it is.
3. Turn your observations into questions (what does this mean, why is it here, why is it said this way?) and answer them first from the passage and its surrounding book.
4. Only after your own study, check your conclusions against a good study Bible, Bible dictionary or commentary, and revise where the evidence points elsewhere.
5. Correlate the passage with the rest of Scripture, asking how other texts on the same subject, and the whole story centred on Christ, shed light on it.
6. Ask what the passage meant for its first readers and what it calls you to believe, pray or do now, and write down one concrete response.
7. Share what you found with others, ideally in a small group, so that your reading can be tested and enriched.

*A small group opens Mark 4:35-41, the stilling of the storm, during a hard season for one of its members.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This story promises that Jesus will make the storms in my life go away if I have enough faith. | The disciples end by asking who Jesus is, since even wind and sea obey him; the passage first shows his identity and calls me to trust him in the storm, whether or not it stops. |

**Why:** Observing the closing question (Mark 4:41) and its echo of God calming the sea in Psalm 107:29 moves the group from a quick application to the passage's own focus on who Jesus is.

Study questions:
- According to Acts 17:10-12, what did the Jews of Beroea do with Paul's message, and how often?
- Ezra 7:10 describes Ezra's commitment to God's law in three verbs. What are they, and why might the order matter?
- James 1:22-25 compares a hearer who does not act to someone looking in a mirror. What point is James making about study and application?
- Read Mark 4:35-41 slowly and list everything you observe before deciding what it means. What did you notice that you had missed before?
- Which of the phases (observation, interpretation, correlation, application) do you tend to skip, and how could your group help you slow down?

### [Lectio Divina](../articles/study-practices/lectio-divina.md) — Practice

Read: Psalm 1:1-3; Joshua 1:8; Luke 2:19; Luke 10:38-42

1. Choose a short passage of a few verses, such as a Gospel scene or a psalm, settle into quiet, and ask God to speak to you through his word.
2. Lectio - read the passage slowly several times, aloud if you can, and first ask what the text says in itself and where it sits in its chapter.
3. Meditatio - stay with a word or phrase that stands out, turn it over, and ask what that meaning says to your life today.
4. Oratio - answer God in your own words, with thanks, confession, questions or requests drawn from the passage.
5. Contemplatio - rest quietly before God for a few minutes without trying to produce more thoughts.
6. Actio - choose one concrete way to live out what you received, and test any strong impression against the wider Bible and with your church or small group.

*A member of a small group prays through Luke 10:38-42, the visit of Jesus to Martha and Mary, during a busy season of church volunteering.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Jesus' words to Martha about the one thing needed jumped out at me, so God is telling me to quit the hospitality team. | In the scene, Jesus defends Mary for sitting and listening to him while Martha is pulled apart by her serving; he invites me to put listening to him first and to notice where worry crowds it out, and my serving can grow from that. |

**Why:** Lectio begins by asking what the text says in itself before asking what it says to me, so a striking phrase is heard within its scene instead of as a free-floating private message.

Study questions:
- According to Psalm 1:1-3, where does the blessed person find delight, and what picture describes the result of meditating day and night?
- Joshua 1:8 says the book of the law should not depart from Joshua's mouth. What does that suggest about how meditation was practised, and what is it meant to lead to?
- What did Mary do with the shepherds' words in Luke 2:19, and how is that like the step of meditatio?
- In Luke 10:38-42, what is Mary doing, what is Martha doing, and why does Jesus defend Mary?
- Which movement (reading, meditation, prayer, contemplation or action) comes hardest for you, and how could a small group help you grow in it?

### [Praying the Psalms](../articles/study-practices/praying-the-psalms.md) — Practice

Read: Psalm 13; Psalm 88; Psalm 31:5; Luke 23:46; Psalm 119:164; Mark 14:26; Acts 4:25; Colossians 3:16; James 5:13

1. Choose a psalm, either one that fits your day or the next one in a regular cycle (your church's daily office, the whole Psalter in a month, or the Byzantine weekly order), and read it aloud slowly.
2. Notice what kind of psalm it is (praise, lament, thanksgiving, trust or wisdom) and look for the place where it turns, such as the move from complaint to trust in Psalm 13.
3. Pray it in the first person, letting its words become your own speech to God; where its mood is not yours today, pray it with and for those who do feel it.
4. Pause after the psalm to name your own situation before God in a sentence or two, using a word or image the psalm gave you.
5. Treat the hard lines honestly, handing anger and the cry for justice over to God rather than turning them into curses on anyone.
6. End with praise, using the traditional "Glory be to the Father" or a sentence of your own, and come back to the same psalm on other days or pray it with others.

*After a hard week, a member of a small group reads Psalm 13 and feels uneasy praying its opening words, "How long, Yahweh? Will you forget me forever?"*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Christians shouldn't complain to God, so I'll skip the first verses and just claim verse 5 about trusting his loving kindness. | David says 'How long?' to God, not about God behind his back; I can pray the complaint honestly, ask God to answer, and keep praying until I can say with verse 5 that I trust his loving kindness, even if that takes longer than six verses. |

**Why:** The lament is itself addressed to God and is part of the prayer the psalm teaches; skipping to the happy line removes the very path from distress to trust that the psalm walks.

Study questions:
- What does the psalmist keep asking "How long?" about in Psalm 13:1-2, and to whom are the questions addressed?
- Where does Psalm 13 turn from complaint to trust, and what does the psalmist decide to do in verses 5-6?
- Compare Psalm 31:5 with Luke 23:46. What does it suggest that Jesus' last words on the cross in Luke are the words of a psalm?
- Psalm 88 ends in darkness without a turn to praise. Why might it still be good that such a prayer is in the Bible?
- Colossians 3:16 and James 5:13 link psalms and songs with teaching, suffering and joy. How could your small group pray a psalm together this week?
- Which psalm would you find hardest to pray as your own right now, and what might it teach you if you did?

### [Scripture Memorization](../articles/study-practices/scripture-memorization.md) — Practice

Read: Psalm 119:11; Deuteronomy 6:6-9; Deuteronomy 11:18; Proverbs 7:3; Colossians 3:16; Matthew 4:1-11; John 14:26

1. Choose a passage that is a whole unit, such as a psalm, a parable or a paragraph of a letter, and read it in its chapter first so you know what it means.
2. Learn a small portion at a time, a verse or a sentence, saying it aloud and repeating it until you can recite it without looking.
3. Add the next portion only when the earlier ones come easily, then recite the whole section from the beginning each day.
4. Review what you have already learned on a regular schedule, daily at first and then less often, so that it moves into long-term memory.
5. Pray the words back to God and ask how they shape what you believe and do, so that memorizing leads to meditation and obedience.
6. Learn with others, for example by reciting a passage together in a small group or with your family.

*A new believer wants to start memorizing Scripture and plans to work through a box of single "promise" verses printed on cards.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| I'll learn fifty unrelated verses this year and quote whichever one fits my mood that day. | I'll learn Colossians 3 a few verses at a time, recite it aloud each day, review what I've learned, and ask what each part means in Paul's letter. |

**Why:** Learning a passage within its setting, with daily review and understanding, guards against treating verses as detached slogans or formulas repeated without being understood.

Study questions:
- According to Psalm 119:11, where does the psalmist keep God's word, and for what purpose?
- In Deuteronomy 6:6-9 and 11:18, where are God's words to be kept, and in which everyday settings are they to be talked about?
- In Matthew 4:1-11, how does Jesus answer each temptation, and what does the devil's own use of Scripture show about the difference between knowing words and understanding them?
- What does Colossians 3:16 suggest about memorizing Scripture together as a church rather than only alone?
- What passage would you most like to carry with you in a hard season, and how could your small group help you learn it?

### [Mosaic Covenant](../articles/themes-and-theology/mosaic-covenant.md) — Covenant
