## interpretation

### [Genre in the Bible](../articles/interpretation/genre-in-the-bible.md) — Method

Read: Exodus 14:21-31; Exodus 15:1-21; Psalm 98:7-9; Proverbs 26:4-5; Luke 1:1-4; Revelation 1:1-4

1. Before interpreting a passage, ask what kind of writing it is (narrative, law, poetry, proverb, prophecy, gospel, letter or apocalypse) and look for clues the book gives about itself.
2. Note the features of that kind of writing, such as parallel lines and imagery in poetry, short general sayings in proverbs, or greetings and arguments in letters.
3. Ask what that kind of writing is designed to do (tell what happened, command, praise, give practical wisdom, warn, comfort or encourage endurance) and let that purpose guide your conclusions.
4. Compare passages where the Bible tells the same thing in two forms, such as the Red Sea in Exodus 14 and 15, and notice what each form adds.
5. Check your genre judgment with a study Bible or Bible dictionary, and hold it humbly where careful readers disagree.
6. Apply the passage in a way that fits its genre, taking a promise as a promise, a proverb as wise counsel and a song as praise to join.

*A small-group member reads Proverbs 26:4 and 26:5 side by side and concludes that the Bible contradicts itself.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| Verse 4 says don't answer a fool and verse 5 says answer him, so one of these must be wrong, and Proverbs can't be trusted. | These are proverbs, not laws. Placed side by side, they teach that sometimes silence is wise and sometimes a reply is needed, and wisdom lies in discerning which situation I am in. |

**Why:** Proverbs are short sayings that capture general truths for particular situations; reading them as rigid rules or guarantees misses how the genre works.

Study questions:
- Read Exodus 14:21-31 and then Exodus 15:1-10. What does the song add to the narrative account of the same event?
- Psalm 98:8 calls on rivers to clap their hands. What is the psalmist inviting us to see and do?
- How does Luke describe his purpose in Luke 1:1-4, and what does that tell you about the kind of book he is writing?
- Revelation 1:1-4 calls the book a revelation, a prophecy and (by its greeting) a letter. How might each of these shape the way you read it?
- Which part of the Bible do you find hardest to read, and could knowing more about its kind of writing help?

### [Reading in Context](../articles/interpretation/reading-in-context.md) — Method

Read: Jeremiah 29:1-14; Philippians 4:10-14; Revelation 3:14-22; Nehemiah 8:8

1. Read the whole paragraph, and then the whole chapter, around the verse before deciding what it means.
2. Ask who is speaking, to whom, and in what situation; note what the book itself tells you about its author and first readers.
3. Trace the flow of thought by asking what comes just before, what comes just after, and how the verse fits the argument or story.
4. Identify the kind of writing (letter, poem, prophecy, narrative) and read it the way that kind of writing is meant to be read.
5. Use a study Bible or Bible dictionary to fill in historical and cultural background, without letting the background drown out the text.
6. Compare the passage with other Scripture on the same subject, especially clearer passages, and see how it fits the whole story that centres on Christ.
7. Only then ask what the passage means for you or your church, applying it in a way its first readers would recognise as faithful to it.

*A friend facing a job interview posts Jeremiah 29:11 as a guarantee that everything will go well.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| God has promised me success and no setbacks, so this interview is bound to work out. | God told exiles in Babylon to settle in and seek the city's peace, promising a future after seventy years; I can trust his good purposes and be faithful where I am, even if this interview fails. |

**Why:** Reading the whole letter (Jeremiah 29:1-14) shows a promise of long-range hope to a community in exile, which still comforts believers today but not as a pledge of quick personal success.

Study questions:
- According to Jeremiah 29:1-10, who first received the letter containing verse 11, where were they, and how long did God say they would wait?
- Read Philippians 4:10-14. What "all things" is Paul talking about in verse 13, and how does that change the way the verse is often used?
- Revelation 3:20 is often used in evangelism. To whom is it first addressed (Revelation 3:14-22), and what does that add to its meaning?
- Which verse do you hold dear but have never read in its surrounding chapter, and what do you notice when you read the whole chapter this week?

### [Scripture Interprets Scripture](../articles/interpretation/scripture-interprets-scripture.md) — Method

Read: Matthew 4:1-11; Psalm 91:11-12; Deuteronomy 6:16; Deuteronomy 8:3; Luke 24:27; Luke 24:44-45; Acts 17:11

1. Read the passage in its own setting first (paragraph, chapter, book) so you know what it says before you compare it with anything else.
2. Look up the cross-references in a study Bible, starting with any passage the text quotes or echoes, and read each one in its own context too.
3. When the New Testament quotes the Old, open the Old Testament passage and ask what it meant there and why the New Testament writer reached for it.
4. Gather the passages that treat the same subject directly and at length, and let these clearer, fuller passages shed light on brief or puzzling ones.
5. Check that each link is a real link of meaning, not just a shared English word that may mean different things in different books.
6. When passages seem to pull in different directions, hold both and ask how they fit together before choosing one and setting the other aside.
7. Test your conclusion against the main lines of the whole biblical story and, as your church teaches, against its wider reading of the faith.

*Someone reads Psalm 91:11-12, "they will bear you up in their hands", and concludes that God guarantees protection from the consequences of any deliberate risk they choose to take.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| God promised his angels will catch me, so I can push my luck and he has to rescue me. | Psalm 91 is a song of trust for the one who makes God their refuge; Jesus, offered this very verse as a dare, answered from Deuteronomy 6:16 that we must not put God to the test. |

**Why:** In Matthew 4:5-7 the devil quotes the psalm on its own, and Jesus answers "Again, it is written", letting another Scripture show how the promise is meant to be held.

Study questions:
- In Matthew 4:1-11, where do Jesus's replies in verses 4 and 7 come from, and what situation of Israel's were those words first spoken into?
- What does the devil leave out when he uses Psalm 91 in Matthew 4:6, and what difference does Deuteronomy 6:16 make?
- According to Luke 24:27 and 24:44-45, how did the risen Jesus help his disciples understand the Scriptures as a whole?
- What did the people of Beroea do with Paul's preaching in Acts 17:11, and what would it look like for your study group to do the same?
- Is there a verse you have built a lot on by itself? Which other passages on the same subject could you read alongside it this week?

## key-passages

### [Jeremiah's Temple Sermon](../articles/key-passages/jeremiahs-temple-sermon.md) — Passage

Read: Jeremiah 7:1-15; Jeremiah 7:16; Jeremiah 7:21-23; Jeremiah 26:1-24; Micah 3:12; Psalm 78:60; Isaiah 56:7; Mark 11:15-18

Study questions:
- Where does God tell Jeremiah to stand in Jeremiah 7:2, and who would be passing by as he spoke?
- What were the people repeating in Jeremiah 7:4 and 7:10, and why did those words feel like a guarantee of safety?
- Which wrongs does Jeremiah list in 7:5-9? How do they line up with the Ten Commandments and with care for the foreigner, the fatherless and the widow?
- Why does God send the listeners to look at Shiloh (7:12-14)? What had happened there?
- In Jeremiah 26 the elders remember Micah's words about Zion (26:18-19). How does that memory save Jeremiah's life, and what does it suggest about how God's warnings work?
- Jesus quotes Jeremiah 7:11 in the Temple (Mark 11:17). What does the original sermon add to your understanding of that scene?
- What "safe words" or religious habits might a church or a believer lean on today instead of a changed life, and how could this passage gently correct that?

### [Laying the Foundation of the Second Temple](../articles/key-passages/laying-the-foundation-of-the-second-temple.md) — Passage

Read: Ezra 3:1-13; Ezra 3:1-6; Ezra 3:10-13; Ezra 5:1-2; Ezra 5:16; Haggai 2:3-9; Haggai 2:18; Zechariah 4:9-10; Psalm 136:1

Study questions:
- According to Ezra 3:2-6, what did the returned exiles build first, and what did they begin doing even though the Temple's foundation "was not yet laid"?
- Ezra 3:3 says the people built the altar while "fear was on them". What does it suggest that they worshipped anyway?
- What did the priests and Levites sing when the foundation was laid (Ezra 3:11), and where else in Scripture does that refrain appear?

### [Parable of the Good Samaritan](../articles/key-passages/parable-of-the-good-samaritan.md) — Parable

Read: Luke 10:25-37; Luke 10:38-42; Luke 9:51-56; Leviticus 19:18; Leviticus 19:33-34; Deuteronomy 6:4-5; Leviticus 21:1-3; John 4:9; 2 Chronicles 28:15

Study questions:
- What two commandments does the lawyer quote in Luke 10:27, and where in the law of Moses do they come from (Deuteronomy 6:5; Leviticus 19:18)?
- The lawyer asks "Who is my neighbor?" (Luke 10:29), but Jesus asks which of the three "seemed to be a neighbor" (Luke 10:36). What changes between the two questions?
- List everything the Samaritan does in Luke 10:33-35. What does each action cost him in time, money or risk?
- Read Luke 9:51-56 just before the parable. How might that scene have shaped the way the disciples heard a story with a Samaritan as its hero?
- Why is it a misreading to treat the priest and the Levite as a picture of Judaism as a whole?
- Who is lying "by the side of the road" in my own week, and what keeps me from crossing over to them?

### [Paul at the Areopagus](../articles/key-passages/paul-at-the-areopagus.md) — Passage

Read: Acts 17:16-34; Acts 17:22-31; Acts 17:23; Acts 17:28; Acts 17:30-31; Acts 7:48-50; Romans 1:19-20

Study questions:
- What did Paul see in Athens that "stirred" his spirit (Acts 17:16), and where did he go to talk with people (17:17)?
- Why did some listeners think Paul was proclaiming "strange gods" (17:18)? What two things had he been preaching?
- Paul quotes a Greek poet in Acts 17:28. What point does he draw from the line in the next verse (17:29)?
- The speech begins with creation and ends with repentance, judgment and the resurrection (17:30-31). Why do you think Paul builds toward Jesus in that order for this audience?
- Luke records three responses in 17:32-34. Which response do you see most often when you talk about faith with friends or colleagues, and what might Paul's example teach you about those conversations?

### [The Song of the Sea](../articles/key-passages/song-of-the-sea.md) — Passage

Read: Exodus 15:1-21; Exodus 14:21-31; Psalm 118:14; Isaiah 12:2; Revelation 15:2-3

Study questions:
- Exodus 14:31 says the people "feared Yahweh" and believed. How does the song that follows in Exodus 15:1 turn that fear and faith into worship?
- List the names and pictures the song uses for God in Exodus 15:2-3 and 15:11. Which one surprises you, and why?
- Verses 1-12 look back at the sea; verses 13-17 look ahead. Where is God leading his people, according to verses 13 and 17?
- What does Exodus 15:18 add to the whole song? How does it prepare you to hear Jesus announce the kingdom of God?
- Who is Miriam (Exodus 15:20), and what does it mean that the women answer the song with drums and dancing?
- In Revelation 15:3 the redeemed sing "the song of Moses ... and the song of the Lamb". What rescue in your own life or your church's story would you put into words of praise?

### [The Lord's Prayer](../articles/life-and-teachings-of-jesus/lords-prayer.md) — Passage

Read: Matthew 6:9-13; Matthew 6:5-15; Luke 11:1-13; Ezekiel 36:23; Matthew 26:42; Romans 8:15; James 1:13; Matthew 18:21-35

Study questions:
- Compare Matthew 6:9-13 with Luke 11:2-4. Which petitions do both share, and what setting does each Gospel give the prayer (Matthew 6:5-8; Luke 11:1)?
- The first petitions are about God's name, kingdom and will; the later ones about our bread, our sins and our testing. What does that order teach about how to pray?
- Every pronoun in the prayer is plural ("our", "us"). How does praying it change if I picture my church, or the believers I find hardest to love, praying it with me?
- In Matthew 6:14-15 Jesus adds a comment on just one petition. Which one, and why might he single it out?
- Jesus prays "your desire be done" in Gethsemane (Matthew 26:42). How does that scene deepen what it means to pray "your will be done"?

## manuscripts-translations-and-archaeology

### [Gospel of Luke](../articles/new-testament-books/gospel-of-luke.md) — Book

- Luke 24 ends with the risen Jesus opening the Scriptures to his disciples (24:27, 44-45). How might reading the Old Testament "concerning himself" shape the way you read it this week?

### [Gospel of Matthew](../articles/new-testament-books/gospel-of-matthew.md) — Book

Read: Matthew 1:1; Matthew 1:22-23; Matthew 4:17; Matthew 5:17-20; Matthew 7:28-29; Matthew 9:9; Matthew 16:16-18; Matthew 18:17-20; Matthew 28:18-20

Study questions:

## study-practices

### [Inductive Bible Study](../articles/study-practices/inductive-bible-study.md) — Practice

Read: Acts 17:10-12; Ezra 7:10; Psalm 119:18; James 1:22-25; Mark 4:35-41; Psalm 107:23-30

1. Pray for understanding, then choose a unit to study (a paragraph, a chapter or a whole short book) and read it through several times without notes or commentary.
2. Observe before you explain - write down who is involved, what happens or is argued, where and when, and note repeated words, contrasts, questions, turning points and the kind of writing it is.
3. Turn your observations into questions (what does this mean, why is it here, why is it said this way?) and answer them first from the passage and its surrounding book.
4. Only after your own study, check your conclusions against a good study Bible, Bible dictionary or commentary, and revise where the evidence points elsewhere.
5. Correlate the passage with the rest of Scripture, asking how other texts on the same subject, and the whole story centred on Christ, shed light on it.
6. Ask what the passage meant for its first readers and what it calls you to believe, pray or do now, and write down one concrete response.
7. Share what you found with others, ideally in a small group, so that your reading can be tested and enriched.

*A small group opens Mark 4:35-41, the stilling of the storm, during a hard season for one of its members.*

| Before | After |
|---|---|
| This story promises that Jesus will make the storms in my life go away if I have enough faith. | The disciples end by asking who Jesus is, since even wind and sea obey him; the passage first shows his identity and calls me to trust him in the storm, whether or not it stops. |

**Why:** Observing the closing question (Mark 4:41) and its echo of God calming the sea in Psalm 107:29 moves the group from a quick application to the passage's own focus on who Jesus is.

Study questions:
- According to Acts 17:10-12, what did the Jews of Beroea do with Paul's message, and how often?
- Ezra 7:10 describes Ezra's commitment to God's law in three verbs. What are they, and why might the order matter?
- James 1:22-25 compares a hearer who does not act to someone looking in a mirror. What point is James making about study and application?
- Read Mark 4:35-41 slowly and list everything you observe before deciding what it means. What did you notice that you had missed before?
- Which of the phases (observation, interpretation, correlation, application) do you tend to skip, and how could your group help you slow down?
